Things no longer feel quite as static as they did a year ago.

That’s because it’s now a war against the rear — a war of rear-ends, Z’s and Laura’s, as it were.

The thought occurred to me this morning looking across the courtyard at a hollowed-out building advertising a film called Rave On, starring Volodymyr Z and Laura Loomer, hanging from a crane hook above a doorway whose elevator hasn’t worked in years — the kind of image that makes you suspect the city itself is doing the editorializing.

Thanks to Laura, the war has also moved into MAGA world. The woman who not long ago told her audience that Ukraine was run by Nazis, then by jihadi apologists, and called its president more dictator than elected leader took the thirty-hour train from Poland — there is no other way in now — and came back with a cruddy film.

They deserve each other, frankly — two people who have never once let the facts on the ground interrupt a good performance.

She toured the ruins near the Lukyanivka McDonald’s, watched children walk dogs past a crater, and decided this wasn’t quite what she’d been told. Whether that’s conversion or good television remains disputed. One camp calls it edgelord diplomacy, a bid to outflank a rival influencer rather than a genuine change of heart. Caolan other shrugs and says a keyboard warrior humbled by an air raid beats one who never left the patio. Both are probably true at once, which is usually how these things go.

Ukraine’s real service to Washington was never sentimental. It was structural: a wall built and staffed at no cost to the country it was protecting, standing in the way of a specific ambition to reconstitute something like the old empire. That transaction, stated plainly rather than left to subtext, is the whole logic behind a rehabilitated MAGA influencer now doing more diplomatic work than that jackass Elbridge Colby.

Some of that work looked like this:

> **Loomer:** Would you be willing to sign a peace agreement with Russia in the Oval Office?
>
> **Z:** Yes, I’m ready. I’ve always told President Trump I’m ready to come to the United States, to the Oval Office, or to Mar-a-Lago. It depends on what you prefer.
>
> **Loomer:** That’s very beautiful. Have you ever been to Palm Beach?

A wartime president offers to fly anywhere to end a four-year war. The follow-up question is about the club.

Laura, for her part, was elsewhere entirely this morning — woken by a siren, heart racing, genuinely rattled, and moved to [post about it](https://x.com/LauraLoomer/status/2080787840376401963), eight hours before I took the photo: this can’t be healthy for anyone’s nervous system, every night, for years. Fair point, sincerely made, not a wrong one. It also happened several kilometers from a rooftop full of people demanding Z get his shit together. No sign she’d noticed that either.

By hour three she’d moved on to better news: the President had reposted her interview, and she was [thanking him for it](https://x.com/LauraLoomer/status/2080866141367419387), tucking in that the White House meeting was now set for July 28 — delivered with the brisk satisfaction of someone who’s just watched her own segment get picked up by the biggest possible viewer. Between the siren and the retweet, she does not appear to have slept, and does not appear to have needed to. The news cycle she’s starring in runs on a different clock from the one on the rooftop, and it was never going to pause for a placard.

At 10:02 a.m., Kyiv time — presumably we’re both in the same city — the picture underneath all this: the front itself is essentially frozen. Four years in, drones have made large infantry advances so costly that even a successful push barely registers. But the war didn’t stop. It moved — into a rear with no real edges: refineries, fuel depots, drone expo centers, shipping routes, the warehouses of the adversary’s biggest online retailer. A country can tell itself it isn’t really at war so long as the packages keep arriving; hitting the warehouse is also a way of making that point stick.

The other thread worth keeping is about advantages, and how thoroughly they’ve flipped. The larger side spent years perfecting exactly the tools that turned out not to matter here: mass, armor, manpower, the reassuring math of nine times the budget. By the time that machine reached full strength, the war had already relocated somewhere it couldn’t follow. What matters now is control of the layer that lets a drone be steered precisely — and there, the larger power spent two years simply borrowing its opponent’s satellite service, only building one of its own once that access was cut off.

The bigger side never had to solve its technological gap. It just kept paying to cover for it, the way a Soviet factory got refunded for output nobody bought. Ukraine never had that luxury — the absence of a safety net is what forced the drone-and-network war into existence in the first place.

The blitz failed. The slower grind it fell back on is failing too, since costs are climbing faster than the front is moving, with no sign of the other side hitting its limit first.

A settlement has even been sketched out loud, mostly as a thought experiment: Crimea formally ceded, everything else vacated, special status for the two contested provinces, some reparations from oil revenue, sanctions eased — generous enough that both sides could plausibly call it something other than defeat. But the one man who’d need to sign it is the least likely to. It would mean admitting, out loud, that the whole thing was a mistake he cannot afford to have made.

And underneath it all, the part that lingers: a Ukrainian president who can’t show strength at home travels abroad — to Washington, DC — to look for somewhere else to show it. While he was packing, several hundred people were gathering on the rooftop across from his own office, having given him until the end of the day to reverse a defense-ministry firing that struck most of them as inexplicable on any grounds except the usual ones. The dismissed minister had become inconveniently good at making procurement transparent. Whoever inherits the job will spend months just learning where the light switches are. The front does not have months.

Defense is not a normal cabinet post here. It controls a trillion-hryvnia budget, the entire pipeline of allied money and weapons, and the only formal mechanism by which a civilian can ask the military why it needs what it’s asking for. Replace the person holding that particular door and the question stops being asked for months — exactly the gap the war doesn’t currently have room for.

No tents were pitched. It’s a rolling ultimatum rather than an encampment. The going theory is that someone’s circle finds a functioning, well-connected minister harder to work around than a decorative one — and that partners abroad, the ones quietly slowing disbursements on contested loans, the ones who say only that they “don’t understand Z’s decision,” which in that dialect means they warned him beforehand and were ignored anyway.

The hope, presumably, was that Z’s trip to Washington would let the misunderstanding dissolve in his absence.

Placards reading SHAME don’t dissolve.

They wait.
