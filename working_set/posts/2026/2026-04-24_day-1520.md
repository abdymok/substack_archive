A historian tried to explain conversations that don’t happen. He’d been asked what he’d say to people close to power—people who shape policy, not comment on it. His answer was simple: those conversations don’t happen.

Why?

Because they’re afraid.

Not physically afraid. The more specific fear: exposure. Having to defend a position that only works at a distance. Sitting across from someone who understands the system well enough to ask the one question you can’t evade.

I nodded along, because I’ve seen this dynamic before, just not in politics.

A godsend apartment in Lichterfelde, back when West Berlin still felt provisional: top floor, two rooms, bay window, roof garden. Rent was \$250, half paid in liquor from the PX. One afternoon I came home to find a party blooming across the street on the balcony of a building that had been boarded up the day before—shoulder pads and glassy cocaine smiles, beer mugs raised to what was clearly a very good investment. A restaurant was opening. German classics. Currywurst, Kassler, the works.

That night the soundtrack arrived. Tinny outdoor speakers, one song in heavy rotation. It would stop—someone flipping the tape—and then start again: the same drum fill, the same chorus, over and over. You could leave the room, shut the window, drink yourself into indifference. It didn’t matter. You still had to listen.

That’s the part people underestimate: what you can’t tune out[1](#footnote-1).

------------------------------------------------------------------------

The same historian won’t be having certain conversations about Ukraine. Not with critics. Not with people inside the system who can describe, in unglamorous detail, how it actually works.

The reason is the same. Fear, yes—but also cost.

Because if the conversation happens, something has to be named. And once it’s named, it doesn’t go back in the box.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

------------------------------------------------------------------------

There’s a pattern here, and it isn’t especially complicated.

It begins with the belief that narrative can substitute for system. Early scandals break; the response is cosmetic. Reset, move forward. The assumption holds: control the story, control reality.

Then the problem shifts. From optics to trust. Or rather, the absence of it. Internal fractures widen. Decisions narrow. Competence gives way to proximity.

Finally, the system learns what it’s for. Money moves the way authority has been structured to move—upward, inward. Funds misallocated, contracts feeding the same circle. At that point, calling it corruption misses the point. This is the system functioning as designed.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

------------------------------------------------------------------------

Now imagine the historian sitting across from someone who can lay this out cleanly, without rhetoric. The same framework he uses elsewhere, applied here without modification.

What does he say?

He can’t deny the pattern—he’s spent years describing it.

He can’t claim ignorance—the pieces were public.

He can’t defend the exception without weakening the rule.

So the conversation doesn’t happen.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

He once said, about another political circle, that their refusal to engage was “entirely their fault.” He would have the conversation; they wouldn’t, because they’d have to defend the indefensible in real time.

Fair enough.

But frameworks don’t stay selective. Apply it here and it holds. Refuse to apply it and something else breaks—credibility, or the quieter sense that you’re curating rather than analyzing.

Neither is a stable position.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

This isn’t cowardice, exactly. It’s self-preservation. If your authority rests on seeing patterns clearly, there are some patterns you can’t afford to acknowledge late.

Because lateness implies choice.

And choice implies that the silence wasn’t forced.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

People inside the system don’t need metaphors anymore. They’re looking for recognition—someone to say: yes, this pattern is real, and it has consequences.

Instead, they get omission. Analysis that stops just short of application.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

I learned, in that apartment, that you can tolerate almost any distraction.

But not everything.

If the same song plays long enough, it stops being background. It becomes the structure of your attention.

Some things, once heard, don’t fade.

Some patterns, once seen, don’t resolve into something nicer.

And some conversations, avoided long enough, don’t just remain undone.

They become impossible.

------------------------------------------------------------------------
