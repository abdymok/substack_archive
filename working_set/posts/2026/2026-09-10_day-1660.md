Take what happened today. And yesterday. And the last two weeks, at least.

The bottom rung of the ladder is association: two things happen together, so we notice the connection. The second is intervention: you change one thing and see what happens to the other. The third is the counterfactual: you ask what would have happened if you had changed something that, in reality, you did not change.

It sounds academic. In Kyiv, it is the difference between watching missiles come through your window and doing something about them.

[Judea Pearl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Judea_Pearl) is useful because he gives that entire exercise a particularly unforgiving test. What have we merely observed? What have we actually changed? And what, given what we now know, can we reasonably say *would have happened* if we had changed something else?

Take what happened today. And yesterday. And the last two weeks, at least.

[Image]

On September 6, Russian strikes on Kyiv stopped while Jared Kushner and Steve Witkoff were shuttling between Moscow and Kyiv. Then the strikes resumed after the Americans left.

It is tempting to draw a line between the two events. It is also precisely the kind of mistake Pearl’s first level warns against.

We have an association. We do not yet have causation.

Maybe the presence of the Americans mattered. Maybe it did not. Perhaps orders were given. Perhaps the timing was coincidence. Perhaps something happened somewhere else in the Russian system that had nothing to do with the diplomatic traffic.

The point is not that the coincidence means nothing. The point is that coincidence is not an intervention. Rinse, repeat.

Meanwhile, Z is over 5,000 miles away in Canada, which is at war with America.

[Image]

He is on the front page of [The New York Times](https://www.nytimes.com/2026/09/09/world/europe/ukraine-war-cost.html) for all the wrong reasons, which, by the way, the article only alludes to.

Here’s the short version, for readers who don’t don’t study ultra-competitive Ukraine-style politics: ministers, prosecutors, state companies, people close to the president’s office — one scandal after another. Intelligence agencies have literally shot at each other. Officials get fired, arrested, investigated. Then come the denials, the leaks, the parliamentary shouting, and the calm assurances that everything is under control.

That’s the first thing worth noticing: things keep happening.

But noticing a pattern isn’t the same as explaining it. A runner knows this. Your IT band screams on the downhills, then your knee gives out, and you think: the band caused the knee pain. It didn't. Both trace back to a glute that stopped firing miles ago. Stretch the band all you like — the knee stays angry. Fix the glute, both go quiet.

So let’s ask a better question. What happens when Kyiv actually does something about a scandal?

An official is fired. A minister resigns. An investigation opens. A department gets reshuffled.

And a new scandal appears in the exact same shape as the old one.

That’s not bad luck anymore. That’s a machine doing what it was built to do.

Now the interesting question: what if things were different? What if ministers answered for their ministries? What if state companies weren’t just piggy banks for the well-connected? What if prosecutors didn’t check a man’s usefulness before deciding whether to investigate him?

We can’t run that experiment. But we don’t need to. Seven years of “unlucky” is not luck. It’s a design.

So the real question isn’t who did what this week. It’s what kind of system keeps producing the same result no matter who’s in the chair.

And that’s the uncomfortable part: swapping out the people while leaving the machine running isn’t a fix. It’s just next week’s headline.
