I spent the greater part of Sunday morning in the company of half a million words — my own words, which is perhaps the most uncomfortable company of all.

What I discovered, sifting through this archive, was not what I expected to find. The strongest trait of the work is not that it “called” particular events — as if a writer were some kind of fortuneteller at a provincial fair, reading palms for coins. No. What the archive reveals is something more stubborn and less glamorous: a repeated insistence on three structural realities, each of which had to wait some time before the respectable world consented to notice them.

First: that Russia’s all-out invasion would not be a clean blitzkrieg. History is rarely clean. Nor are the people who make it.

Second: that the war would be politically harder to end than it was militarily to begin. This is the oldest lesson in the world, and the world forgets it with impressive regularity. The parties of 1914 rushed toward the guns with considerably more enthusiasm than they would ever bring to the table.

Third: that information distortion inside institutions would come to matter as much as anything happening on the battlefield itself. A shell destroys what it hits. A lie destroys what it doesn’t. The pattern is recognizable across a century — from the professors of Kiel writing indignant letters to the newspaper desks of London running photographs that required a certain squinting, to the gentlemen of the Department of War who were not so much dishonest as genuinely unable to distinguish between their interests and the truth. The information environment of 1914 and the information environment of 2022 are separated by a century of technology and united by everything that matters.

The archive itself, exported in June of 2026, runs to 1,694 posts — 1,413 of them available only to paid subscribers, and 281 open to anyone who wanders past. Roughly five locked doors for every open one. One post in six freely readable, which is perhaps a reasonable ratio for a world that has never quite decided how much truth it actually wants.

“[Common Sense About the War](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/13635/13635-h/13635-h.htm)” reached millions through a newspaper supplement and was denounced by Christabel Pankhurst, Arnold Bennett, the editors of two continents, and one suspects the ghost of every military attaché who had ever been shown too much at Aldershot. The parallel is imperfect. But there is something recognizable in the experience of writing about a war in real time, for a dispersed audience, knowing that what is disregarded on Tuesday has a way of becoming conventional wisdom by Thursday, attributed to someone else.

A small technical note, offered in the spirit of honesty that the archive attempts to practice: the audience status of each post reflects its setting at the moment of export, not at the moment of original publication. A post that was later opened or later locked is counted as it stands now, not as it once was. Even the archive is subject to revision. The same was true of the White Papers and the Blue Books and all those diplomatic dispatches which, by the time they reached the public, had been selected, arranged, and in certain crucial passages quietly amended. History is always the export. The original files are always somewhere else.
