There are performances in politics that resemble village weddings. Everyone knows why they have gathered, yet for a long time nobody mentions the bride.

Last week Mykyta Poturaiev, chairman of the parliamentary committee responsible for information policy, participated in such a performance on live television[1](#footnote-1). He was asked a simple question: among the nearly two hundred deputies who recently met with President Z, did anyone ask about the Dynasty compound near Kozyn? About the four mansions that appear in the NABU recordings? About the house designated R1, allegedly intended for a certain “Vova”?

A simple question is dangerous because it leaves little room for escape.

Poturaiev’s answer began not with the houses but with the pipes.

The deputies, he explained, were concerned with matters of genuine public importance: air defense, winter, energy infrastructure. Then he offered a memorable image. If frozen excrement begins flowing from Kyiv’s taps, he suggested, people will have little interest in discussing the affairs of Yermak, Mindich, or anyone else.

The image was so vivid that it seemed to consume the question itself.

This, apparently, is the official position regarding the possible fourth mansion: we were too busy worrying about the plumbing.

The difficulty is that the plumbing and the mansion may not be separate subjects. One does not need to be a prosecutor to understand the connection. If public money intended for infrastructure is diverted into private networks, then the frozen apartment and the luxury estate become two ends of the same pipe.

That is why Poturaiev’s answer was so revealing. He did not deny anything. He did not explain anything. He merely moved the conversation from one place to another. The house became the faucet. The accusation became winter. Accountability became a distraction from survival.

[Video: https://youtu.be/mssgNlsle8g]

It is an old trick, though war gives it fresh strength.

A citizen who asks about corruption can be portrayed as a citizen who does not care about missiles. A journalist who asks about a mansion can be portrayed as a journalist who does not care whether people freeze. In this way the question itself becomes suspect.

Yet questions have an unfortunate tendency to survive.

The Dynasty compound existed before the invasion. It existed during the invasion. It continued to grow while ordinary Ukrainians endured blackouts, shortages, and uncertainty. Whatever investigators ultimately conclude, the timeline itself raises questions that cannot be answered by discussing pipes.

Western admirers of Z should pay attention to this distinction.

They were not wrong about everything. His conduct in the first days of the invasion mattered. His refusal to flee mattered. Those were real acts, and history will remember them.

But symbols are simpler than systems.

Many foreign observers saw the symbol and assumed the system behind it must be equally admirable. The Dynasty affair suggests something more familiar: that wartime patriotism and ordinary post-Soviet patronage may have been operating side by side all along.

The investigators are uninterested whether Z himself bears responsibility.

But Poturaiev’s performance told us something regardless. A government confident in its innocence usually welcomes a clear answer. A government that responds to a question about a mansion with a lecture about sewage has made a different calculation.

The calculation is that enough people, for long enough, will accept the substitution.

Perhaps they will.

But winter has a way of clarifying things. So do pipes.

And the more insistently officials speak about the faucet, the more people begin to wonder about the fourth mansion.
