Funeral services for the late [Sen. Lindsey Graham](https://time.com/article/2026/07/12/the-world-pays-tribute-to-sen-lindsey-graham/) will take place on July 28 and 29 in both the nation’s capital and the Republican’s home state of South Carolina. Graham died on July 11 after a trip to Kyiv.

“I’ve never been more optimistic than I am today. We have a magic moment in time here.” - Lindsey Graham, [Kyiv](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=o3n2mWvtOcA), July 10, 2026

Lindsey died coming back from Kyiv. Days later, Laura arrived by train. Ick.

They were the same species of Ukraine lover, just at opposite ends of the food chain — he supplied gravitas, [she pumped out selfies](https://united24media.com/war-in-ukraine/maga-commentator-laura-loomer-visits-kyiv-after-years-of-echoing-kremlin-narratives-what-changed-20990). Yesterday the deputy PM and deputy head of the president’s office [took her for a stroll](https://x.com/BerezhnaT/status/2080020056931442905) around Kyiv-Pechersk Lavra.

Z might crash Lindsey’s funeral later this week at the Washington National Cathedral.

And so the process of figuring out when the National Cathedral would be made available at the exact same time that the global leadership would want to come, and all the other national leaders who will be there, that has made it a little more difficult to get it done this week.” - South Carolina Senator Tim Scott (R), [The Hill](https://thehill.com/homenews/senate/5980028-senator-graham-memorial-services/), July 17, 2026

Maybe Laura will hitch a ride back home with Ukraine’s president.

Lindsey and Laura were not chummy.

Graham called her “toxic” to her face, more or less — told a reporter last September that whatever policy disagreements existed, “the history of this person is just really toxic,” citing the incident where she’d suggested a political rival’s teenage daughter should hang herself. Loomer’s response was not measured. She spent the following days on X informing Graham he was “gay,” repeatedly, festively, as though it were an argument rather than an insult, and reminding her followers that he’d once been booed off a stage at a Trump rally in his own state — proof, in her taxonomy, of disloyalty rather than merely proof that South Carolina crowds get bored.

None of this makes her wrong about Kyiv-Pechersk Lavra being worth a stroll. It only makes it stranger that the deputy PM gave her the tour.

The through-line, for anyone who read the Times [profile](https://www.nytimes.com/2025/07/08/us/politics/laura-loomer-trump.html) from last summer, is that Loomer’s currency was never friendship — it was fear. She doesn’t do warmth. She does “scalps,” her own word for the officials she gets fired by being a heat-seaking missile for Trump’s ass. Graham was one of the very few Republicans willing to say the emperor’s new adviser had no clothes, and he paid for it in gay-panic memes rather than policy rebuttal, which tells you what register the argument was actually being fought in.

So no: not chummy. And a woman who plays, by her own account, for an audience of exactly one, is not going to make an exception for a man’s funeral just because he happened to die well.

On the war jacket, you can’t out-appoint a war this politically strangled.

In feudal Ukraine, when someone gets sacked, people first check whether it was fate or theft — and only much later, if ever, do they bother asking which. Watching [Bessmertny](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=2g4ScSfV5C8&t=4728s) and [Butusov](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=9ho_H8qfkjA) work through the same week of upheaval — Z sacking Syrsky, sidelining Fedorov, installing Drapaty and Khmara — one senses two men circling the same goat, each certain the other has missed the horns.

Bessmertny plays the tragedian. For him this is theater with a hidden author. He walks the audience through Fedorov’s ultimatum — the minister refusing every consolation prize Bankova offered, insisting his goal was never simply to hold a ministerial title for its own sake — and reads it as proof that the real fight isn’t personnel, it’s power itself, with Andriy Yermak’s uninvited shadow stretching across a frontline command post where, by rights, a man under investigation has no business standing. Bessmertny keeps returning to Valeriy Zaluzhny’s [old warning](https://www.telegraph.co.uk/news/2025/11/29/how-to-defeat-putin-and-build-a-better-ukraine/), unfashionable until now: **that no reshuffling of surnames fixes a war being choked by political meddling from Kyiv**.

Butusov is the accountant among the mystics. He wants ledgers, not omens. His sharpest insight is the coldest one: Hungary lifted its veto on the EUR 90 billion defense credit in May, and within weeks — before the money could touch a ministry account — the minister most likely to control its distribution was gone. Not exile as tragedy, in his telling, but exile as bookkeeping. He’s equally unsentimental about the army itself, noting that no after-action review was ever done on Syrsky’s tenure, no diagnosis handed to his successor — just a man swapped for a man, which he calls, not unkindly, a kind of messianism: the peasant faith that changing the icon changes the harvest. And he lands his real blow on procurement, describing tenders written so vaguely — “characteristics,” never brand or maker — that a single firm can absorb an order for 250,000 fiber-optic drone spools no single firm could actually spool, which he treats less as scandal than as the honest signature of the system’s design.

What both men arrive at, from opposite temperaments, is the same joke told two ways: a state that cannot explain its own decisions to itself will always look, from a distance, like either a conspiracy or a shakedown — and it is usually both, taking turns. Bessmertny hears the shadow footsteps of Yermak, Butusov hears the quieter click of a ledger closing early. Neither is wrong. In a house without walls, everyone claims to have heard the thief, because everyone did — they just heard him from different rooms.
