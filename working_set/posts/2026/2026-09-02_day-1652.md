More than 100 days ago, I ended a piece about Alex Karp and Mykhailo Fedorov with a single sentence and no explanation:

*Just ask the techno-messianists in baseball caps.*

I want to finish that thought now, because the clip below, posted yesterday, has made the joke less funny and more literal.

In April, Palantir published twenty-two points summarizing *The Technological Republic*, the book Karp wrote with Nicholas Zamiska. Among them was the observation that some cultures had produced “vital advances” while others remained “dysfunctional and regressive.” Civilizational judgments, delivered from the technological heights of Denver.

Around the same time, *The New York Times* profiled Fedorov as the visionary architect of “the mathematics of war”: autonomous weapons, algorithmic killing, a 35-year-old civilian explaining what modern warfare would require of everyone else.

Fedorov got to test the mathematics directly when Z made him defense minister. He lasted six months before the comedian who was then running the war he had been optimizing kicked him out of the laboratory.

None of this is disqualifying in itself. Plenty of sound military judgment has come from men who never carried a rifle. But there is a particular vanity that belongs to the untested. It surfaces when someone who has never put his own body between an enemy and a city begins explaining, with great fluency, what civilization requires — and which cultures have earned the right to survive.

Not one of these men — Karp, Thiel, Fedorov, Yermak, Z, Trump — has served a day of active duty in any military, in any country.

Karp funds the theory. Thiel funds Karp. Fedorov got to run the experiment for six months before being kicked out of the lab. Yermak managed the theatre until his own name turned up on recordings. Z, as his own biographer notes, never made it to the induction centre at all. Today, he models black miltec pajamas. Trump had bone spurs.

Not one of them has done the arithmetic that only a body under a roof can do.

And yet nearly all of them have, at one point or another, explained what the future owes to people willing to risk everything for it.

That, I think, is what I meant by the techno-messianists in baseball caps.

The phrase sounds like a joke because, at first, it was. But there is an old idea underneath it: that history can be redeemed by something arriving from outside ordinary human limitations.

Once, it was God.

Then it was revolution.

For the techno-messianist, it is the machine.

Technology is not merely a tool but an instrument of redemption: something capable of abolishing suffering, disease, death, ecological catastrophe — and, increasingly, the inconvenient human beings who stand between their theories and their realization.

The vocabulary is secular. The structure is not.

There is the fall: Western decline, civilizational decadence, cultural regression.

There is the chosen remnant: the engineers, entrepreneurs and statesmen who still possess the will to act.

There is the promised transformation: artificial intelligence, autonomous weapons, rockets, biotechnology, whatever new machinery happens to stand closest to the altar.

And there is the great historical wager — that if the right people build the right machines quickly enough, the old human limitations can finally be escaped.

The machine becomes the miracle.

And once technology has been elevated from instrument to instrument of salvation, ordinary political questions begin to look terribly small.

Who gets to decide?

Who bears the risk?

Who pays?

Who dies?

Who gets to say no?

These are questions for democrats, bureaucrats and other people who still believe human beings are supposed to be in charge of their own inventions.

The techno-messianist has a different faith.

He believes the future will vindicate him.

And then, yesterday, there they were.

Karp and Fedorov, standing together in Washington at Palantir, announcing that Karp would become the first major investor in Fedorov’s new defence-technology company.

Karp looked into the camera and told him that he and his colleagues were launching a company that would be “defining for the world,” bringing their “knowledge and acumen about the art of war” to allied countries.

It is a remarkable little film.

Not because either man says anything particularly surprising. Quite the opposite. The language is almost perfectly calibrated to the faith: experience becomes knowledge; knowledge becomes technology; technology becomes a product; the product becomes a contribution to the future of civilization.

The battlefield has become a laboratory.

The laboratory has become a company.

And the company has become an answer to history.

That is the part that makes *techno-messianists in baseball caps* less of a joke than I had intended.

The remarkable thing about the techno-messianist is that he rarely volunteers for crucifixion.

He prefers to build the machine.
