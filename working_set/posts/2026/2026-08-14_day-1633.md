Ukraine has a funny way with numbers.

America does too.

The trouble begins when the two countries use the same number to describe entirely different things.

Ukraine, according to Z, has about 1 percent of the ballistic-missile interceptors it needs.

The United States, he says, could give Ukraine 5 percent of its Patriot missiles. Or perhaps 10 percent.

Five percent sounds like a lot more than one percent. It isn’t necessarily.

The great thing about percentages is that they are meaningless without a denominator.

And the denominators in this case are doing almost all the work.

Ukraine’s number is:

`Ukraine’s missiles ÷ Ukraine’s requirement = 1%`

America’s number is:

`U.S. contribution ÷ U.S. stockpile = 5% or 10%`

Those numbers cannot simply be added together, compared side by side, or turned into some reassuring calculation about how much closer Ukraine is to solving its air-defense problem.

They are funny numbers.

Consider the Patriot arithmetic.

A Russian attack involving 20 to 30 ballistic missiles is not an especially large Russian missile attack by the standards of the war. But if the assumption is that two PAC-3 interceptors are required for every ballistic missile, then the arithmetic becomes rather less funny.

Twenty ballistic missiles require 40 interceptors.

Thirty ballistic missiles require 60.

At approximately \$4 million per PAC-3 MSE interceptor, that gives us:

`40 × $4 million = $160 million`

and

`60 × $4 million = $240 million.`

So, under those assumptions, defending against one such attack costs somewhere between \$160 million and \$240 million in interceptors alone.

One night.

One attack.

And that is before we start counting launchers, radar, command-and-control, personnel, maintenance, reloads, logistics and everything else required to make a Patriot battery actually function.

This is the arithmetic that tends to disappear when politicians start talking about percentages.

Because now comes the interesting question.

What exactly does America’s 5 percent mean?

Five percent of what?

Five percent of the missiles the United States currently has available for transfer?

Five percent of the American stockpile?

Five percent of some larger inventory that includes missiles that are already committed, deployed, reserved for other contingencies, awaiting refurbishment or otherwise unavailable?

And what does 10 percent mean?

Ten percent of the same thing?

Or ten percent of something else?

Without the denominator, the number is political decoration.

And there is another number floating around: Ukraine’s 1 percent.

One percent of what?

According to the formulation, it is one percent of what Ukraine says it needs.

That is a completely different proposition.

If Ukraine says it needs 1,000 interceptors and possesses 10, then Ukraine has 1 percent of its requirement.

If America has 1,000 missiles and gives Ukraine 50, America has given Ukraine 5 percent of its stockpile.

Ukraine could therefore go from 1 percent of its requirement to 6 percent of its requirement — if those 50 missiles were actually suitable for the requirement being discussed.

Or it could go to 5 percent.

Or 3 percent.

Or something else.

It depends on the denominator.

This is not a pedantic point. It is the entire point.

Funny numbers are not a bug in Ukrainian politics. They are a feature.

Governments here have long understood that a number sounds like a fact the moment someone reads it aloud with enough confidence. A minister stands up, recites a column of figures, and the figures acquire the appearance of truth. But numbers aren’t facts merely because they are numbers.

They are arguments waiting for a denominator.[1](#footnote-1)

We have arrived at a similar problem now, except that the numbers are attached to Patriot interceptors and Russian ballistic missiles instead of inflation and GDP.

And the stakes are considerably higher.

There is another number that makes the arithmetic considerably less funny.

Z says that this year Ukraine has two and a half times fewer interceptors than it had in 2025.

In other words, if it had 100 before, it now has 40.

He also says Russia is firing twice as many ballistic missiles per month as it did before.

If both numbers are correct, the problem is not simply that Ukraine has fewer interceptors.

The numerator is shrinking.

The denominator is growing.

Imagine, for the sake of arithmetic, that Ukraine had 100 interceptors available against 100 incoming ballistic missiles.

Now reduce the interceptors by two and a half.

Forty.

Double the ballistic missiles.

Two hundred.

Ukraine has gone from 100 interceptors for 100 missiles to 40 interceptors for 200 missiles.

The ratio has deteriorated from one interceptor per incoming missile to one interceptor for every five.

That is an 80 percent collapse in interceptor coverage.

Or, looked at another way, the ratio has deteriorated fivefold.

This is why the percentages are so slippery.

Ukraine’s 1 percent is a percentage of a requirement.

America’s 5 percent is a percentage of a stockpile.

And the requirement itself is moving.

Russia is firing more ballistic missiles.

Ukraine has fewer interceptors.

So when somebody says that 5 percent of an American stockpile will get Ukraine through the winter, the obvious question is not merely:

Five percent of what?

It is also:

Five percent against what?

Because the target isn’t standing still.

The great thing about numbers is that eventually they have to meet reality.

And reality is very good at arithmetic.
