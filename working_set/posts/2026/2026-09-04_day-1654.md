It’s six o’clock in the morning and I’m still a little groggy. Herself and I take the elevator down. The doors open on the first floor and five амбалы — goons, basically — in blue uniforms are standing there with pistols.

Who are they? What do they want? What are they doing in our building?

More importantly: should I say “Good morning”?

Press the rewind button to Wednesday morning, in another residential neighborhood of Kyiv. Dozens of armed men from two different security services confront one another in the street. Shots are fired. Men are wounded. Z calls it “an absolutely shameful event” and orders an investigation.

Carlotta Gall writes it up for [The New York Times](https://www.nytimes.com/2026/09/03/world/europe/rival-ukrainian-intelligence-units-open-fire-against-each-other-in-kyiv.html) as a turf war — a power struggle between two agencies inside the defense establishment. This is not wrong.

It is also not the story.

The story is that Ukraine’s entire security apparatus has, since 2019, been selecting for personal fealty at every level: president to agency head to department head to whoever is standing outside my elevator at six in the morning. The criterion is not “Are you good at this?” so much as “Whose man are you?”

And once you build a bureaucracy that way, you get departments run by people selected for whom they answer to rather than what they are good at. Two agencies staffed on that principle do not naturally coordinate on a sensitive counterintelligence operation. They glare at one another, withhold information, protect their own people, and eventually shoot one another in the street over custody of a source.

This raises a fairly obvious question:

Why would foreign intelligence trust Ukrainian services that don’t trust each other?

Consider who is actually running the military counterintelligence operations in Kyiv.

Gall treats Wednesday’s shootout as a scandal. But structurally, it was less a scandal than a scheduled maintenance problem. Once appointment criteria become “whose man are you?”, this is where the machine eventually takes you.

A service that promotes for loyalty cannot easily practice the patience actual counterintelligence requires. You do not turn a suspected double agent into a controlled asset if publishing his chat logs on Telegram gets you a better afternoon with the press. You do not patiently cultivate a source when the institutional incentive is to demonstrate that you have caught somebody — preferably somebody belonging to the other team.

And two intelligence services built on exactly the same principle, pointed at each other instead of at Moscow, will eventually do what they did on Wednesday: mistake a rival’s asset for a threat.

It is easier to imagine treachery in the next building than to admit that yours and theirs were constructed by the same method, for the same reason, and were therefore always going to end up here.

We revoked Ukraine’s security clearance back in December 2020, after the SBU’s spectacularly botched attempt to force down a Wagner charter flight out of Minsk — an operation Putin called a joint U.S.-Ukrainian job and the President’s Office denied had ever existed.

Six years later, the agencies still cannot run an OPSEC-sensitive operation without getting caught out.

The only thing that has changed is who is catching them.

This morning, apparently, it was me.

The elevator doors opened.

There they were.

Five men with pistols.

Good morning.
