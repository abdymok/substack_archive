*R2’s mansion c/o National Anti-corruption Bureau of Ukraine (May 11, 2026)*

And here the international press begins to resemble reporters everywhere who spend their careers translating obvious absurdities into respectable prose.

When power behaves irrationally—or obscenely—the institutional instinct is often to impose coherence where none exists.

A leader rambles. Advisers reinterpret. Stenographers insist everything is under control. Reporters quote the stenographers because the stenographers sound sane.

The performance continues because everyone involved benefits from pretending there is still a script.

In Ukraine, the mechanism is quieter but recognizable[1](#footnote-1)[2](#footnote-2)[3](#footnote-3)[4](#footnote-4).

Officials urge patience. Allies murmur about stability. Foreign correspondents retreat into the familiar language of palace intrigue: who is rising, who is falling, who still has access to Z.

[The Financial Times](https://archive.ph/q0c9K#selection-1581.0-2031.240), [Politico](https://www.politico.eu/article/former-zelenskyy-chief-staff-charged-with-corruption-andriy-yermak/), and [the Wall Street Journal](https://www.wsj.com/world/ukraine-corruption-investigation-ensnares-zelenskys-ex-top-aide-f6298cec?mod=europe_news_article_pos3) reported yesterday that Andriy Yermak—once Z’s closest adviser—has been served with a notice of suspicion involving roughly \$10 million in allegedly laundered state funds and participation in what investigators describe as a criminal group.

[The New York Times](https://www.nytimes.com/2026/05/11/world/europe/ukraine-corruption-yermak-zelensky.html?searchResultPosition=2) offered the same facts with the same careful distance.

And then reality intrudes in embarrassingly specific ways.

Not anonymous whispers.

Not strategic leaks.

Actual transcripts.

Voice notes.

Construction schedules.

Architectural renderings.

Requests for roof tiles, window deliveries, wardrobe revisions, landscaping plans.

A communal spa complex.

Conversations about accelerating construction timelines while Russian missiles were landing on Ukrainian cities.

The material is almost too revealing because it requires so little interpretation.

While ordinary Ukrainians were buying generators, body armor, and one-way train tickets west, a small circle of powerful people was discussing heated floors, design revisions, and how quickly their private estates could be completed.

And still much of the coverage remains strangely procedural.

The charges.

The legal process.

The political implications.

The careful language of institutions trying not to look directly at what is in front of them.

But sometimes the most accurate form of reporting is simply refusing to sanitize what power openly reveals about itself.

The transcripts did that.

The videos did that.

And what they revealed was not merely corruption.

It was a governing class behaving as though war were an inconvenience happening to other people.
