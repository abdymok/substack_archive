This is someone’s kitchen in Kyiv yesterday morning. An embroidered tablecloth. Tulips — pink and yellow, the determined colors of people who have decided, against considerable evidence, that spring is worth celebrating. Two paskas, freshly baked. A cat asleep in a patch of sunlight, with the complete moral indifference that cats bring to all human occasions, including historic ones. And in the window, hanging from a string of lights, a small wooden tag that says Paris.

Sellars would have loved that detail. [Wilfrid Sellars](https://conservancy.umn.edu/items/494b3d5f-7c7d-4fe9-afc2-6dc25544690f) — not widely read, which is a pity — was a philosopher who said that philosophy’s only real job was to explain how two images of the world hang together: the manifest image, which is the world of ceremony and meaning and embroidered tablecloths, and the scientific image, which is the world as careful inquiry reveals it, indifferent to tulips. Not to abolish one in favor of the other. To hold both, honestly, at once, stereoscopically.

This is harder than it sounds.

Yesterday Z addressed the nation from Saint Sophia Cathedral. He quoted the inscription above the altar — God is in the midst of the city, it shall not be moved — and spoke of Ukraine as one great family, of children’s laughter, of the first peaceful Easter that was surely coming. His voice was steady. The manifest image was, for six minutes, complete and luminous and — I want to be careful here — genuinely moving. The cathedral does stand. The children do laugh. Someone did bake those paskas.

On April 10th, two days before Easter, [Kyrylo Budanov](https://www.ukrinform.ua/rubric-ato/4111141-budanov-bez-ludej-vijni-ne-vigrautsa-droni-ne-zroblat-ce-za-nas.html) — who has headed the President’s Office since January 2nd, a promotion whose backstory [repays attention](https://abdymok.substack.com/p/day-1457?utm_source=publication-search) — gave an interview to Ukrinform and Novyny.LIVE that read like a dispatch from a different country entirely. He called draft evasion a “terrible problem” — страшенна проблема — and named the central contradiction of Ukrainian public life with a bluntness rare in any senior official, let alone the head of the President’s Office: everyone says the war must be fought to victory, and everyone flees mobilization. But his sharpest observation was this: Ukraine has somehow produced two kinds of hero simultaneously. The soldier at the front, bleeding. And the man who films himself telling the recruitment officer where to go, or boasting about how he slipped across the border. Both are heroes at the same time, Budanov said. This is a terrible problem. It shows the absence of unity. He asked the only question that follows logically from that observation: if everyone evades, will Ukraine remain on the political map of the world? His answer was unambiguous. No. It will not.

The scientific image underneath his words is not comfortable. Two million Ukrainians wanted for violating military registration rules. Systemic violations documented during conscription — beatings, arbitrary seizures, men taken from streets. Support for a negotiated end to the war risen from twenty-seven percent in 2023 to sixty-nine percent in 2025. A practice called busification so common it has its own word, which is always the sign that something has become ordinary.

Z almost never discusses any of this publicly.

It would be tempting to read the distance between Z’s cathedral monologue and Budanov’s interview as a contrast between liturgy and honesty. It is not. Budanov is Z’s man — completely, structurally, from the beginning. His first public act after his 2020 appointment as head of military intelligence was to go on camera and deny that Wagnergate was a Ukrainian operation, a claim Bellingcat demolished fifteen months later with several hundred verified documents. He has been operating in the gap between the manifest and scientific image his entire career — not as a corrective to the presidency, but as its instrument. Z does the liturgy. Budanov gets deployed when the scientific image needs to be named without Z having to name it. The two monologues don’t come from opposing camps. They come from the same apparatus, performing different functions. Which makes the crack in the manifest image harder to repair, not easier. The divining rod and the icon are held by the same hand.

Here is what Sellars gives us to understand the distance between these two monologues. The difference is not that one is honest and the other dishonest. It is that one is doing what Sellars called philosophy — navigating between the two images, letting the scientific image correct the manifest one, naming the crack so that it might be repaired. Z is doing something older and in its proper place more dogmatic: liturgy. Liturgy sustains the community in the image it needs to hold. The cathedral, the inscription, the family, the first peaceful Easter — these are not lies. They are the image as icon, and icons have their purpose.

When the manifest image cracks down the middle, when a society makes heroes simultaneously of the man who bleeds at the front and the man who films himself evading the recruiter, liturgy alone cannot hold it. The image needs repair from within. Philosophy — honest, uncomfortable, blunt grammar and unanswerable questions — is what repair looks like.

Wartime leaders have always preferred liturgy to philosophy. Churchill did it. Lincoln did it. This is not a character flaw; it is a structural requirement of the role. But Sellars’ warning was precise: the manifest image becomes dangerous when sealed off from correction entirely. The ceremony must remain in contact with what it is sustaining people through — or it stops sustaining them and starts simply performing itself.

But there is a further complication now. When the scientific image is being managed by the same hand that holds the icon — when the divining rod and the liturgy are instruments of the same apparatus — the Sellarsian repair becomes structurally unavailable. Budanov naming the crack is not philosophy. It is pressure management. The crack is acknowledged so that it need not be addressed. We have written about this arrangement [before](https://abdymok.substack.com/p/day-1457?utm_source=publication-search).

This is not the first time these questions have been worth asking about Z. In the early months of the COVID epidemic, writing about his government’s pandemic response, we invoked Rudolph Carnap — a logical positivist who, as it happens, was one of Sellars’ formative influences — and his observation that the classical theory of probability “puts a premium on ignorance.” The argument then was that Z’s mitigation thinking substituted comfortable assumptions for honest inquiry. The argument now is not so different. The philosopher changes; the premium on ignorance remains. (See: Plague Mitigation, June 2020)

The religious tradition Z invoked this morning knows something about this too. The Easter story does not skip the tomb. Христос воскрес is not a denial of what came before. It is an insistence that what came before was not the final word. The resurrection is a claim made on the far side of catastrophe, not instead of it.

That is the harder Easter — the one that holds both images at once. The embroidered tablecloth and the two million outstanding summonses. The cat asleep in the sun and the men hiding from their summons in apartments not entirely unlike this one, in buildings visible, perhaps, through that very window.

The Paris tag hangs in the light. The tulips are open. The paska is on the table.

Христос воскрес. Воістину воскрес.
