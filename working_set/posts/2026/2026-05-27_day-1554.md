------------------------------------------------------------------------

There is a particular kind of theater that appears when a feudal system briefly loses its chief administrator. Not reform—that would imply the existence of institutions—but reassurance. Everyone must reassure everyone else that the hierarchy still functions, the extraction continues, the servants remain indoors, the street stays quiet. It is exhausting work, especially when nobody truly believes it.

So [on May 25, our president gathered the familiar faces](https://www.ukrayinska-pravda.com/). Officially, the meeting concerned legislation and creditors. In reality, it concerned panic. His former chief of staff had just posted bail, the machinery of control had jammed, and the oligarchs required confirmation that the house still had an owner.

So he performed consultation. Poroshenko. Tymoshenko. Opposition figures arranged like furniture in a provincial wedding hall. He spoke of unity, of the spirit of 2022, of six more months until Russian collapse—the phrases people repeat when the floor beneath them has already begun to shift.

The difficulty is that Andriy Yermak understood something his employer never fully grasped: in a feudal autocracy wearing democratic makeup, the important word is not democratic. It is feudal. Such systems are not reformed. They are managed. Milked carefully. Their subordinates reminded where they belong.

[The recordings made this almost embarrassingly clear.](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=rQrnaTEzUpY&t=804s) Ten thousand dollars for an interview. Entrance fees for state positions. Corruption so open it had ceased even pretending to be embarrassed. But Western institutional fetishists still misunderstand the essential point. The system did not fail because Yermak was corrupt. Corruption is the operating system. The crisis began because Yermak was arrested.

Visibility, not corruption, is what threatens the arrangement.

Once the canary starts singing from inside the cage, the aristocrat suddenly finds himself standing in full light. Z must pretend he has governed all along. Parliament must pretend it is not merely a plumbing system for patronage. The oligarchs must pretend the patron still exists. Everyone performs stability for everyone else, and especially for the street.

Because the street is the only thing in this arrangement that actually matters. Not elections. Not committees. Not constitutional language lovingly translated for Brussels conferences. The street. The possibility that people may eventually stop pretending alongside the regime. That possibility alone imposes the faint outline of accountability.

Now consider what both regimes face in May 2026, which the macro analysts keep missing because they are too busy admiring their own abstractions.

[Russia is exhausted.](https://...) The war that was supposed to stabilize Putin’s system through patriotic intoxication has produced instead fatigue, resentment, and the dull silence of people who no longer believe but continue out of habit. The militarist narcotic has lost potency. Not casualties, not sanctions, but duration itself has broken the spell. The Kremlin threatens Kyiv with obliteration because it no longer knows what other lever still moves the machinery.

Ukraine is exhausted differently. Not with the war, but with the apparatus managing the war. A system capable of receiving American assistance to extract a witness to Russian war crimes, then sabotaging the operation for the sake of domestic prisoner-swap optics. A system where the president resembles an amiable provincial actor while the valet understands the mechanics of extraction.

[Europe, meanwhile, supports Ukraine qua state,](https://www.nytimes.com/2026/05/26/world/middleeast/iran-ukraine-wars-similarities.html) though increasingly not Ukraine qua Z. The distinction matters. Europe will finance the war because the alternative is worse. It will not reform the system. It will preserve a feudal arrangement while quietly hoping somebody else eventually cleans the stable. But preservation is not stabilization. It is merely the slowing of collapse into reorganization.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

Ukraine can be described at many levels simultaneously. There is the level of drone statistics and artillery ratios - Stubb mathematics. The level of parliamentary theater and Telegram leaks. The level of oligarch bargaining, street psychology, European financing, Kremlin exhaustion. Each level contains immense detail. The mistake is believing that more detail necessarily produces more understanding.

It does not.

When we move from microscopic descriptions to macroscopic ones, we inevitably discard information. But the important point is that we do not do this merely because our brains are small or our computers slow. We do it for a principled reason. We coarse-grain reality in order to separate signal from noise.

A chemist does not track every individual molecule in a kettle in order to understand boiling water. A biologist does not describe a wolf pack through the quantum states of carbon atoms. The lower-level details are real, but many become irrelevant at higher scales of description. One extracts the pattern by discarding the chatter.

Politics works the same way.

At the microscopic level, the Ukrainian system contains endless noise: leaked recordings, procedural scandals, parliamentary quarrels, corrupt intermediaries collecting envelopes in dim restaurants while pretending to discuss patriotism. One can spend years cataloguing these details like an elderly aunt inventorying spoons after a funeral.

But coarse-graining reveals the actual structure beneath the commotion.

The pattern is feudal. Patronage flows downward, extraction flows upward, institutions function theatrically rather than substantively, and crises are managed through performance rather than reform. Chervinsky’s interview matters not because it revealed isolated corruption—everyone already understood corruption existed—but because it clarified the macro-pattern hiding underneath thousands of disconnected anecdotes.

More importantly, coarse-graining does not merely remove information. It changes the categories through which we understand reality.

At one descriptive level, Yermak appears as a uniquely corrupt official. At another, he becomes something more illuminating: a perfectly normal feudal administrator operating exactly as the system requires. At one level, Parliament appears dysfunctional. At another, it reveals itself as a mechanism for distributing patronage. At one level, Z appears weak. At another, he appears almost incidental—a symbolic figure floating atop deeper structural currents he neither controls nor fully comprehends.

And this is why the Chervinsky interview matters.

Not because of any single revelation. Not because one more scandal surfaced in a country already buried beneath scandals. But because, for ninety minutes, the system accidentally described itself at the correct level of abstraction.

The noise fell away. The pattern emerged.

Shared knowledge is what everybody inside the system already knows but politely leaves unspoken, like a family avoiding mention of the uncle who steals watches. Common knowledge is when everyone knows that everyone else knows. Once Chervinsky spoke publicly, the mechanism ceased to be deniable. The system can no longer convincingly pretend to be something other than a patronage structure managing a war.

And that is the real signal.

The system will not reform. It will reorganize. Yermak may disappear, but the logic he embodied—extraction, patronage, sabotage of inconvenient operations—remains intact. The canary sits in the cage, singing loudly, while the structure around him continues creaking forward.

The drones still fly. Militarily, Ukraine retains genuine advantages. But the apparatus managing those advantages is now visibly feudal in a way that can no longer be explained away with wartime sentimentality.

And feudal systems under sufficient stress do not evolve. They fracture.

So we arrive at the autumn window Z promised: six months until something breaks. Perhaps he is right, though not necessarily in the way he intended. The eurocrats depart for holiday. The Kremlin promises apocalypse with the weary monotony of a drunk uncle repeating himself at dinner. The patronage networks quietly reorganize behind closed doors while everyone publicly performs stability.

Only the street remains unpredictable.

Until it rises—or until it decides not to—we are left watching the performance continue: the canary in its cage, the showman pretending to govern, the entire feudal machinery grinding forward while everyone involved knows exactly what it is.

But once the canary has sung, the theater of stability can never quite recover its former credibility. The signal is now unmistakable to anyone willing to listen.
