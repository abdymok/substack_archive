*A digression on maps, boys, and the distance between Chernihiv and the Maidan*

———

On Monday morning, the air raid sirens over Kyiv did what they always do — they arrived before the understanding did. I was a couple blocks away when the debris began to fall on the Maidan, wreckage from drones that had traveled, in some cases, from as far as Chernihiv, strung out across the sky at 1,500 meters like a slow, determined procession. The Ukrainian air defense, which has grown very good at this, knocked most of them down. Many shots were fired. A few pieces made it through. No one was killed.

[Image]

*Zala Lancet drone (Photo c/o Wikipedia )*

What made this attack weird — and strangely instructive — was what the drones were not. At least one was a Lancet, a weapon designed for close frontline work, with a combat range of 30 to 80 kilometers. EW expert Serhiy “Flash” Beskrestnov—appointed advisor to Ukraine’s new Defense Minister Mykhailo Fedorov in January—[posited](https://t.me/serhii_flash/7118) that to reach Kyiv, the Russians had stripped out the warheads and replaced them with extra batteries, launching from the nearest possible point, waiting for a favorable wind. They were not, in any meaningful sense, trying to destroy anything. They were trying to produce a photograph, he said. A fragment on the Maidan. Something to show Putin. Something to show the Russian people. The whole elaborate operation — forty Lancets, a swarm of accompanying Shaheds carrying planted Lancet debris — was, at its core, a content strategy.

[Image]

*Fedorov and Flash (Photo c/o Defense Ministry of Ukraine )*

Wut? I thought about Serhiy’s dubious unsubstantiated wacko explanation for a long time afterward. About the distance between the weapon and its purpose. About a military that will spend enormously, and sacrifice real capability, for the appearance of a result.

And then I read about an American general, Antonio Aguto, who traveled here with a tube of maps, concluding that the explanation looks less like a reconstruction of events than an attempt to impose narrative order on uncertainty.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

In a certain ministry, long before the war it would later explain, there was a briefing about small, cheap drones that did not return. They flew one way, like determined swallows, and ended their journey by exploding. The Ukrainians, who had already been living with such birds for years, spoke at length about them—about their cost, their numbers, their habit of arriving in flocks, and the curious arithmetic by which a machine worth \$30,000 could compel the launch of a missile worth a hundred times more. The ministry listened carefully, thanked the Ukrainians, filed the presentation, and then prepared for a different war. When, some years later, those same one-way drones began to [visit American bases in the desert,](https://edition.cnn.com/2026/03/17/politics/drones-us-iran-war) the ministry was surprised—not by the drones themselves, which it had seen, but by their persistence, which it had not believed.

It is a particular habit of institutions to learn in detail and forget in practice, to study the future as if it were a foreign country whose customs need not be adopted at home.

General Aguto traveled to Kyiv [with a tube of maps so large they could not be folded](https://www.militarytimes.com/news/your-military/2026/03/16/army-general-left-classified-maps-on-a-train-in-poland-watchdog-finds/), only carried like a minor piece of artillery. Somewhere between [Kyiv and Wiesbaden](https://news.liga.net/en/society/news/pentagon-us-army-general-brought-classified-maps-to-ukraine-then-forgot-them-on-train), the tube remained behind on a train, as if it had decided, quite sensibly, to stay closer to the war than its owner. For twenty-four hours the maps belonged to no one, which is to say they belonged to everyone.

They were later returned—untouched, we are told. This word, *untouched*, is a comforting one. It suggests a kind of moral hygiene, as if secrets, like porcelain, can be inspected for fingerprints and declared intact. But a secret is not a teacup. It is more like a rumor: once it has had the opportunity to travel, it is already changed, even if it returns to you looking exactly as before.

The Americans, being practical people, opened an investigation. They discovered that no courier had been assigned, that responsibility had been diffused in the democratic manner of all modern bureaucracies, and that the general, being the senior man, accepted responsibility in the ceremonial sense—like a toast, or a wreath. The investigation also noted the general’s fatigue, including a night of overindulgence at [Tootsie Club](https://uk.tootsie.kiev.ua/) in Kyiv that left him disoriented for a meeting with the Secretary of State the following morning.

This might have been the whole story, a small parable about a tube and a train, if not for another story, earlier, and in its way more instructive.

In 2023, a young man named [Jack Teixeira, who served in the Massachusetts Air National Guard](https://www.justice.gov/archives/opa/pr/former-air-national-guardsman-sentenced-15-years-prison-unlawfully-disclosing-classified), discovered that secrets, when photographed carefully and shared among friends, acquire a new and lively existence. The documents he released—briefings on the war in Ukraine, assessments, fragments of the great machine of knowing—traveled much farther than any tube on a train. They crossed continents in seconds, were examined in Discord chats, argued over, denied, confirmed, and finally absorbed into that vast modern archive where everything is both known and disbelieved.

The boy did not leave the documents behind. He carried them with him, in his pockets, in his phone, in that peculiar space where loneliness and performance meet. If the general’s error was one of fatigue and procedure, the boy’s was one of appetite—the desire to be, for a moment, at the center of a story larger than himself.

Between the general and the boy lies the entire structure of modern secrecy.

On one end, a tube: physical, heavy, governed by rules written for a world in which distance was an ally. On the other, a chatroom: weightless, immediate, governed by the much older rules of vanity and belonging.

The institution, confronted with these two failures, asks the same question twice: What lessons were learned?

It is a serious question, and like all serious questions, it invites unserious answers.

The first lesson, we are told, is procedural. Assign the courier. Track the object. Log the movement. Ensure that the tube is never without a guardian, like a minor prince who cannot be trusted to be left alone.

The second lesson is technical. Restrict access. Monitor downloads. Watch the watchers. Build systems in which a 21-year-old cannot so easily carry the state home in his pocket.

These are sensible lessons. They are also incomplete.

Because both stories share a quieter assumption: that secrets fail at the edges—through negligence, through youth, through the occasional lapse in discipline. But the truth is that secrets fail at the center, where human beings remain stubbornly human.

The general drinks too much in a city at war, because the war is long and the evenings are longer. The boy shares documents with his friends, because to possess a secret and not share it is, at twenty-one, an almost unnatural act.

And now, a third figure enters the story, attempting to bridge the gap between the heavy tube and the weightless chatroom. [In an interview with the New York Post](https://nypost.com/2026/03/16/world-news/ukraine-ready-to-jump-in-to-help-us-as-other-american-allies-drag-feet-zelensky-tells-post/), Z proposes a “drone deal” to Donald Trump, offering to turn Ukraine into the world’s laboratory—a factory where American finance meets Ukrainian scar tissue. He speaks of a world where missiles are ending and thousands of drones are beginning, where the distance of 1,000 kilometers is crossed tomorrow by a machine made for the price of a used car. Yesterday he [presented King Charles an Ipad](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Go_iRIs-7qc) to keep track of combined air attacks on Ukraine. “Flash” [posted](https://t.me/serhii_flash/7120) a picture of it on his Telegram page.

Z offers to train American troops in the art of this new, cheap war, noting with a certain grim politeness that despite their previous wars, they have not experienced this one. What he may not have fully accounted for is that [the drone market he is pitching into is already being carved up](https://finance.yahoo.com/news/military-drone-company-tied-trump-195629023.html). Donald Trump Jr. sits on the board of drone maker Unusual Machines, holds a major stake in Anduril through his investment firm 1789 Capital, and has received Army orders connected to his position—orders whose price, charged to U.S. taxpayers, was not disclosed. Eric Trump, meanwhile, promotes Israeli drone maker Xtend to his followers on X. A drone company counting both brothers as “notable investors” has just announced a merger with a Florida golf course operator, the combined entity promising to support “American drone industry dominance” through “strategic defense partnerships.” The golf courses, the press release notes, may serve as proving grounds. Elon Musk [announced](https://finance.yahoo.com/news/tesla-could-become-major-player-105417517.html) drone manufacturing plans last summer..

Z is not merely asking America to learn from Ukraine. He is, perhaps without quite realizing it, competing with the president’s sons and the richest man in the world, who also happens to be Trump’s biggest campaign donor. I’ll leave out the bits about Palantir, whose reps are also regulars at Tootsie Club.

The response from the center, however, remains consistent. Trump, speaking to Fox News, demurs. *We don’t need help*, he says. *We have the best drones in the world.*

It is the peculiar confidence of those entrusted with secrets, and those who possess the most expensive systems, to believe they cannot be tutored by the cheap, the small, or the young. Yet, as [David Petraeus observes](https://www.foreignaffairs.com/middle-east/autonomous-battlefield), the era of autonomous warfare is already emerging, quietly and inexorably, in the skies of eastern Ukraine. The advantage will not go to the side with the largest fleet, but to the side that first develops the operational concepts to employ them.

The US military, Petraeus warns, risks fielding “autonomous trinkets” instead of mastering autonomous warfare if it fails to grasp the stakes. While Ukraine updates its software every two weeks, the NATO doctrine revision cycle takes 15 to 20 months.

In this gap between the speed of the front and the weight of the ministry, a new and stranger kind of ownership has emerged: the [Danish Model](https://en.interfax.com.ua/news/general/1110185.html). It is a system of direct financing where foreign allies bypass the traditional, slow-moving donation of old stockpiles and instead send cash directly to Ukrainian manufacturers. It is a quiet revolution in how a state survives when its own pockets are empty but its factories are humming. While the physical drones are delivered to the Armed Forces of Ukraine, the intellectual property is increasingly being anchored in European hubs to give Western investors the legal comfort they require while the actual combat-testing remains in the hands of the engineers in Kyiv. It is ownership by osmosis: the knowledge flows west to be protected, while the hardware stays in the east to be used.

But where the Danish model is polished, [the business model of the Trump family](https://english.elpais.com/international/2026-03-16/the-persian-gulf-a-booming-business-for-the-trump-family.html) is more visceral. As the President urges allies to help unblock the Strait of Hormuz, his sons, Donald Jr. and Eric, have assumed control of an empire that has extended its reach to Dubai, Riyadh, and Jeddah. Here, ownership is a franchise—a name lent to luxury resorts and digital assets for hefty fees. The family has found generous allies in Gulf sheikhs, investing in bitcoin mining and AI data centers like the \$20 billion project announced with Hussain Sajwani. Even Jared Kushner’s Affinity Partners has raised billions, primarily from the Saudi public investment fund.

In this landscape, the lines between statecraft and private interest become as thin as a drone’s wing. Steve Witkoff, a business partner and Special Envoy to the Middle East, leads negotiations in Gaza while his sons partner with the Trumps in cryptocurrency ventures. The “Art of the Deal” has moved from Manhattan skyscrapers to the geopolitics of the Gulf, where secrets and investments are traded in the same breath.

Against this gold-plated backdrop, [the story of FirePoint](https://babel.ua/en/news/120997-kyiv-independent-nabu-investigates-the-activities-of-the-manufacturer-of-the-flamingo-missile-they-suspect-price-gouging) is the dark reflection of what happens when “flat organizational culture” meets the old habits of patronage. FirePoint, once a casting agency, now claims to build long-range drones from YouTube tutorials. This “Cinderella story” has curdled into a disaster of quality and corruption, with investigations by NABU into inflated pricing and quality failures, including a production run of drones that came out pink.

The burning question remains: who, specifically, owns Ukraine’s drone interceptor technology and how is ownership stipulated? It is a legal and bureaucratic gray zone. Ownership is stipulated not by patent law, but by access. In the FirePoint case, technology is a commodity of the connected. It is “owned” by those who can navigate the corridors of power in Kyiv to secure the contracts, even if the machines themselves are duds.

Even the high-gloss promises of Silicon Valley have stumbled when they hit the dirt. Anduril Industries arrived with the confidence of a “disruptive” alternative, yet in the intense electronic fog of Ukraine, the hype met a brutal reality check. State Security Service of Ukraine found that the Altius loitering drones repeatedly failed, leading the Ukrainians to abandon them entirely. Despite these frontline failures, the ministry’s faith remains unshaken; [in mid-March 2026, the U.S. Army awarded Anduril a monumental enterprise contract worth up to \$20 billion](https://www.jpost.com/defense-and-tech/article-889950). While Anduril suggests these failures are merely the “messy” but necessary iterations of their development cycle, the Ukrainian operators see it differently.

The ministry returns to its conclusions. It writes them down carefully. It files them.

Meanwhile, the Dnipro river registers its objections in a more direct form—
a meter-thick granite section of wall, neatly removed by an errant drone.
