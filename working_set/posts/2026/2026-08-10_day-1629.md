We wrote about the consolidation of Ukraine’s ultra-competitive arms-procurement system under the Office of the President — via Rustem Umerov — in the last days of January 2025, Day 1060-something of this newsletter, right around the time Donald installed Pete and announced the dawn of a new Golden Age of America. That was the same year Fire Point, the murky missile-and-drone manufacturer, began pulling in the billions it was given to build Ukraine a domestic ballistic-missile system. Eighteen months on, the Atlantic filed two dispatches on Ukraine within forty-eight hours of each other last week, and between them they managed to describe two different countries, neither of which mentions Umerov, Zhumadilov, or Fire Point by name. Michael Weiss’s was [the “model ally”](https://www.theatlantic.com/international/2026/08/ukraine-ally-trump-patriot-missile/688226/) — ten million drones a year against America’s hundred thousand, oil terminals burning in Siberia, a CIA director charming Trump on the golf course with casualty figures, a Patriot license practically signed. [Missy Ryan and Simon Shuster’s](https://www.theatlantic.com/national-security/2026/08/ukraine-war-zelensky-trump/688203/), filed two days earlier, was the retraction nobody announced as a retraction: the license quietly walked back, the Starlink favor still refused, a defense minister fired to street protests, an ambassador resigned and then charged with enrichment. Both pieces are competently reported. Neither one is wrong, exactly. But read together they describe a correspondent corps still filing on the assumption that the war is a story about what Washington decides to give Kyiv, when the more interesting story — the one we’ve been tracking since those last days of January 2025, and the one that actually explains why ministers get fired and who gets promoted afterward — is happening entirely inside Bankova, in a language the Atlantic doesn’t file dispatches in.

By any Western accounting Umerov should be a liability by now. He left the defense ministry trailing his own procurement scandals on top of Reznikov’s — nine-hryvnia eggs, mysterious NABU wiretaps, a starring role in the Mindich tapes. By the logic of “model ally,” a man like this gets quietly retired. Instead he has been floated for Washington ambassador, installed as secretary of the RNBO — Ukraine’s National Security and Defense Council, the body that coordinates the war effort across the security services — and is now running Ukraine’s Foreign Intelligence Service, having never produced a day of intelligence work in his life. The Atlantic doesn’t ask why. We do, and the dates, once you lay them out, tell you the answer is not really a scandal — it’s a job description.

On January 23, 2025, the supervisory board of the Defense Procurement Agency — the body built after Reznikov specifically to take weapons purchasing out of the minister’s hands — voted unanimously to extend the contract of its director, Maryna Bezrukova, an anti-corruption reformer who’d cut intermediary involvement in arms deals from eighty-two percent to twelve. Umerov overruled the board the next day, January 24, refusing to renew her contract and, in the same order, dismissing the two state representatives who’d voted for her — Taras Chmut of the Come Back Alive foundation and Yuriy Dzhyhyr, a former deputy finance minister — for the crime of ignoring the ministry’s preference. He named Arsen Zhumadilov, then head of the separate non-lethal procurement body, as her replacement. NABU opened a criminal probe into Umerov on January 28 for abuse of office. It went nowhere, as these things do. By March 6 Zhumadilov held the DPA seat formally.

The merger is the part that matters more than the purge. Umerov had been pushing since 2024 to fold the two procurement agencies — lethal and non-lethal, roughly a trillion hryvnia a year between them — into a single body under Zhumadilov, over NATO’s own objections that consolidation should wait until after the war. He didn’t live to finish it in office: it was his successor, Denys Shmyhal, who announced on December 3, 2025 that the merger would take effect January 1, 2026, and it did. But Zhumadilov ran the reorganization commission throughout, under both ministers, and Zhumadilov is Umerov’s man. The upshot is that the entire architecture of Ukrainian arms procurement — everything the state buys, lethal and otherwise — now sits inside one agency, run by one loyalist, installed by the minister who broke the law to install him and kept there by the one who followed. Whoever holds the title of defense minister, or RNBO secretary, or head of foreign intelligence, the purchasing itself answers to the same chain regardless. That’s what the promotions were for.

That is the entire Umerov story, and it is also the entire Ukrainian governance story, the one I’ve been filing from this address since long before the war made it fashionable to notice: institutions here are not weak so much as provisional, tolerated only until someone with the president’s ear decides they’re inconvenient, at which point they’re merged, gutted, or handed to a loyalist. Umerov isn’t surviving corruption allegations. He’s being promoted for having ended an experiment in independent oversight and delivered its remains to Bankova gift-wrapped. Loyalty, not competence, is the currency — a Ukrainian commentator said as much bluntly this week, and he wasn’t being cynical, he was describing the org chart.

The same week’s talk-show circuit filled in the rest of the reshuffle in similar terms. Davyd Arakhamia is accused, on leaked NABU audio, of running financed cells of ten to fifteen loyal MPs inside his own faction. Yermak’s circle — Vereshchuk, Shurma, Smirnov, Tatarov — gets described not as corrupt so much as constitutionally foreign, “little Russians” building, in one host’s phrase, a small Russia inside Ukraine while Putin builds the large one; the charge is credited, with visible relish, to Oleksiy Arestovych, which ought to tell you something about the durability of anyone’s reputation here.

We worked out the mechanics of the reshuffle itself [last week](https://abdymok.substack.com/p/day-1623), and it’s worth restating because nobody else has bothered to trace it. Umerov left Defense, so Denys Shmyhal — the former prime minister, whose own ouster from that post was never explained — was installed in his place. Shmyhal then had to be pulled back out to cover a hole at Energy, so Fedorov, until then the digitalization minister, moved into Defense by default. When Fedorov’s arrangement with the Presidential Office stopped working, Klymenko was floated to replace him at Defense — until the street protests made that toxic, and Klymenko got rerouted instead to the vacant RNBO secretaryship. That left Umerov holding no portfolio at all, which is how a man just removed from one security post arrives running foreign intelligence. Every step was forced by the one before it. Nobody chose Umerov for that chair; elimination did.

There’s a further irony worth pulling forward, courtesy of Denys Shtilerman, founder of Fire Point, the drone-and-missile manufacturer that has absorbed most of the money once destined for a Ukrainian Patriot analog. Shtilerman published [an op-ed in the Washington Post](https://www.washingtonpost.com/opinions/2026/08/03/ukraine-shows-war-how-build-modern-defense-industry/) arguing that modern war punishes single points of failure and rewards redundant, auditable supply chains — a fair argument, undercut by the fact that his own company is the closest thing this war has to the dependency he was warning about, and that Tymur Mindich, the sanctioned businessman at the center of the president’s own corruption case, reportedly spent 2024 and into 2025 trying to buy half of it, with recordings that read, to investigators, less like a rejected suitor than an owner discussing his asset. Umerov surfaces there too, on the other end of those calls, back when he still ran Defense. The reshuffle and the procurement story are not two separate scandals. They’re the same failure mode at two altitudes: a system built, on paper, for redundancy — competing agencies, supervisory boards, alternate ministers waiting in the wings — that keeps collapsing back onto a single point of trust, whether that point is a company or a man.

None of this is verified to Atlantic standards, and some of it — the ethnography of Yermak’s inner circle especially — tells you more about the domestic opposition’s own instincts than about the men it describes. But it explains the mechanism where Weiss and even Ryan and Shuster only describe the weather. “Model ally” is a real category, and Ukraine has earned a version of it on the battlefield, with drones and Delta and the Highway of Death. It is not a category that extends to Bankova’s cadre policy, which still runs on the oldest rule in this country’s politics — the one the Soviets called krysha, the roof: you don’t ask whether the tenant is honest, you ask who’s covering him. Umerov built Bankova a roof over its own procurement. That’s the whole appointment.
