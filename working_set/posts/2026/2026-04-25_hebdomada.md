**Introduction: The Pattern Beneath the Chaos**

On April 18, the relative stillness of central Kyiv was shattered by gunfire. Seven people died while two police officers, faced with the immediate choice to protect or preserve themselves, fled the scene. This tragic failure of duty provides more than just a case for a prosecutor; it offers a window into what the philosopher Daniel Dennett called “economical patterns.”

Dennett proposed a test for reality that has the charm of a joke. A checkerboard can be written down quickly, its logic easily encoded. But a field of flax, seen from above—hundreds of small blue and violet flowers, scattered without order, light catching some and losing others, a dark human shadow cutting through the middle—takes longer to encrypt. There is nothing to say about it except itself. From this, he concluded: a pattern is real if it allows you to say less.

In the wake of the shooting, the voice of President Z fluctuated with a telling frequency. By examining the shift from procedural calm to moral outrage, we can begin to see the underlying arithmetic of modern statesmanship: a system where a leader’s intensity is rarely a measure of conviction, but rather a calculation of political cost.

**Takeaway 1: Conviction as a Calculation**

The “Director of Victories” approach defines a leadership style that prioritizes optics over structural resolution. On April 19, President Z’s initial response to the Kyiv shooting was almost clinical. He spoke of investigations, personnel reviews, and protocols—the “plumbing problems” of governance. However, by April 20, as public fury mounted, his tone underwent a radical transformation. Suddenly, the officers hadn’t just failed; they had “fled,” and the word “accountability” became the centerpiece of his rhetoric.

This shift reveals a leader who seizes moral clarity only when it aligns perfectly with popular sentiment and carries zero political risk. He can be decisive when everyone already agrees, but he remains silent on the harder, more divisive structural questions. He avoids the “Difficult Questions” that follow such a tragedy: Should the police force be mobilized to the front despite domestic security needs? Should civilians be permitted to own handguns for self-defense? How should post-war demobilization work? On these matters, he is silent, because any answer would alienate a key constituency.

“You can tell everything about him by which crises he rushes to meet, and which ones he approaches as if they were invoices requiring a filing stamp.”

President Z calculates the cost of every position. He develops convictions precisely at the point where they become inexpensive. When the bill for a genuine, unpopular decision comes due, he is often found elsewhere, preserving his popularity by avoiding the friction of leadership.

**Takeaway 2: The “Donnyland” Strategy and the Pawned Overcoat of Dignity**

Pragmatism in a time of crisis often requires the sacrifice of pride. Z understands that dignity, like a good overcoat, must sometimes be pawned when the winter is very cold. This was never more evident than in the proposal to rename a fifty-mile strip of depopulated Donbas coal country “Donnyland.”

This was a blatant appeal to Donald Trump’s vanity—an attempt to trade a piece of geography for political momentum. The project carried a poignant level of detail: a fifty-mile-long strip of land populated mainly by soldiers and flower shops, a newly designed green and gold flag, and a national anthem composed by an AI that had read everything but understood nothing. It was a grand gesture across a great distance, made all the more absurd by the fact that the Americans likely never even saw the flag. It was the ultimate expression of a leadership willing to perform for an audience that wasn’t even looking.

“A man who perfects the art of kneeling will eventually forget how to stand.”

**Takeaway 3: The Baltic Misstep and the Weaponization of Fear**

In a recent Sunday interview, President Z attempted to pressure NATO by claiming Russia was preparing an imminent attack on the Baltic states. This was a tactical attempt to use fear as a lever for aid, but it resulted in rare public friction with his closest allies.

Estonian Foreign Minister Margus Tsahkna was quick to push back, noting that Z’s claims did not match intelligence assessments and that Russia remains militarily and economically weak. More pointedly, Marko Mihkelson, chair of the Estonian parliamentary commission, critiqued the habit of “pointing fingers at Europe.” He argued that such public warnings actually strengthen Russia’s psychological warfare by feeding their narrative that the Baltics are inevitable victims. By broadcasting these warnings to the press instead of discussing them quietly between allies, Z risked his credibility and generated irritation rather than the intended surge of support.

**Takeaway 4: When Corruption is the System Functioning as Designed**

To understand how narrative eventually fails, one must understand the concept of “heavy rotation.” I recall a godsend apartment in Lichterfelde, back when West Berlin felt provisional. One afternoon, a party bloomed across the street on a boarded-up balcony—shoulder pads and glassy cocaine smiles, beer mugs raised to a new restaurant investment. That night, the soundtrack arrived. A single song played on tinny speakers in heavy rotation. It didn’t matter if you shut the window or drank yourself into indifference; the song became the structure of your attention.

In modern governance, there is a similar progression. It begins with the belief that narrative can substitute for system. Control the story, control reality. But eventually, the problem shifts from optics to an absence of trust. Internal fractures widen, and competence is replaced by proximity to power. Money begins to move exactly as authority is structured: upward and inward. Funds are misallocated, and contracts are funneled to a tight inner circle.

“At that point, calling it corruption misses the point. This is the system functioning as designed.”

Policy-shapers often avoid honest conversations about these mechanics because they fear exposure—having to defend a position that only works at a distance. When the “heavy rotation” of a narrative plays long enough, it stops being background noise and becomes a reality that can no longer be ignored.

**Conclusion: The Cost of the Impossible Conversation**

The patterns of leadership are real because they allow us to say less. If we apply Daniel Dennett’s test for reality, we see that President Z’s behavior is consistent: he speaks when it is inexpensive and remains silent when speech requires a difficult choice between two consequences. This clarity is not a virtue, but a diagnosis.

The arithmetic of leadership works as long as “advantageous” and “necessary” overlap. But eventually, the numbers stop adding up. When the narrative can no longer hide the absence of trust and when the “Donnylands” of the world go unnoticed, the conversation becomes impossible. It leaves the observer with a persistent, uncomfortable question: Is a leader truly guiding events toward a better future, or has he simply become the most economical expression of the chaos surrounding him?

I didn’t write this week about quietly **rewiring my propulsion system** in pursuit of a sub-six minute mile—a project that turns out to be less about lungs and more about teaching a reluctant posterior chain to behave like one.

This has involved a series of highly specific, faintly punitive interventions: slow-eccentric **Bulgarian split squats** that feel like negotiating a treaty with your hip stabilizers; isometric holds at end-range hip extension where the **gluteus medius muscle** begins to tremble like a small, overworked civil servant; and banded lateral walks designed to wake up fibers that have apparently been on administrative leave since 2019.

There are also **single-leg Romanian deadlifts**, performed slowly enough to expose every lie your balance system tells you, and hip thrusts that convert abstract anatomical diagrams into immediate, local pain. The **gluteus maximus muscle**—long accustomed to outsourcing its responsibilities to quads and lower back—has been recalled to active duty.

The technical goal is straightforward: restore clean hip extension, stabilize the pelvis under load, and eliminate energy leaks that show up, at pace, as inefficiency rather than drama. The lived experience is less elegant. It’s a steady accumulation of micro-failures: shaking holds, asymmetries you can’t unsee, and the distinct sensation that parts of your body are being introduced to each other for the first time.

Progress, if that’s what it is, comes in small, measurable shifts. A stride that feels marginally more elastic. Ground contact a fraction cleaner. The sense—brief, unreliable—that forward motion is being generated from somewhere behind you, rather than assembled awkwardly in front.

It’s technical. It’s tedious. It’s a little painful.
