On the Ukraine jacket, desert arithmetic (with guarantees).

[Image]

*c/o Interfax-Ukraine (March 25, 2026)*

Z says Europe may block him, so he looks to the Gulf. It sounds like initiative. It is, in fact, subtraction.

The February note from [Carnegie Politika](https://carnegieendowment.org/russia-eurasia/politika/2026/02/arab-world-russia-support) reads like a quiet audit: the Arab states have already adjusted—to Moscow. Trade grows. Mediation flatters everyone. No one chooses.

Now the arithmetic sharpens. In his [interview with Reuters](https://www.reuters.com/business/aerospace-defense/us-links-security-guarantees-ukraine-giving-up-donbas-zelenskiy-says-2026-03-25/), Z describes an American offer: guarantees, in exchange for Donbas. Security, priced as territory. He calls it risk. He is right. But he is already negotiating inside the price.

He thanks Donald Trump for Patriots while explaining that Patriots are not enough. Gratitude and insufficiency, in the same breath. This is not strategy. It is exposure.

Z arrives in the Gulf with urgency, offering alignment to partners who prefer options. He speaks in war; they answer in margins. Iran has rearranged the language. Moral clarity now travels at a discount.

So the Middle East becomes a “prospect.” A polite word for a market where your adversary already has better terms.

There is no vacuum there. Only pricing.

And Z, increasingly, is the asset being priced.

Jonathan Chait has written an interesting piece in *[The Atlantic](https://archive.ph/Z3AEm#selection-691.0-1071.431)* about Joe Kent[1](#footnote-1). We wrote about Joe Kent exactly one year ago[2](#footnote-2). We mention this not out of vanity — well, not *entirely* out of vanity — but because the gap between these two dates is itself the story.

On March 27, 2025, in a newsletter with a paid subscriber list considerably smaller than *The Atlantic*‘s readership, we called the Gabbard-Kent-Witkoff arrangement what it was: a jamboree of jackasses sharing time-sensitive US attack plans on a commercial app while regurgitating Kremlin narratives about Russia’s war. We noted that Joe Kent’s posts on Twitter mirrored content from Ukraine Watch, a Telegram channel endorsed by the Russian government. We noted that Kent had described Putin’s territorial demands as “very reasonable.” We noted that his failure to shut down the Houthi Signal group was borderline dereliction of duty. We called his boss’s boss, Steven Witkoff, the reincarnation of unwitting idiot Carter Page. We did all of this, we should add, while Kharkiv was being hit by UAV attacks and the Associated Press was filing credulous dispatches about a ceasefire that did not exist.

Chait, writing a full year later, calls Kent’s opinions “strange.” This is generous. Strange is your uncle at Thanksgiving insisting the moon landing was filmed in New Jersey. Kent was the chief of staff of the Director of National Intelligence, pending confirmation to lead the National Counterterrorism Center, while mirroring Kremlin Telegram channels and blaming the CIA or Ukraine for a Moscow terror attack. The word Chait is looking for is not strange. The word is *installed*.

This is the distinction *The Atlantic* essay, for all its analytical elegance, cannot quite bring itself to make. Chait sees a faction — hawks versus populists, a coalitional struggle playing out on podcasts and op-ed pages, a rhetorical war over who truly speaks for MAGA. What he misses is the personnel operation running underneath it. Kent was not merely voicing heterodox opinions about Ukraine. He was Gabbard’s chief of staff. Gabbard’s colleague Nicholas Kass, whose resume suggests intelligence work as a recreational hobby, was installed atop the National Intelligence Council, which produces the assessments on which policy is built. The faction wasn’t just talking. It was redecorating the building.

Chait’s framing — Republican hawks versus populist anti-interventionists, with Trump as the weathervane — is not wrong. But it treats the problem as essentially ideological, a battle over ideas that will be won or lost depending on whether the Iran war succeeds. Our diagnosis, offered from the cheaper seats a year earlier, was structural: you cannot perform incomplete defenestration and call it a solution. It does not matter whether you throw the body from the 29th floor or the 9th floor. What matters is whether it hits the pavement. Kass, for his part, spent seven months atop the National Intelligence Council — seven months producing or overseeing the assessments that shape how America understands the world — before quietly stepping down in early 2026. No hearing. No reckoning. No *Atlantic* essay. He did not hit the pavement so much as drift gently to street level and walk away. The building remains redecorated.

We do not begrudge Chait his platform or his prose, which is considerably more elegant than ours. We only note that the watchtower of prestige journalism has a peculiar relationship with time — it confirms what the smaller, more exposed outposts reported long ago, and it does so with the calm authority of a man who has just discovered fire and would like to explain combustion to you. By all means, read *The Atlantic*. But consider also that somewhere in the Substack wilderness, amid the paid tiers and the Ukrainian jacket updates and the digressive notes about the Queen of the Netherlands, someone was already telling you this. They were telling you when it still might have mattered.

There is no truce. There is no ceasefire. There is only the photograph of Donald, standing next to Putin in Anchorage, hanging in the White House — and a year’s worth of Sunday magazine prose catching up to what the jackasses were doing all along.
