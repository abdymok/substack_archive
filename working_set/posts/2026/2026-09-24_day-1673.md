I left work early Wednesday evening after the air alert so I could save UAH 30 and get a free metro ride. The time was 20:40.

I had spent the afternoon trying to establish that the Parkovy data center had been hit once.

As I walked to the metro, I heard a swoosh above me that sounded a lot like the jet-propelled drones I’d been watching from my apartment balcony over the weekend. Seconds later: explosion.

By the time I got home, Telegram was telling me the Parkovy data center had been hit again.

I had spent the afternoon trying to establish that it had been hit once. I had just become a witness to it being hit again[1](#footnote-1).

This reminded me of something I wrote in *[Target practice](https://abdymok.substack.com/p/day-1008?utm_source=publication-search)* on Day 1008, back in November 2024:

> *Basically we’re target practice for the maniacs next door until Donald gets his shit together, or doesn’t.*

There’s a trap in numbers.

One is small; a thousand is large, so we imagine the road from one to a thousand as just a longer version of the road from zero to one.

It isn’t.

That’s roughly the point Oleksiy Babenko makes in his [long interview with Bihus.Info](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=6W3R5Q9Hd24) about Ukraine’s struggle to intercept Russia’s new jet-powered Shaheds.

The new drones aren’t quite the same problem as the old ones. They’re faster, which shrinks the window between spotting a target and having something in position to shoot it down. A system built around slower drones discovers that time itself has turned into an enemy.

But Babenko’s sharper point comes after that.

Ukraine had already seen jet-powered drones intercepted — successful tests, even successful combat kills. That produced a comforting conclusion: the problem was being solved.

Except one successful interception isn’t a defense system.

Babenko says manufacturers, his own company included, believed for too long that they’d found the answer. Some of the early successes were flukes — tied to specific conditions, or to flaws in the attacking drone itself.

Somewhere along the way, “we managed to do this once” quietly became “we know how to do this.”

A large transformation disguised as a small sentence.

Then comes the second problem: production.

A prototype needs one clever engineer. A thousand machines need engineers, suppliers, testing, quality control, money, logistics, and people who know how to turn the first working unit into the thousandth — without discovering that some crucial part only exists in batches of fifty.

Babenko keeps returning to this gap: grant money isn’t production money, a successful flight isn’t deployment at scale, and a small team that can invent something isn’t automatically a team that can manufacture it at scale.

He’s blunt that Ukraine’s real shortage isn’t cash but engineers — and beyond that, people who’ve actually made the leap from one unit to thousands before. Because that leap brings its own unfamiliar failures.

Modern war is an exam in arithmetic.

The attacker doesn’t need one brilliant machine. It needs enough machines, made fast enough and often enough, to turn a momentary weakness into a permanent one.

The defender has to answer in the same currency, on the same clock.

So the real distance was never zero to one.

It’s one to a thousand — and then, worse, from today’s thousand to tomorrow’s, while the problem itself keeps changing shape underneath you.

That’s the part no demonstration flight, however successful, can settle.

**Poem of the Day — The Remainder**

There is always something left over.

A sentence.
A photograph.
A person standing at the edge of the frame.
A day after you said the last day was finished.

The other day I put 41 into the middle of the forty.

It didn’t improve the number.
It didn’t complete the archive.

It did something more irritating.

It kept it open.

Perhaps that is what an archive is for.

Not to finish the past.

Just to make sure the remainder has somewhere to go.
