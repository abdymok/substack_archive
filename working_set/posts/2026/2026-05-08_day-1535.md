May 8 is recognized in the United States by proclamation as **Victory Day for World War II**, marking the end of the war in Europe. It is not a federal holiday. Government offices stay open. Banks stay open. Markets open on time.

Meanwhile, May 8 is also National Coconut Cream Pie Day.

There is a kind of honesty in this. Not admirable honesty — but honesty nonetheless.

Victory over fascism merits a proclamation and little else. No pause in ordinary life. No interruption of commerce. The machinery hums along. This is not exactly callousness. It is something more revealing: forgetfulness disguised as normalcy. The luxury of distance.

The war was won long ago, decisively, and at staggering cost — much of it borne elsewhere. Americans can afford to treat the day as historical background noise, something acknowledged if convenient, ignored if not.

Ukraine, where I happen to be, marks May 8 differently.

People lay red poppies at monuments to the dead. Quietly. No military spectacle. No triumphalist rhetoric. Maybe this year will be different. Probably not.

The arithmetic explains the difference. Around eight million Ukrainians died in World War II. Seven million were mobilized. Entire cities vanished. The war consumed not only soldiers, but the architecture of ordinary life.

When loss reaches that scale, celebration begins to feel obscene. You remember instead.

And remembrance here remains deliberately quiet because the dead have already been exploited once before.

That was the Soviet trick: take catastrophe, dress it in uniforms, march it through Red Square, and tell citizens their dead proved the eternal righteousness of the state.

Bertolt Brecht understood this instinctively.

While fleeing Nazi Germany, Brecht clipped wartime photographs from newspapers and assembled them into *[War Primer](https://www.e-skop.com/images/UserFiles/Documents/Editor/bertolt-brecht-war-primer.pdf)* — a brutal little archive of images paired with bitter poems. In one photograph, an American soldier stands over a dying Japanese soldier he has just shot. Brecht writes:

*And with their blood they were to colour red*
*A shore that neither owned.*
*I hear it said*
*That they were forced to kill each other. True.*
*My only question is: who forced them to?*

That question survives every victory parade.

Who forced them to?

Brecht understood that states turn the dead into arguments. They convert suffering into legitimacy. They take the young men who died terrified on beaches and in trenches and convert them into symbols of national virtue.

The Soviets perfected this.

Americans sanitized it.

Ukraine, perhaps because history has offered so little room for illusion, is trying something harder: memory without spectacle.

The flowers are quiet because the dead will not be conscripted again — not into empire, not into propaganda, not into someone else’s mythology of strength.

And this is where the comparison becomes more interesting than a simple contrast between two holidays.

Donald Trump speaks about freedom as something possessed — something won once and defended against outside threats. It is a comforting story: we defeated fascism, secured liberty, and now must protect what is ours.

Ukraine suggests something less comfortable and far more demanding.

Freedom is not something you possess.

It is something you choose.

Repeatedly. Daily. Sometimes at extraordinary cost.

The laying of flowers matters because it rejects triumph as the final meaning of history. It insists memory belongs to the dead, not the state.

Totalitarianism speaks in the language of inevitability. It tells you history has already rendered its verdict, that power has been justified, that obedience is rational.

Freedom sounds different.

It asks something of you every day: remember honestly. Resist mythology. Refuse the lie that history’s worst chapters stay safely in the past.

This is harder than pie.
