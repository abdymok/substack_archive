*Enchanted c/o ai (February 12, 2026)*

In an ideal world, the three journalists who co-authored yesterday’s piece in the *Financial Times* would resign. But we don’t live in an ideal world.

Consider the backdrop to their fantasy: in the early hours of February 12, Russia unleashed one of its largest barrages yet on Ukraine—24 Iskander-M/S-300 ballistic missiles from Bryansk, Voronezh, Rostov, and occupied Crimea; one Kh-59/69 guided missile from Donetsk; and 219 strike drones (around 150 Shaheds) from multiple Russian sites and Crimea, zeroing in on Kyiv, Kharkiv, Dnipro, and Odesa. Ukrainian air defenses downed or jammed 213 targets (15 ballistic missiles, 1 guided missile, 197 drones), but 9 missiles and 19 drones hit 13 locations, with debris at 14 more—amid Abu Dhabi "talks."

The chances that Ukraine will hold presidential elections and a referendum on an as-yet-unagreed peace plan by June 2026 are zero. Not slim. Not vanishingly small. Zero. That anyone anywhere is treating the suggestion with anything other than derisive laughter is a cause for concern. So why file the story[1](#footnote-1)?

Cognitive scientists talk about the “laws of thought” — the principles that govern rational inference. Chief among them: when new evidence arrives, you update your beliefs accordingly. You do not, as George Boole might have put it, simply reproduce the output with the highest prior probability. You do not stenograph what sources feed you and dress it up as analysis. And you are certainly not supposed to let three years in a Moscow bureau calcify your inductive bias to the point where you can no longer tell a peace process from a performance.

Permit us to introduce the FT’s three co-authors, each a distinguished violator of the above.

Henry Foy, the statement sock guy of Moscow correspondence, gained a certain notoriety in July 2019 for a profile of Putin so oleaginous it left grease stains on the reader’s screen. If P is “Putin says he wants a deal” and Q is “Putin wants a deal,” Foy’s career has been a sustained refusal to examine the conditional. Max Seddon is cut from similar cloth — another Moscow alumnus whose priors have, to put it charitably, not been updated since the Kremlin was a more congenial beat. The third, Christopher Miller, is perhaps most remarkable for having been based in Kyiv while for years failing to distinguish a Russian invasion from a separatist uprising аs it happened. Modus ponens, gentlemen: *if* there is a war, *and* you are the war correspondent, *therefore* you report the war. The logic is not difficult.

Together, this trio has produced a piece that injects not even a tiny droplet of political reality into the clear waters of our imagination.

Here is the reality. The Abu Dhabi talks — currently the designated venue for this diplomatic theatre — are not negotiations in any meaningful sense. Russia is there to discuss ending the war on its terms; Ukraine is there to discuss a ceasefire on its terms. The two conversations barely overlap. When negotiators start swapping prisoner lists, it generally means they have run out of anything else to discuss. They have run out of anything else to discuss.

The FT piece presents Z’s election gambit as a serious political pivot — a bold concession to American pressure. It is nothing of the sort. It is an attempt to convert the endgame into a show: proposing a personal meeting with Putin that everyone knows is a non-starter, thereby blocking substantive discussions from happening elsewhere. Whether you read this as clownish or strategically rational depends on your priors. The trouble is, the FT’s priors — shaped by years of Kremlin briefings, Brussels lunch circuits and the peculiar epistemology of the statement sock — are exactly the wrong tool for the job. Like a large language model trained on the wrong corpus, these journalists have become very good at producing fluent, high-probability output that sounds authoritative and explains nothing.

Meanwhile, this weekend Munich plays host to its annual Security Conference — this year more aptly described as the Insecurity Conference, a gathering of the anxious and the bewildered, convening to marvel collectively at the hole where Western strategic coherence used to be. The old order is not coming back, Washington has left its allies very little room for manoeuvre, and every deadline is performative. The dog-and-pony show will feature the usual procession of ministers affirming their commitment to things they are visibly not committed to, flanked by think-tankers who will explain, at considerable length, why everything is very complicated. Somewhere in a side room, someone will brief the FT. If both parties are merely imitating readiness for peace, then a dispatch treating a wartime election timeline as a workable proposition is not journalism. It is Boole without the algebra — the appearance of logical structure with none of the content.

The will to negotiate is the black cat in a dark room — and it isn’t there, on either side. No amount of Munich speechifying or FT sources “familiar with the matter” will conjure it into existence. You cannot reason your way to a conclusion your premises do not support. If P, then Q. But there is no P. Therefore, no Q. Therefore, perhaps, no story.

Perhaps, gentlemen, fewer sources. More thought.
