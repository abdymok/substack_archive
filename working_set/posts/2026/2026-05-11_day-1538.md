In May, the lilacs bloom in Kyiv and Moscow alike. The flower is indifferent to borders, to war, to political circumstance. It blooms because that is what lilacs do in spring—a cycle so regular, so predictable, that watching it feels like observing the only honest thing left in the world.

Donald Trump announced the latest ceasefire during this season: three days of suspended hostilities, prisoner swaps, the beginning of peace—timed to coincide with Russian Victory Day, as if the rhythm of diplomacy could somehow align with nature’s indifference.

One day later, Russians and Ukrainians were accusing one another of violating the ceasefire.

This was not surprising. A ceasefire, like a promise to let the lilacs bloom undisturbed, often contains within itself the seeds of its own failure.

In Moscow, Victory Day produced an unintentionally honest image. The parade meant to display imperial confidence looked nervous. Fewer tanks. Mobile internet shut down. Air defenses repositioned to protect a ritual of strength from very modern anxieties. Even nostalgia now requires drone protection.

At the front, meanwhile, mathematics remains cruel. Russia spent winter grinding forward at enormous human cost and appears to have exhausted the gains. Ukrainian drones now stalk supply routes, strike oil facilities, and make distant rear areas feel less distant. Russia continues feeding men into a machine that consumes them faster than it replaces them. Ukraine continues finding cheaper ways to destroy more expensive things.

The drones will return after every ceremonial pause because drones do not respect political theater.

But war is not the only repetitive system.

The leaked recordings now circulating in Kyiv—what local media calls the Mindich tapes—have given Ukrainians the unpleasant confirmation that powerful men often sound exactly as one imagined in private: discussing placements, inconvenient associates, investigations, and personal exposure with the calm of people reorganizing seating charts at a wedding.

Move this person here. Hide that problem there. Wait for public attention to wander elsewhere.

The conversations are striking not because they reveal criminal genius, but because they reveal banality. The old post-Soviet habit survives: rearrangement instead of reform.

And Ukrainians appear increasingly tired of it.

Military leaders remain widely trusted. Political leadership increasingly does not. More striking still: many Ukrainians now say corruption poses a greater threat than Russia itself.

Z’s response has been familiar—small cash payments, subsidies, temporary relief. Enough to dull anger, not restore trust.

And so the loops continue.

Russia repeats military failure.
Ukraine’s elite repeats institutional habits.
The public repeats its disappointment.
Foreign observers repeat selective blindness.

Then someone announces a ceasefire, which is often what people do when they have run out of solutions but still possess microphones.

The war resumes. The corruption resumes. The explanations resume.

Only public trust, once exhausted, has a habit of not returning.
