From the eighth floor, the sunrise behaves itself.
It arrives modestly, like a guest who understands the limits of hospitality. The pink clouds gather at a polite angle; the light distributes itself with a certain civic fairness: a portion to the apartment blocks on the left, a portion to the villas below, and a diluted remainder to the distant high-rises.

Nothing argues. The world appears arranged.

From the twenty-first floor of the same building, the sunrise becomes something else entirely.
The angle shifts, and with it the hierarchy of illumination. The pink clouds are no longer pink in the same way. What was soft sharpens; what was visible withdraws; what was distant advances. The same sun, encountering the same city, produces a different account of reality.

One begins to suspect that the sunrise is not a thing, but an agreement—fragile, conditional—between height and perception.

You are not seeing different sunrises.
You are seeing the same event from different levels of description. And each level, with quiet stubbornness, insists on its own completeness.

This is the difficulty with the Ukraine debate.

Everyone is watching the same sunrise—the same war, the same May of 2026—but from different floors of the same slightly unsteady building. And because no one specifies their floor, each concludes that the others are either blind or acting in bad faith.

[Yesterday](https://abdymok.substack.com/p/day-1547), in Latvia, we encountered what might be called the architecture of courtesy: drones losing their way with improbable consistency, air defenses responding with professionalism and apology, each side observing the forms while quietly disregarding the substance. Everything proceeded correctly, and almost nothing made sense.

Today, a less polite question suggests itself: what do we mean when we say Ukraine is winning?

------------------------------------------------------------------------

### Layers of Lunacy

Wars, like old cities, accumulate rather than resolve.
Trypillian traces beneath Soviet concrete beneath glass towers reflecting a future no one entirely inhabits. Each layer remains, and each claims, with some justification, to be the real foundation.

Looking at Ukraine in May 2026, one begins to suspect that what analysts call “levels of description” are better understood as layers of managed contradiction—stacked carefully, like floors no one has recently inspected.

Philosophers will note, with some relief, that before asking whether a system has agency, one must specify the level at which the question is posed. A system may be rational at one level and incoherent at another. The answers do not contradict each other; they simply fail to meet.

So it is here.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

### Layer 1: Tactical (Where Reason Still Performs Its Duties)

At the tactical level, the war retains a certain sobriety.
Drones arrive where they are sent. Refineries burn with geometric regularity. Attrition behaves, reassuringly, like arithmetic. Logistics—unimaginative but indispensable—continues to govern outcomes.

Here, the maps produce a coherent sentence: Ukraine adapts, strikes, learns. If one asks, without embellishment, “who is winning the drone war?” the answer, for now, is Ukraine.

On this floor, the sunrise remains orderly.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

### Layer 2: Strategic (Where Reason Becomes Self-Conscious)

Climb one level higher, and reason begins to hesitate, as if aware it is no longer entirely appropriate.

Victory, at this level, has already been redefined. It no longer means breakthrough or triumph, but endurance—the slow raising of the cost of war until it becomes politically intolerable for Russia.

In principle, this is achievable.
In practice, it requires decisions that remain unmade.

Six hundred thousand additional soldiers are required—and are not mobilized, because mobilization is unpopular during an existential war. The state conducts not only a war, but a continuous referendum on its own survival.

It is not that the necessary actions are unknown.
It is that they are deferred.

Here, the sunrise flickers.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

### Layer 3: Social (Where Sacrifice Is Carefully Distributed)

Higher still, and the tone changes again—not tragic, exactly, but arranged.

Society is invited—persistently, respectfully—to demonstrate virtue through sacrifice. Some accept, some resist, most endure. Meanwhile, above this arrangement, another one develops with less publicity.

In early 2022, while ordinary Ukrainians were deciding whether to stay or flee, certain plans were already advancing elsewhere: not shelters, but estates. Kozyn, with its discreet geometry—R1, R2, R3, R4—offered continuity of a different kind, financed through the quiet circulations of energy and defense.

The principle is simple enough:
those who die will be honored; those who remain near the center will be accommodated.

Patriotism becomes, in this configuration, a system of allocation—dignity downward, comfort upward.

A society can sustain this—for a time.
But not indefinitely.

Here, the sunrise is observed through well-insulated glass.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

### Layer 4: Nuclear (Where Reality Is Replaced by Announcement)

At the highest level, the structure no longer attempts coherence.

If victory at the strategic level depends on raising the cost of war, then the counter-move is equally clear: raise the stakes faster than the cost can accumulate.

Actors unable to secure immediate objectives speak with composure about ultimate ones. Nuclear weapons reappear—not as instruments of use, but as instruments of suggestion. Alongside them, another line of effort: the removal of uncertainty by removing the decision-maker.

A different negotiator, a different Ukraine—that, too, is a form of victory.

Here, the sunrise is no longer seen.
It is declared.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

### The Real Question

So: is Ukraine winning?

The question does not fail; it disperses—unless one specifies the floor.

- **Tactical:** Ukraine is winning the drone war, the daily work of adaptation.

- **Strategic:** Ukraine is pursuing a winnable strategy—raising the cost of war—but without fully committing to the means required to sustain it.

- **Social:** Ukraine distributes sacrifice in ways that reveal the limits of its cohesion.

- **Nuclear:** The war expands upward into signaling, where outcomes are shaped by perception as much as force.

Victory is possible.
But it is conditional.

It depends on two absences: that nuclear escalation remains theatrical, and that internal rupture does not occur.

One cannot win an attrition war below while losing coherence above. Russia, for its part, inverts the problem: losing tactically, it escalates vertically—nuclear signaling, informational pressure, and the persistent search for a political break inside Ukraine itself.

The contest is no longer horizontal.
It is architectural.

Which layer gives way first?

Both systems exhibit strain. Neither maintains what might be called unified agency across its own structure. Victory, in its classical sense, requires alignment. What is visible instead is misalignment, carefully managed.

The building remains standing.
But the floors have ceased to recognize one another.
