Five stories from this week. Separate on the surface. One mechanism underneath, if you know where to look: the extraordinary measure, invoked as necessity, that quietly becomes the ordinary way of doing business.

**1. The Monaco hitwoman**
([Interfax-Ukraine, July 7](https://interfax.com.ua) / [ZN.ua](https://zn.ua/ukr/UKRAINE/pidozrjuvanu-u-zamakhu-na-jermolajeva-znajshli-mertvoju-bilja-kijeva-zmi.html))

June 29: an explosion in Monaco critically injures sanctioned Dnipro businessman Vadym Yermolaiev, his companion, and his 13-year-old son.

Interpol identifies the suspect: Anastasiia Berezovska, a Ukrainian citizen reportedly traveling disguised as a man. She re-enters Ukraine on July 1.

By July 3, Ukraine’s Prosecutor General opens a case, attempted contract murder of multiple victims. Investigators follow her contacts after her return: family, and two men, one a former law enforcement officer, the other a serving officer in Defense Intelligence (HUR). Both had transferred her money, via crypto and bank, enough to put them on the radar.

Under questioning, the HUR officer confesses: he killed her, together with the other man. He claims he acted alone, no orders, no reporting up the chain, no authorization for the contact, the payments, or the killing itself.

A search of the former officer’s home finds a basement room resembling a torture chamber. A reconstruction leads to her body: gunshot wounds to the head, shell casings recovered.

Both men are charged with premeditated murder by a group acting in concert. The SBU confirms the account. Prosecutors say Monaco has been fully briefed. The original заказчик, the one who sent her, remains unknown.

In plain terms: a state security officer carried out an extrajudicial killing, entirely outside his chain of command, inside a case his own government had just opened. The exception, here, isn’t the murder. It’s that no ordinary process, no order, no oversight, no authorization, was apparently required for a servant of the state to decide a woman should die before she could talk.

**2. The gag order**
([Radio Svoboda, July 7](https://www.radiosvoboda.org/a/news-rozsliduvannia-sukhachov-cpk-sud/33798487.html))

Journalists spend months documenting 143 properties linked to the brother of the head of Ukraine’s State Bureau of Investigation, the agency meant to investigate official corruption.

The story never runs.

Instead, the brother’s company sues. A Kyiv court bans publication outright, preemptively, before a single line appears.

In plain terms: prior restraint, a legal instrument meant for genuine, provable, irreversible harm, deployed here against a story that hadn’t even been published. The exception swallows the rule it was carved out from.

**3. The explosion no one owns**
([YouTube, July 6](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=BX6rd0tY_8c))

A blast at a munitions depot in Vyshneve, near Kyiv. The footage suggests multiple stockpiled explosives detonating together, not a single strike.

The obvious questions: why was a major ammo depot placed in a residential area, and why was so much concentrated in one location?

Instead of answers, the president goes on camera asking his own security services to investigate, publicly outsourcing responsibility to the same system responsible for making sure stockpiles of explosive materials are not stored in the middle of a populated area.

In plain terms: the ordinary machinery of accountability, an investigation, is invoked here as a way to defer accountability rather than deliver it. Asking the question in public substitutes for answering it.

**4. Sanctioning a critic**
([Interfax-Ukraine, July 7](https://interfax.com.ua/news/general/1182801.html))

Z signs a sanctions package: Russian propagandists, a Moscow official, companies feeding Russia’s missile industry. Standard, justified.

Folded into the same decree, alongside fourteen Russians, is one Ukrainian: Boryslav Bereza, former MP and government critic.

Bereza’s response: being sanctioned by both Putin and Z is “bingo.” He’s not wrong.

In plain terms: sanctions are, by design, an emergency power, no trial, no charge, no right of appeal, justified only by wartime necessity. That is precisely what makes the instrument attractive for uses a courtroom would never permit. The exception was built for the enemy. It gets used on whoever is inconvenient.

**5. The drone maker raid**
([Kyiv Independent, July 8](https://kyivindependent.com/top-ukrainian-drone-ceo-searched-by-law-enforcement/))

July 7: the SBI conducts roughly 70 searches targeting Vyriy Industries, offices, contractors, even the CEO’s family homes.

Vyriy produces roughly a quarter of Ukraine’s FPV drones, at prices low enough to disrupt the market. The allegation: price inflation on about \$157 million in state contracts, use of shell companies, and nearly \$900,000 in undeclared cash on site.

No charges yet. CEO Oleksiy Babenko says he is a witness, not a suspect. The cash, he says, is payroll, withdrawn the way Ukrainian firms often operate. The accusation, he says, makes no economic sense.

Industry reaction is telling. Concern isn’t about compliance, it’s about motive. One veteran-led initiative warns this could mark the start of something broader: corporate raiding, political pressure, or a reshaping of the market. Another source puts it more bluntly: the real question is not the producer, but who upstream benefits from removing a cheap, high-output competitor.

In plain terms: a search-and-seizure regime built for wartime urgency lands on the cheapest, most productive contractor in the sector, while costlier, better-connected ones proceed untouched. The emergency justifies the raid. It does not explain the target.

A killing with no order behind it.
A gag order with no publication to justify it.
An investigation that exists to avoid a finding.
A sanction that requires no proof.
A raid that requires no charge.

Ukraine is fighting two wars. One is against an invading army. The other is against the normalization of the exception, the wartime logic holding that due process, a free press, and reasoned prosecution are luxuries for peacetime, and that whoever controls the emergency measure gets to decide who it applies to.

Each of these five stories is the same discovery, made separately: that the extraordinary tool, once available, gets reached for on whoever is inconvenient, not only whoever is dangerous. Parliament cannot investigate who failed to prepare the country for invasion, or who has profited since, because the people empowered to authorize that investigation are the same people who would be its subject.

Russia never needed perfect spies. It needed something simpler: people positioned inside the right systems, security services, courts, procurement, who found it convenient to be useful, and profitable to stay so.

That arrangement does not disappear when the war starts. It adapts, and it learns to wear the emergency’s own clothing.

The exception was supposed to be temporary. Increasingly, it is just how the state now operates, and it keeps getting the last word, after the facts are fixed, after the witness is gone, after the story is stopped before it runs.
