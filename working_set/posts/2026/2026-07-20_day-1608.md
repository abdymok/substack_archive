There were many more people on Saturday afternoon at the Missing in Action rally on Independence Square than a block away at Ivan Franko Square protesting Z’s decision to sack Defense Minister Mykhailo Fedorov.

One crowd was searching for sons, husbands and fathers swallowed by Russia’s war. The other was trying to make sense of a political system that suddenly seemed to be talking to itself.

A few hours later, Russia settled the argument in its customary way. Ballistic missiles pounded Kyiv for hours. Fires broke out across the city. The entrance to Lukianivska metro station, where generations of Kyivans have sheltered from Russian air raids, was itself shattered by the blast wave.

Wars have a way of restoring perspective. They also expose whether a political system can still hear itself think.

The most revealing sentence of the week came not from the protesters but from Z himself.

“Without me,” he told reporters, “they don’t sit down together.”

He wasn’t speaking metaphorically. Ukraine’s defense minister and commander-in-chief had stopped talking to one another. Messages passed through the president.

It reminded me of something I wrote years ago about corruption. Corruption wasn’t the disease. It was the thermometer. The disease was a political system that had stopped correcting itself.

The *Financial Times* [reports](https://www.ft.com/content/1b34caa6-d909-4df8-a90f-b928f2199261?syn-25a6b1a6=1) that Z is now weighing Syrsky’s dismissal after firing Fedorov. The headlines are about personalities. The more interesting story is about feedback.

Every institution depends on disagreement. Engineers argue with engineers. Generals argue with civilians. The argument isn’t the malfunction. It’s the repair mechanism.

Russia long ago found a different repair mechanism. It doesn’t correct mistakes. It consumes the people who point them out. For more than a century the Kremlin has devoured its own prodigal children. Ukraine has always been noisier. People argue, protest, resign, leak, complain. That noise is not a defect. It is often the sound of a society correcting itself.

Which is why the danger isn’t that Ukrainians gathered in Franko Square to criticize their government. The danger is if, somewhere behind the walls of Bankova, disagreement no longer circulates unless it passes through a single office.

From the outside, everything still looks normal. Orders are issued. Press conferences are held. The machinery keeps running.

Only the feedback has been disconnected.

Sunday’s missile barrage answered none of the questions raised in Franko Square. It merely reminded everyone that Russia gets a vote.

Wars are won not only by courage or technology, but by institutions capable of hearing bad news before the enemy delivers worse.

Last week, the quietest sentence was also the loudest.

“Without me they don’t sit down together.”

Everything else followed from that.
