**The Shooting**

On Saturday, April 18, a gunman opened fire in central Kyiv, killing at least seven people and wounding others. Body camera footage later revealed what happened when two patrol officers arrived at the scene: they saw wounded civilians including a child who was bleeding and pleading for help. Instead of engaging the shooter, the officers ran away. As Prosecutor General Ruslan Kravchenko stated, the armed man “continued unimpeded to move down the street and shoot defenseless passersby.” The gunman was eventually killed by special forces, but not before the damage was done.

[Video: https://youtu.be/bAXcWm1\_k1s]

The officers faced charges of negligence with grave consequences—potentially five years imprisonment. The incident sparked immediate public outrage and raised urgent questions about accountability, training, and leadership.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

You know, there’s a curious thing about watching a man respond to a crisis. You can tell everything about him by which crises he rushes to meet, and which ones he approaches as if they were invoices requiring a filing stamp.

President Z, when faced with the Kyiv shooting, demonstrated remarkable flexibility. On April 19—his first response—he was calm, measured, procedurally sound. An investigation was underway. Personnel decisions would be made. Protocols required review. Very sensible. He mentioned it the way one mentions a plumbing problem: real, requiring attention, but not something that demands you raise your voice.

Then came April 20. Overnight, something changed. The officers hadn’t merely failed to act; they “should have done everything to stop the killer” but instead “fled.” There must be accountability. Accountability! A word that had been oddly absent the day before, suddenly appeared, impossible to miss.

What happened in those twenty-four hours? Public outrage, certainly. But also something more revealing: Z discovered that condemning the officers cost him nothing politically. Everyone agreed. This was the rare moment when moral clarity and popular sentiment aligned perfectly. So he seized it.

**The Difficult Questions**

This is the pattern worth examining. Z can be decisive. What he avoids is the harder thing: taking positions when the cost is real.

Consider the questions that followed the shooting. Should Ukraine mobilize its police force to the front, despite domestic security needs? Should civilians be permitted to own handguns for self-defense? How should post-war demobilization work? On these matters, Z remains silent—not because he’s unclear about the facts, but because any answer alienates someone. Military commanders want police manpower. Police families want their relatives home. Gun control advocates oppose armed citizenry. Security hawks demand preparation.

Rather than navigate this tension, Z has perfected what might be called the “director of victories” approach. He claims credit for successes, distances himself from failures, and speaks with moral force precisely when speaking costs him nothing. When the bill comes due, he’s elsewhere.

This isn’t cowardice in the physical sense. It’s something more interesting: the spinelessness of a leader who avoids structural decisions to preserve popularity. A genuine statesman would accept the cost of unpopular positions. Z calculates the cost and moves on.

**The Baltic Misstep**

Recent events reinforce this pattern. In a Sunday interview, Z speculated that Russia is preparing a major attack on the Baltic states and used this to question NATO’s commitment to Article 5. Estonian officials [responded](https://news.liga.net/ua/politics/news/estonskyy-ministr-zaperechyv-zelenskomu-shcho-rosiia-mozhe-napasty) with the kind of patience one extends to an unreliable acquaintance. Foreign Minister Margus Tsahkna said Z’s claims don’t match their intelligence assessments and that Russia is actually weak militarily and economically.

More pointedly, Parliamentary commission chair Marko Mihkelson observed that Ukraine keeps “pointing fingers at Europe” with warnings that if Ukraine loses, the Baltics are next. This feeds Russian narratives, he noted, and should be discussed quietly between allies, not broadcast to the press like a cry for help.

Z’s apparent goal—to pressure allies for more aid by raising existential threats—backfired. Instead of rallying support, he generated friction with committed allies and gave ammunition to those who argue he weaponizes fear to extract resources.

**A Forced Reckoning**

There’s an irony worth noting: Z appears to have finally abandoned the pursuit of American favor. For over a year after Trump’s return, he played the long game with remarkable patience: participated in fruitless peace talks, agreed to mineral deals meant to enrich Americans, lavished praise on Trump himself. But as U.S. aid dried up and pressure mounted to cede Ukrainian territory, something shifted.

Now Z speaks with unusual candor about [American alignment with Russia](https://archive.ph/SULLP#selection-607.0-1050.0) and the need for Europe to build independent security architecture—language that would have been unthinkable a year ago. “Russia played the Americans again,” he said recently. He’s advocating for a European security framework independent of NATO, seeking new military partners, and attacking Russian oil infrastructure despite American objections.

But notice: this honesty emerged not from principle but from necessity. Ukraine’s improved military position—driven by homegrown drone innovation and battlefield success—gave Z the confidence to stop performing. He could finally afford to tell the truth.

Even this demonstrates the pattern. Z speaks honestly when circumstances eliminate the option of silence. When he has the luxury of deflection, he takes it. The moment the calculation changes, so does his rhetoric.

**The Question Remains**

Z can be decisive when it matters and costs him nothing. He can be honest when forced by circumstance. The question that lingers is whether he’ll ever take a genuinely difficult position—one where conviction matters more than calculation.

Based on the record, the answer appears to be no. And that, perhaps, tells us everything we need to know.
