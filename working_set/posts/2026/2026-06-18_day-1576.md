Kyiv runs on a small number of rooms built for exactly one purpose: letting a muckraker, a case officer, and a man who introduces himself only as being “in logistics” share a bar without anyone having to admit which one he is.

Decadence on Rustaveli was one of these once. Opened in 2004 as an Italian restaurant by day and a disco on weekends — no signboard over the door, strict face control, curtained couch-zones for those who preferred their evenings unobserved — it ran for several years as the defining address for the city’s nightlife elite before closing around 2010. Many venues since have tried to fill the same room, and several have managed it, at least for a season. Decadence faded into oblivion rather than be switched off, which here counts as a dignified exit.

[Tootsie Club](https://uk.tootsie.kiev.ua/) did not get that courtesy. It was switched off, by force, in January of last year, when searches turned up rather more than dancers — an underground brothel, women coerced under threat, hidden cameras confirmed to have been filming guests for blackmail, and a quantity of drugs and weapons no theatre café should require. The premises sit inside a Neo-Renaissance monument from 1901, once a salon for the city’s literary intelligentsia, later a deli, then a casino, then Tootsie.

Kyiv’s rooms close for every reason except the one anyone admits to out loud — irrelevance, or eventually the goons — which is the local specialty: managing the appearance of an ending rather than its substance.

I was reminded of a particular kind of wartime sketch early this morning, when a founding member of this publication — an old friend and a veteran of many an evening at Tootsie in its heyday — sent me [a link](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=3njlIlPayYg&t=1954s) to a curious conversation, with no message attached beyond an unusual quantity of exclamation marks, which from him constitutes a full literary review.

Forty-five minutes of two middle-aged white American men talking the way men talk when they have already decided they agree: warmly, quickly, with the cadence of people building a case for the room rather than examining one. One had run operations against the Iranians for three decades. The other edits an investigative outlet. Between them sat a combined expertise spanning the Middle East, the Caucasus, and most of what used to be called the Near Abroad. Neither man has ever been within a thousand miles of Lake Baikal. Neither, I would wager what remains of my friend’s Tootsie tab, has ever gone in — not even to the ankle.

The trouble is not distance from the subject. The trouble is mistaking warmth for depth, the way a man in a sauna mistakes a temperature someone else set for a fact about his own body. And what these two gentlemen were warm about was Kyrylo Budanov, whom one described — not exaggerating — as “beloved.” The Jason Bourne of Ukraine, he called him, a phrase that sounds like a compliment until you remember that Jason Bourne is a deniable asset whose own employers were never quite sure who he was working for by the third act.

The case rested on three pillars, none of which would survive five minutes in a Kyiv kitchen with the lights on: that he was built into a unit assembled by people Langley trusted; that he is known “intimately” for “a decade,” as though intimacy were a vetting procedure rather than often its opposite; and that he has personally inconvenienced a great many Russians — true, admirable, and entirely beside the question of whether the man now sitting across a negotiating table on Ukraine’s behalf can be trusted with the rest of the country’s business.

None of the following made it into the forty-five minutes: that Budanov, a fortnight into his intelligence appointment, attributed a Ukrainian operation to the Russian Federal Security Service agent on camera, that he was found sharing an estate with the family of a deputy interior minister whose résumé includes a Rostov childhood and considerable proximity to the president, that the chair he now occupies was vacated by a man who had himself arranged for thirty-three mercenaries to land in the wrong country’s custody so as not to spoil a ceasefire announcement. The chair was not cleaned before the new man sat down. It was simply reupholstered, and the upholstery is doing a magnificent job.

There was also, earlier in those same forty-five minutes, a word offered as a compliment: a visiting officer, fresh from the field, had called the whole arrangement “a lab experiment.” This publication had occasion to use the same word twice in the preceding eight days, in neither case as a compliment — once to note that a country can supply battlefield adaptability and own none of the industry that results, and once to observe that the laboratory, as always, belongs to whoever owns the building. The two gentlemen were filing a confirmation report on an argument already on file. They had simply not been told which side of the glass they were standing on.

The two gentlemen moved on to discussing Cuba and what makes a good dive bar, on which subject I have no quarrel with them whatsoever. A man can be entirely wrong about Kyiv and still know his way to last call. I only wish that before the next forty-five minutes, someone would hand him a map of the lake.
