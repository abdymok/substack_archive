This morning, during my constitutional — a modest act of civic maintenance — I surrendered my thoughts to Huxe’s algorithm. It is a modern habit: instead of consulting sages, we consult the feed.

Around mile four it queued up U.S. War Secretary Pete briefing journalists with John about the [Iran operation](https://example.com). One imagines maps, acronyms, firm nods. Then came little [Marco Rubio’s press spray](https://example.com) — a phrase that suggests either light rain or industrial solvent. After him, [Sean Hannity chatting with Benjamin Netanyahu](https://example.com), both sounding as though history were something that could be arranged with sufficient confidence.

Next was [Trey Yingst reporting from Israel](https://example.com) — the chief foreign correspondent of [Fox News](https://example.com), who balances water bottles on door handles and prepares his mind through cold plunges. The ancients had ascetics. We have idiot correspondents.

When I returned home, I opened a quieter document.

The Constitution of the United States. Specifically, Article I, Section 8.

Clause 11 reads:

> “The Congress shall have Power… To declare War, grant Letters of Marque and Reprisal, and make Rules concerning Captures on Land and Water.”

Article I enumerates the powers of Congress.
Article II defines the President as “Commander in Chief” of the armed forces.

The framers divided authority with deliberate suspicion:

The power to start war → Congress.
The power to conduct war → President.

The framers separated ignition from steering because they understood momentum. Once something is set in motion, it does not pause to reconsider the fine print.
