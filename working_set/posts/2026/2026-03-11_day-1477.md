I once heard about a man in Vilkhivets — though perhaps it was not Vilkhivets; perhaps it was Yakhny; my neighbor from Semihory told the story, and he had the habit of knowing everyone — who spent several years in his youth stealing goats.

He was not, by temperament, a thief.

My neighbor from Semihory said he was simply a young man with an unusual amount of moral energy and nowhere particular to put it. The goats, in this sense, were incidental.

When he stopped stealing goats, he did not simply stop. He became, with the same enormous energy, a passionate opponent of goat theft.

He gave speeches.
He wrote pamphlets.
He visited schools.

He could describe, in devastating detail, the psychology of the goat thief, the social conditions that produce such a person, the algorithmic pathways by which an impressionable youth encounters his first stolen goat and discovers — to his surprise — that it feels like solidarity.

The schools found him very impressive. He had, after all, been there.

My neighbor from Semihory observed that this man knew more about goat theft than anyone alive, and understood it less than almost anyone — and that these two facts were not unconnected.

## I. On the Interview as Genre

I raise this man only because I have been watching, with the mild fascination one gives a persistent weather system, the career of a young Irish influencer and vlogger who works in Ukraine and interviews presidents on camera while air raid sirens sound tastefully in the background.

The interview in question lasts approximately twenty-two minutes.

In the first two of these minutes the influencer explains to us — and incidentally to Z, who is present — what the interview will be about, why it matters, and what the world is currently experiencing.

Z, who has been experiencing the world rather intensively for several years, waits with the patience of a man who has learned to wait for many things.

It is a feature of a certain kind of vlogging that the vlogger’s presence in a place becomes the news.

Not: what is happening here.
But rather: I am here, observing what is happening, and I have feelings about it.

The war becomes a backdrop.
The backdrop becomes atmosphere.
The atmosphere becomes — and here a small alchemy occurs — a credential.

“How does that feel?” the vlogger asks, when the siren sounds.

It is a question of some metaphysical ambition.

One imagines Z briefly considering the entire catalogue of what he feels: the particular weight of responsibility, the calculus of acceptable losses, the memory of his children’s faces when he last saw them, the specific texture of fatigue that is not tiredness but something older.

Then he says, with the brevity of a man who has learned that honesty and completeness are not the same thing:

“I don’t have too much time to feel.”

It is, as answers go, a complete essay.

The influencer moves on to the next question.

## II. On the Redemption Arc, and Its Maintenance Costs

The influencer’s story, which he tells with practiced fluency, is genuinely interesting.

A gay boy from Kilkenny.
A Catholic school.
A migration to London in search of tolerance.
An encounter instead with men who preferred him out of their neighborhood.
A mass shooting in Orlando.
A YouTube search.

And then — the particular horror of the modern world — a recommendation engine that knows what you are looking for before you know it yourself.

He spent three years making videos for people who were, by his later account, building toward real-world violence.

He left.
He recovered.

He now makes videos about those people, explaining how they work.

Which is to say: he makes videos with the same urgency, the same production values, the same millions of views, the same essential grammar of civilizational threat — only the villain has changed.

The villain is now more straightforwardly villainous, which makes everything much more comfortable.

A man who has changed his cause has not necessarily changed his method.
He has merely found better material.

He received, recently, an award: the Honorary Ambassador of Ukraine Award for Digital Diplomacy, issued by the Ministry of Foreign Affairs and supported by the president’s wife.

“I’m so proud,” he said while accepting it, “to be able to show the resilience of Ukraine and tell the truth about what this incredible country represents.”

I do not mock this sentiment.

I mock only its texture, which is the texture of a press release rearranged into the first person.

“Show the resilience.”
“Tell the truth.”
“This incredible country.”

These phrases mean everything in general and refer to nothing in particular. They are the verbal equivalent of a flag — all color and flutter, the flag itself never explaining what it is planted in.

There is a Ukrainian word, or so I am told by people who know Ukrainian, for the feeling of hospitality that persists even in terrible circumstances.

Z speaks of it at the end of the interview, haltingly, with the grammar of a man translating from a language that does not translate.

It is the most alive moment in twenty-two minutes of footage.

The influencer thanks him and calls it amazing.

## III. On the Comfortable Comparison

Around the sixteenth minute, as reliably as a clock striking the hour, the vlogger raises Hitler.

Fascists use sport to cover up their crimes.
The 1936 Olympics.
We know the end of Hitler.
We understand the end of Putin.

Z agrees, as he must.

The influencer agrees with Z agreeing with him.

One has the impression of two men standing before a mirror, each gesturing toward the reflection of the other.

The Hitler comparison is not wrong, exactly.

It is something more troubling than wrong: it is correct in ways that make thinking unnecessary.

Once you have said Hitler, the thinking stops.
The emotion begins.
The camera — which has been waiting for this — leans in.

And the influencer, who has spent years studying how extremist videos achieve their emotional effects, deploys those effects with the confidence of a man who has discovered that a technique is not made innocent by its new employer.

*My neighbor from Semihory would probably have said that the only real difference between a former propagandist and a current one is the direction in which the wind happens to be blowing.*

## IV. What My Neighbor from Semihory Would Have Said

My neighbor from Semihory, who distrusted anyone with a clean conscience, would have liked the influencer well enough.

He would have noted that the vlogger is not stupid, not cynical in any simple way, not indifferent to the suffering he films.

He would have noted that the war is real, the drones are real, the dead are real, and that any camera pointed honestly at any of it performs a service.

But he would also have noted — pouring a second glass and settling back — that there exists a type of person for whom belief is not a position but a metabolism.

Such a person does not choose causes.
He processes them.

The far-right was a cause.
Ukraine is a cause.

Both causes produce the same heat, the same output, the same sensation of being — at last — on the right side of something enormous.

The only difference is that one cause was monstrous and the other is just.

Justice, my neighbor from Semihory would have said, does not automatically improve the character of those who are enthusiastic about it.

The goat thief, after all, was genuinely against goat theft.

This was not the problem.

The problem was that he loved the feeling of being against something more than he loved the goats.

The influencer, to his credit, is in Ukraine. He is not filing from a hotel bar in Warsaw or a comfortable desk in West London.

He has been sanctioned by the Russian government and issued with a warrant for his arrest, which is, in its way, an honest review.

He has done things that are difficult and some things that are brave.

And yet — here my neighbor from Semihory would pause, tilting his glass toward the light — the camera follows him through all of it, and the camera’s eye is always, at some angle, admiring.

Not the war.
Not even Z, who is — if you read the transcript carefully — a genuinely complex and melancholy man trying to explain something true in a second language to someone who has already written the caption.

The camera admires the vlogger admiring the war.

This is a small thing.
Perhaps even a forgivable thing.

But it is not quite journalism.

My neighbor from Semihory would have said that it resembles something else: the man who travels very far to visit a holy place and then spends the rest of the evening explaining the journey.
