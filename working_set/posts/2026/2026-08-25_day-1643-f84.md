In Ukraine there exists a species of official action called **негласні слідчі дії** — “covert investigative actions” — a phrase I have always found almost tender, since *негласні* means “voiceless,” as though the state were tiptoeing past your door in its socks, warrant in hand, whispering: *let us not wake her just yet.*

To listen to your telephone, or walk unseen through your apartment while you are out buying bread, the state must first ask an investigating judge — not the prosecutor, who has already convicted you in his heart, but a judge, whose main talent is signing things quickly and worrying about it later.

Then, for months, you go about your business — freezing Russian assets, raising bail money for jailed ex ministers, arranging your daughter’s comforts in the USA — unaware that your voice has quietly become state property, put up like preserves for winter.

And all of this listening must eventually be written down: not played aloud in some cinematic courtroom moment, but typed, sheet by sheet, into a document with the splendid name **протокол НСРД** (NSRD) — the Protocol. Every sigh, every crude joke, every “I’ll call you back,” is numbered like a butterfly pinned to cardboard, catalogued not by beauty but by page.

And it is this typed ghost of your voice that the prosecutor eventually carries into court like a grandfather with a photo album nobody asked to see — reading it aloud, sheet by sheet, while you sit and listen to yourself, performed by a stranger in uniform, saying things you don’t remember saying quite that way.

One rule of decency remains, at least: the album may not be shown to the defense until “declassified” — a word that always struck me as the state’s quiet admission that it was, until that moment, a little ashamed of itself.
