A [fascinating collection](https://szru.gov.ua/news-media/news/putin-vymahaie-rozkhytaty-vnutrishnyu-sytuatsiyu-v-ukraini) of screen captures from online government agency sources on May 20. The urgency and directness of FISU’s [front page](https://szru.gov.ua/) is striking: there’s such pure logic in the stripped design. If you can keep your head while others, et cetera.

And it’s fun to remember how clueless and dotcomified Ukraine’s Foreign Intelligence Service still was that day. You can practically see Oleksandr Litvinenko pacing around in Belgrade with the expression of a provincial bank manager discovering the auditors have arrived early. *If I return to Kyiv, will I still have an office? Will the parking pass work?*

The communiqué has a touching informality. Lower-case abbreviations. Parenthetical whispers. Quotation marks scattered like ash on a ministry carpet. Not the language of intelligence work but the tone of a nervous uncle forwarding Telegram rumors after his third cognac.

Which raises the obvious question: if you truly possessed Kremlin materials, would you present them this way? Would actual intelligence officers write like district-level PR interns in an energy-drink emergency? Or would you quietly use the information for something useful?

But on May 21, the very next day, something interesting happened.

“Ukraine will send reinforcements to its northern regions and step up diplomatic pressure on Belarus to counter what Kyiv believes are Russian plans to launch a new offensive north of the capital,” [Z announced](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2026/may/21/ukraine-war-briefing-fresh-threat-of-attack-from-belarus-front-warns-zelenskyy). “We analysed in detail the available data from our intelligence agencies on Russia’s planning of offensive operations in the Chernihiv-Kyiv direction.”

Kyiv knows of five scenarios Russia has drawn up. Yikes!

So FISU had real intelligence. Actionable intelligence. The kind that moves armies and changes deployment. And yet on May 20, when they addressed the public, what occupied their bandwidth? A lengthy statement about how Russian disinformation campaigns work—complete with lower-case abbreviations and nervous quotation marks.

The subtext becomes clear: while intelligence officers were presumably analyzing Belarusian attack vectors and Chernihiv logistics, someone was deciding they needed to issue a domestic political statement. About foreign interference. About why Ukrainians shouldn’t believe their own skepticism.

Once any uncomfortable question can be described as Russian orchestration, citizens are invited to choose between patriotism and eyesight.

The statement confirms what it denies. Yes, there is a campaign against Z and his circle. But—and here the anxious little quotation marks flutter around like frightened pigeons—Moscow is behind it. For people already skeptical of Z, this sounds less like refutation than confession: the intelligence apparatus entering domestic politics with all the elegance of a drunk traffic policeman directing tanks.
