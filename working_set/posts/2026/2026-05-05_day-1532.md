It is Tuesday, which already feels excessive.

Yesterday, Z was in Yerevan at the European Political Community summit, moving from one bilateral meeting to another with the endurance of a wedding guest who understands that leaving early would be noticed. Finland promised \$300 million in defense support and discussed a drone partnership. Norway offered nearly \$1 billion for air defense and natural gas for winter. Britain discussed sanctions on the shadow fleet and peace negotiations. The Czech Republic spoke again of Ukraine’s future in Europe.

And somewhere on the margins of all this respectable diplomacy, there was also a meeting with Georgian Prime Minister Irakli Kobakhidze, who remains on Ukraine’s own sanctions list.

“There are unresolved issues between our countries,” Z said afterward. “It is important to maintain dialogue at all levels.”

He did not explain what kind of dialogue one conducts with a man one is simultaneously sanctioning. Perhaps this is what modern diplomacy looks like: everyone speaking earnestly while pretending not to notice the seating arrangements.

From Yerevan he flew to Bahrain, where officials confirmed he had arrived for talks on “security cooperation,” a phrase broad enough to include anything from missile defense to polite tea.

The summit circuit continues.

Meanwhile, the ceasefire situation has developed the sort of complexity that usually appears only in old imperial archives.

Russia announced a unilateral ceasefire for May 8–9, not through diplomatic channels but on a state-backed messaging app, while also warning that if Ukrainian drones appeared over Moscow during the Victory Day parade, central Kyiv could expect massive missile strikes. Residents and foreign diplomats were advised, with touching concern for their wellbeing, to leave the city.

Ukraine responded with its own unilateral ceasefire, beginning three days earlier — tonight at midnight.

Neither ceasefire was negotiated. Neither side appears especially committed to the concept. Yesterday, while both governments were drafting statements about peace, Russian strikes killed nine civilians across Ukraine.

Speaking in Yerevan, Z noted that Moscow’s parade this year will proceed without tanks or missile systems.

“They are afraid drones may buzz over Red Square,” he said. “This is telling.”

It is difficult to disagree.

According to a leaked European intelligence report, Vladimir Putin has been spending increasing amounts of time in underground bunkers in Krasnodar Krai and rarely visits his Moscow residences.

History has a vulgar sense of humor.

For two decades, Victory Day served as an annual demonstration of Russian power: tanks crossing Red Square, missiles rolling past reviewing stands, choreographed certainty presented as national destiny.

Now the missiles stay home and the president goes underground.

Such revisions are rarely planned.

And yet the week was not finished.

The Midas Operation recordings remain where they have been for days: on desks, in encrypted chats, in the nervous hands of people deciding whether to publish more names.

MP Yaroslav Zhelezniak says he has reviewed all 1,400 pages of transcripts. He describes three parallel financial ledgers: “Manhattan,” “Project 23,” and “Trading.”

Nuclear energy. Military procurement. Electricity trading.

Three separate compartments, arranged with the careful neatness of people who believed walls could hear but microphones could not.

On the morning of July 8, 2025, at 7:37 a.m., Timur Mindich allegedly called Defense Minister Rustem Umerov.

The factory was about to stop, he explained. Two thousand workers. Bulletproof vests sitting in a warehouse. One phone call from the minister could solve everything.

“Say you don’t want to hear about the vests anymore,” Mindich reportedly suggested, “and I’ll meet with you twice a week.”

They also discussed selling a 33 percent stake in drone manufacturer Fire Point to Emirati investors and the mechanics of structuring the sale.

Umerov has not been charged.

He was moved from the Defense Ministry to secretary of the National Security and Defense Council and remains involved in negotiations with both Russia and the United States.

Yesterday, in Yerevan, he was presumably somewhere nearby, walking through the same hallways as everyone else.

This is also modern governance: one man discussing ceasefires, another discussing parade routes, a third explaining offshore transactions, and all of them insisting these are separate conversations.

Meanwhile, much of the Western press prefers the cleaner narrative: the parade, the bunker, the ceasefires, the symbolism of May 8 and May 9.

It is, admittedly, a very convenient story.

Europe marks one date. Russia marks another. Eighty years of memory compressed into a midnight border between calendars.

Editors appreciate stories that can be illustrated cleanly.

The other story is less cooperative.

It is unfolding in Ukrainian.

And this week, that remains a language many foreign editors still do not read.
