It is Friday again, and there is a great deal I did not write about this week. I did not write about mitochondrial function, about what twenty-five years of regular running does to the cell, about the inner membrane folds that multiply with training until the surface area for generating energy is, in the language of the researchers, dramatically enhanced. I did not write about Kilian Jornet, who posted [a short video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=EXjH3nG8hJU) last week announcing his racing plans for 2026 — Western States, Sierre-Zinal, UTMB — from what appears to be a very old house with a leaking roof, which he likes precisely because it leaks. *If everything is easy,* he said, standing in it, *you get too comfortable. Making things not comfortable is important.* He is forty-one years old. His mitochondria, the researchers would tell you, are in better condition than those of most men half his age.

The running season started two weeks ago. I have been wondering, as I do each year, how long it will take the organelle to snap back into shape — how many weeks of patient z1 work before the machinery remembers what it is for. This is a small and private preoccupation, familiar to readers of this newsletter who have followed my perhaps excessive interest in becoming, if not a better person, at least a better athlete. How one pursues this in a genocidal war, with interrupted sleep and a diet governed by what the shops have and what the power allows one to cook, is a subject for another Friday.

Today I am less concerned with my own mitochondria than with those of the men currently directing Ukraine’s war and American foreign policy.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

The mitochondrion — to briefly inconvenience the reader with biology — is the organelle responsible for converting oxygen and fuel into energy. [Nick Lane](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Zn7bCggQCtQ), professor of evolutionary biochemistry at University College London and author of *Transformer: The Deep Chemistry of Life and Death*, describes what is happening inside it with a precision that is also, somehow, a kind of poetry: we are stripping the electrons out of food, passing them into respiration, passing them on to oxygen to produce water as a waste product. We are, in his phrase, burning hydrogen in oxygen. That is what is powering us. The Krebs cycle — the strange loop of reactions at the heart of this process — is what makes it possible. And what ageing does, Lane notes, is make that cycle slow. The respiration runs down. Instead of burning hydrogen to generate energy, the cycle loses its spin. The intermediates that once built the molecules of life begin to behave differently. The cell, still nominally alive, is running on something closer to memory than metabolism.

*One thinks, in this context, of several people.*

Long-term endurance training counteracts this. It increases both the number and the density of mitochondria in muscle cells. It stimulates the removal of damaged mitochondria and the growth of new ones. Master athletes — older runners with decades of consistent training — carry unique mitochondrial proteins not observed in sedentary people of the same age. The researchers call this maintained capacity. The rest of us call it being able to do hard things without falling apart.

Secretary Hegseth, who has been conducting press briefings on Operation Epic Fury with the confidence of a man who has never had occasion to doubt his instruments, does not appear to be a man who has spent twenty-five years doing hard things in order to find out what he is made of. He appears to be a man who has spent twenty-five years telling us what he is made of, which is a different kind of fitness entirely.

It produces a certain fluency.
A certain ease in front of cameras.
A certain capacity for the declarative sentence.

It does not, as far as the available evidence suggests, produce many mitochondria.

> *[A related data point](https://www.washingtonpost.com/business/2026/03/11/hegseth-press-briefings-photos-iran/), for those keeping records. On March 2nd, Hegseth appeared at the Pentagon briefing room podium for the first time since June — days after the joint strike on Iran that killed Ayatollah Khamenei. Press photographers from the Associated Press, Reuters, and Getty Images were present. They published their photographs, as photographers do. Members of Hegseth’s staff reviewed the images and found them, in the language of two people familiar with the decision, “unflattering.” Photographers were barred from the two subsequent briefings, on March 4th and March 10th. Only the Defense Department’s own staff photographers have been permitted in since. The Pentagon press secretary explained that the department was “using space effectively.” The National Press Photographers Association noted that excluding photographers because officials disliked how they looked in published images was, for a public servant, “not a good look.” One raises this not to mock the secretary’s vanity, exactly, but to observe: a man who has genuinely found out what he is made of tends, over time, to become less concerned with how he appears in photographs. The mitochondria, if maintained, provide a certain equanimity about these things.*

Z is a more complicated case.

Two years into the war, performing at a festival in Jūrmala, Latvia, he stood on a stage and described Ukraine’s condition with the unsentimental accuracy of a man who had not yet decided to be its president:

> *«Україна, якщо чесно, між нами, нагадує актрису з німецьких фільмів для дорослих. Тобто, готова прийняти в будь-якій кількості з будь-якого боку. Якщо вам не шкода.»*

Ukraine, honestly, between us, resembles an actress from German adult films: ready to accept, in any quantity, from any side. If you don’t mind.

He later clarified that the joke was about financial dependence — the IMF, the creditors, the structural condition of a country that cannot say no because it cannot afford to. He was not wrong. He was merely, as it turned out, describing the metabolism he would one day inherit.

The black tactical gear arrived six years later. The condition did not depart with it.

The cell is still running. One watches the briefings, the summits, the appeals, and thinks: this is a man who once named the thing clearly, from the outside, and has since moved inside it — and cannot, from inside, name it anymore.

That is not cowardice. It may simply be what the costume costs.

Both men are in their mid-forties. Both are, by any observable measure, men of considerable output — press conferences, summits, appeals, declarations, briefings, tours of allied capitals in matching outfits. What neither of them appears to have is maintained capacity: the kind that comes not from being inside the story, but from having asked something genuinely hard of the body and the mind, repeatedly, over many years, without an audience. Hegseth has the cameras. Z has the gear. Between them, they have produced an extraordinary quantity of declarative sentences. The mitochondria, one suspects, are another matter.

> *A note, for completeness: the most-lethal, most-precise aerial operation in history, which Hegseth described from Tampa on day six with the composure of a man reading good news, has since been found by its own preliminary investigation to have directed a Tomahawk missile into an elementary school. The walls were blue and pink. The picture was ten years old. There are, officials emphasize, important unanswered questions.*

Kilian Jornet, in his leaking house, chose this year’s race calendar not because it was easy but because it was where the best competition was. He was sick at Western States last year and the execution wasn’t perfect. He intends to go back. This is what twenty-five years of accumulated mitochondrial adaptation looks like as a decision-making framework: you return to the hard thing, you find out what went wrong, and you try again. You do not hold a press conference announcing that the previous attempt was picture perfect.

*The organelle does not care about your confidence.
It cares about what you have asked of it,
and for how long.*

My neighbor from Semihory, who ran every morning until he was seventy-three and who distrusted all men who had never been seriously cold, would have had one question about the current leadership of the American defense establishment. Not about the school. Not about the piano. Not about the ten-year-old picture. He would have asked, quietly, while repacking his pipe: when did any of these men last do something that required them to find out what they were made of?

And then he would have waited, patiently, like a man whose mitochondria were, all things considered, in excellent condition.
