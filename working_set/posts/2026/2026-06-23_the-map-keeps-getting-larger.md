I should say at the outset that I am not an impartial witness. This seems obvious, but it is worth stating, because a great deal of writing about Ukraine has been produced by people who believed themselves to be impartial witnesses and were not, and the difference between knowing this about yourself and not knowing it turns out to matter considerably.

I have lived in Kyiv since 1999. Before that I spent years on a hill above Berlin — Teufelsberg, the devil’s mountain, a heap of rubble the size of a small Alp constructed from the wreckage of the Third Reich and topped with listening domes — intercepting Soviet and East German military communications. The job was to detect signals that the other side considered impossible to detect, to find the side-loop emissions from relay towers at distances the East had decided were beyond the range of such frequencies. That was their error. The signals were there. You simply had to know what you were listening for.

I mention this not as biography but as method. The hill does not leave you when you leave the hill. You carry forward a permanent suspicion of institutional assessments, a specific impatience with what intelligence analysts call “bounded rationality” — the assumption that adversaries will behave within the limits of what seems reasonable to the person doing the assuming. This assumption has cost more lives than most weapons. Putin, as I have noted here many times across many years, is not a bounded-rationality problem. He is a man who does what he says he will do. The only mystery is why this keeps surprising people.

\* \* \*

After Berlin, before Ukraine, there was Russia and Belarus, but that’s another story.

\* \* \*

The archive before you contains 1,029 posts across six years: 2021 through the third week of June 2026. Of these, roughly 750 were published and the rest abandoned — drafted, reconsidered, left in the condition of thoughts a man decides not to finish in front of witnesses. The published pieces total somewhere north of 400,000 words.

Of those words, the overwhelming majority were written after February 24, 2022. Such is the arithmetic of full-scale invasion: years of intermittent observation compressed into a daily obligation that has not yet ended. Between January 2021 and the morning the Russian army attempted to decapitate the capital, there were 42 posts. From that morning through the week before this preamble was written, there were nearly a thousand more. The longest concerns Vladimir Putin, Karl Popper, and the Russian Orthodox Church, which explains, without further comment, why so many others are so short.

The subject is a country that has been called, variously, a post-Soviet democracy, a failed state, a frontline civilization, a laboratory for Western defense technology, and a living argument against the idea that history moves in one direction. I have called it none of these things. The people who have are mostly writing from a comfortable distance, which is itself a useful fact about their descriptions.

\* \* \*

The governing method of this archive is conjecture and refutation — Popper’s phrase, adopted not as academic decoration but as operational discipline. You state what you believe to be true. You look for the evidence that would falsify it. You update when you find it. You do not protect your prior assessments by refusing to look.

This sounds simple. It is not simple. It is especially not simple when the thing you are trying to assess is also, intermittently, trying to kill you.

What it produced, across six years, is a record with a property I will describe plainly because there is no modest way to describe it: the predictions mostly held. Phase 3 of the war — the protracted slog of de-occupation — was named and described in May 2022 as the coming phase, the longest and most deadly, something that would last months or years. It has now lasted four years. The mobilization crisis was documented as a systemic failure before it was acknowledged as one. The fortification deficit was flagged before Avdiivka fell. The institutional rot — documented in the Wagnergate posts of early 2021, visible in the December 2019 transcript of officials trying to explain macroeconomics to a president who did not know the difference between a balance of payments and a balance of trade, traceable to every SBU scandal and parliamentary maneuver of the pre-invasion years — did not emerge from the war. The war revealed what was already there.

None of this is presented as clairvoyance. The archive contains wrong calls, dead ends, and positions later revised. What it does not contain is the retrospective adjustment of memory that afflicts most war commentary — the gradual rewriting of what one thought at the time into what one now believes one should have thought. The posts are timestamped, public, and unchanged. This is the only form of epistemic accountability available to a writer who does not work for an institution that can protect him from being wrong in public. I have been wrong in public. The record shows it. It also shows, I think, the more important thing.

\* \* \*

A word about voice, since readers sometimes ask.

I call Z “Ukraine’s moron president” in the first post of this archive, in January 2021, and continue doing so through posts that simultaneously document his government’s intelligence failures with granular sourcing, named sources, and classified testimony. Some readers find this inconsistent. It is not inconsistent. It is the specific position of someone who wanted the country to survive and watched its leadership make survival harder than it needed to be — someone who had, as described above, watched this particular dynamic operate before, in another capital, and knew how it tended to end when the institutions built to check executive power were systematically hollowed out before the crisis arrived.

The acid in the prose is not performance. It is the residue of watching things that could have been prevented not be prevented, and of writing about them in real time for an audience that could see the same evidence.

The treatment of the Western press — the ISW analysts with their bounded-rationality models, the correspondents who spent eight years calling Russian-directed fighters “rebels and separatists” and have since, in some cases, apologized, and in most cases not — is similarly specific. I tracked this not as sport but because the quality of Western analysis about Ukraine had direct consequences for the quality of Western policy, and the quality of Western policy was, from 2022 onward, a matter of life and death for people within earshot.

\* \* \*

The posts collected here begin in a city that was at peace, or what passed for peace in a country that had been at war in its east since 2014, and end in a city that has absorbed four years of bombardment and is still, against considerable evidence, standing.

The first year documents what the war would inherit. The second year documents the invasion itself, day by day, from this apartment, at the pace the days arrived. The years that followed document the long, grinding, expensive, partially preventable catastrophe of Phase 3, in which Ukraine demonstrated simultaneously, and in equal measure, its capacity for tactical improvisation and its incapacity for institutional reform.

Long before the archive began, it occurred to me that the first year of a design education should focus on nothing but maps. Students would be dropped into alien environments — Luhansk, say — armed with whatever maps were available, and required to figure out where they were. The point was not cartography. The point was to learn what a map actually does: not describe the territory but navigate it, imposing just enough structure on chaos to make movement possible.

This archive is a map of that kind. Ukraine is among the more alien environments available to the Western reader — a country that has been mislocated, mislabeled, and misread for thirty years by people holding the wrong chart. What follows is one attempt at orientation, conducted in real time, from inside the territory, over six years. The maps are imperfect. The environment kept changing.

What I can tell you is that the wolf they were looking for in the Botanical Garden on January 5, 2022 — trackers with heat-vision goggles, five in the morning, 117 hectares, no luck — was never found. Seven weeks later, Russia invaded. The wolf, presumably, is still out there.

\* \* \*

*Kyiv, June 2026*

## A Reader’s Guide to the ukraine@war Archive, 2021–2026

------------------------------------------------------------------------

There is a particular kind of boredom that comes from being right too early. It is not quite the same as ordinary boredom, which can be relieved by a walk or a glass of wine. It is closer to the boredom of a man who has already read the last page of the book and must now sit politely through the middle chapters while everyone else discovers what he has known for some time. I do not recommend it. But I have had considerable practice.

What follows is a guide to the posts in this archive that did the most work — not the most entertaining, not the most widely read, but the ones that named what was happening before it was comfortable to name it, and the ones whose predictions turned out to matter. I have tried to say, for each year, what the year was actually about, which is sometimes quite different from what it appeared to be about at the time.

\* \* \*

### 2021: The Rot Was Already There

The year before the full-scale invasion is not, in retrospect, a prelude. It is an explanation. Everything that would fail in 2022, 2023, 2024, and beyond had already failed by the time this archive opened in January 2021. The question was not whether the institutions were compromised. The question was whether anyone in a position to do something about it was willing to look.

[SBU Goes Coup Coup](https://abdymok.substack.com/p/sbu-goes-coup-coup) (January 25) opens the record with the SBU — Ukraine’s State Security Service, the institution whose primary job was to detect and counter Russian intelligence operations — actively eating itself over control of contraband revenue. I made an observation then that seems worth repeating now: an institution fighting over illicit income is not, simultaneously, an institution capable of executing a sophisticated operation against Russian war criminals. These things are mutually exclusive not as a moral proposition but as a practical one. You cannot do both. The SBU had chosen.¹

[Ukraine is Not a Piano](https://abdymok.substack.com/p/wagnergate) (February 28) and the Wagnergate series (March 3) document the fate of an operation that had taken years to build: a plan to lure Wagner PMC fighters — several of whom had shot down Ukrainian military aircraft and the MH17 airliner, killing 298 people — out of Russia and into Ukrainian custody. The operation was prepared brilliantly, down to the smallest detail, according to the man who authorized it. In the last hours before the arrests, information was leaked from the President’s Office to Moscow. The mercenaries went home. Andriy Yermak, head of that Office, said the entire affair was “a detective story made up from A to Z.” Several Ukrainian intelligence officers contradicted him under oath. Their testimony was classified. It remains classified.²

I want to be precise about what this means. This is not merely a story about a botched operation. It is a story about an institution — the presidency — that, when confronted with evidence of its own culpability in a catastrophic security failure, chose to lie, systematically, to the parliament, to the press, and to the Western partners who had cooperated on the operation. This choice was made in 2020. The same institution would be managing a full-scale war by February 2022.

[Operation Г](https://abdymok.substack.com/p/ukraines-plan-) (September 11) follows the parliamentary investigation into this affair — an investigation chaired by a doctor with no military or intelligence background, which produced a report raising more questions than it answered and was largely ignored.³ The Soviet comedy Operation Ы features a criminal who practices for a warehouse heist by placing a handkerchief over the nose of a ceramic cat. I had used this comparison once already. I would use it again.

[Z’s New Clothes](https://abdymok.substack.com/p/zelenskys-new-clothes) (November 30) introduces a distinction from game theory that proved useful throughout the following years: the difference between mutual knowledge — information that everyone knows — and common knowledge, which is not only information that everyone knows, but information that everyone knows that everyone else knows. The proposition that Z was unqualified to lead a country at war had been approaching mutual knowledge in Ukraine for some time. His five-and-a-half-hour press conference, in which he referred to himself in the third person and declared dozens of times that he was Ukraine’s president, began to move it toward common. The charade continued regardless. This, as it turned out, was the more important fact.⁴

\* \* \*

### 2022: Day One, and What Came After

On January 5, 2022, I published a short post about a wolf running loose in Kyiv’s Botanical Garden. Trackers with heat-vision goggles had arrived at five in the morning. The garden is 117 hectares. They hadn’t found anything. They said they would keep looking.

Seven weeks later, Russia invaded.

[Day 1](https://abdymok.substack.com/p/day-1) (February 24) was, I think, characteristically dry. I noted which media outlet had the best coverage on what I called “the jacket” — a term borrowed from print design, where the jacket is the thing that wraps the story. I noted a first Russian prisoner of war. I noted that Christopher Miller of Buzzfeed, who had spent eight years describing Russian-directed fighters in Donbas as “rebels” and “separatists,” was still describing them that way with what appeared to be the same expression. What the post did not contain was panic. I mention this not as a boast but as a data point: the invasion, when it came, was not a surprise to anyone who had been reading the signals.⁶

The first weeks of war I spent not catastrophizing but parsing. I watched the ISW maps and maintained what I hoped was a productive skepticism about their “bounded rationality” framework — the assumption that Putin would behave within the limits of what seemed strategically rational to an American analyst. I watched the 40-kilometer Russian convoy north of Kyiv stall and then disintegrate. I woke at 1:40 a.m. to four large explosions, stumbled around the apartment for twenty minutes, and went back to sleep. The methodology held. The city, for the moment, also held.

[Day 27, Phase 2](https://abdymok.substack.com/p/day-27-phase-2) (April 21) is where I named the framework that would structure everything that followed. Four phases: the failed attempt to decapitate Kyiv in February and March; the Russian pivot to Donbas in March and April; Phase 3, which was beginning now, which would be de-occupation, which would be the longest and most deadly part, which would last months, probably years; and Phase 4, which I described as a conflict that would never truly end. I made this prediction at Day 100 of the war. It has since been confirmed by 1,300 additional days of evidence.⁷

[Day 25, Phase 3](https://abdymok.substack.com/p/day-25-phase-3) (June 2) states it plainly: “We’re only in the beginning of Phase 3, which will last for months, maybe years. It will be the most deadly and depressing part.” I have quoted this line in several subsequent year-end assessments, not because I take pleasure in having been right, but because the prediction was made publicly and should be publicly acknowledged. It was right. It remains right.⁸

Ignore Analysts (June 18) is the methodological statement of the war period, and I think the clearest argument I have made in this archive for dismissing a certain kind of accredited expertise in favor of watching the thing directly. ISW’s December 2021 forecast — which assumed Putin would not invade because invasion was not rational — is the central exhibit. Bounded rationality assumes that your adversary shares your definition of rationality. Putin does not. He does what he says he will do. The only thing surprising about this, I wrote, was that it kept surprising people.⁹

[Day 22, Phase 3](https://abdymok.substack.com/p/day-22-phase-3) (December 28) closes the year by listing what I had taken to calling the gate scandals — Wagnergate, Chausgate, Semenchenkogate — as a single continuous thread: the President’s Office managing perception while the war managed the dead.¹⁰

\* \* \*

### 2023: Positional Warfare, and the Problem of Being Right

[This is (still) ‘ukraine@war’](https://abdymok.substack.com/p/this-is-still-ukrainewar) (January 1) is a recommitment. Conjecture and refutation. The old tools. The bloody mess I hoped to survive.¹¹

By November, General Zaluzhny would publish his famous essay describing the war as a World War I-style stalemate. I had been describing it that way since June 2022. I do not fault Zaluzhny for the timing. He was operating under constraints I was not. What I will say is that 2023 introduced me to a particular variety of frustration: the frustration of being right about things that do not improve because you are right about them.

[Day 499](https://abdymok.substack.com/p/day-499) (July 7) is simply a list. Three things I had asked for repeatedly, in various formulations, since the early days of the invasion: close the sky, authorize the Third Army Corps, send longer-range weapons. The format — numbered, uncommented — says everything I had to say about what it had been like to be asking.¹²

[Day 516](https://abdymok.substack.com/p/day-516) (July 24) names the journalists who spent eight years calling Russian-directed fighters in Donbas “rebels” and “separatists.” One had apologized by then. Most had not. The list of the unapologetic is long and includes correspondents from publications that have since received considerable praise for their Ukraine coverage. I do not find this surprising. Institutions that produce wrong analysis for eight years do not typically respond to being proven wrong by extensively reconsidering their methods. They respond by producing new analysis, in the same manner, about the next thing.¹³

[Day 673](https://abdymok.substack.com/p/pause-operationnelle) (December 28) ends the year with the observation that the gap between what Russia will accept and what Ukraine can agree to was not closing. It would not close in 2024. It has not closed since.¹⁴

\* \* \*

### 2024: The Chair is Reupholstered, Not Cleaned

On the second anniversary of the full-scale invasion, [Day 730](https://abdymok.substack.com/p/day-730) (February 23) carried a number that required sitting with for a moment: 600,000. This was the figure Arakhamia presented to parliament — the number of ghost soldiers in the AFU’s rolls, men counted as present who had deserted, gone AWOL, or existed only on paper. The mobilization system, it turned out, was not failing at the margins. It was failing at its foundation.¹⁵

Three days later, [Day 733](https://abdymok.substack.com/p/coming-to-a-theater-near-you) (February 26), Zaluzhny was dismissed and replaced by Syrsky. The same week, the New York Times published a 6,000-word investigation into the CIA’s relationship with Ukrainian intelligence. It was the kind of article that required years of access and dozens of sources. It did not mention Wagnergate. The chair, I wrote, has been reupholstered. It has not been cleaned.¹⁶

[Day 748](https://abdymok.substack.com/p/day-748) (March 13): no demobilization until the war ends. No rotation plan. No exit date. One in ten soldiers AWOL, by the most conservative estimates available. I did not editorialize. The numbers did.¹⁷

By year’s end the archive’s tone had shifted in a way I recognized but could not quite name at the time. Less acid, more compression. The compression of someone who has been counting days in a city that is both standing and not standing, depending on the hour.¹⁸

\* \* \*

### 2025: The Four Failures, Named

The year opened with the 155th Mechanized Brigade. France had trained and equipped it. Before it fired a single shot, 1,700 soldiers had gone AWOL. The brigade commander was dismissed immediately upon deployment. One of the senior officers responsible for its formation died of a heart attack. Yuriy Butusov, the most rigorous military journalist in Ukraine, was precise about what this was: not a crime of soldiers, a crime of leadership. [Day 1044](https://abdymok.substack.com/p/day-1044) (January 2) carries his full assessment.¹⁹

[Day 1049](https://abdymok.substack.com/p/day-1049) (January 7) is the post I would choose if I were permitted to choose only one from 2025. It lists four things: fortifications not built, forced conscription a systemic failure, new brigades a systemic failure, domestic production a systemic failure. Four hundred words. No commentary beyond the list. Every one of these failures had been documented in this archive for years before they were acknowledged anywhere else. None of them had been fixed.²⁰

The mortar scandal ran through the middle of the year: \$500 million in domestically produced shells that, upon being fired at the enemy, declined to explode. This was the moment at which domestic production was supposed to be reducing Ukraine’s dependence on Western supply chains. The shells did not explode. No one was held accountable in any way that mattered.²¹

[Day 1402](https://abdymok.substack.com/p/day-1402) (December 26) closes 2025 with the negotiation gap. Russia’s position, Ukraine’s counter-position, the arithmetic of the distance between them. “Check for logic,” I wrote. “There isn’t any.”²²

\* \* \*

### 2026: The Barometer Does Not Cause the Weather

The year’s most useful post is also, structurally, the simplest. [Day 1409](https://abdymok.substack.com/p/day-1409) (January 2) applies Judea Pearl’s framework for causal reasoning to Z’s New Year’s address. Z’s speech constructs a causal diagram in which presidential rhetoric produces hope, which produces peace. But the barometer does not cause the atmospheric pressure. You can move the needle on public morale without generating electricity or ending wars. What you produce, instead, is a society increasingly unable to receive accurate information about its own situation — which is, as I had occasion to note earlier in this archive, a familiar condition.²³

[Day 1454](https://abdymok.substack.com/p/day-1454) (February 16) is the parabiosis post — old blood, young blood, the research into whether you can reverse aging by mixing them. I found it a useful frame for the European diplomatic situation: Orbán as senescent cell, Medinsky at the Geneva negotiations as a séance, the entire Western response to the war as a gerontological problem. It is, I think, the funniest post in the archive about a situation that is not funny.²⁴

Day 1524 (April 28) states plainly what this archive has maintained since January 2021 but rarely put so directly: military resilience is not the same thing as liberal democracy. Ukraine’s soldiers and its society performed something extraordinary in resisting the invasion. This is true and should be said clearly. It does not follow that the political leadership presiding over that resistance exemplified the highest standards of democratic governance. Power concentrated. Media consolidated. Parliamentary dissent narrowed. Courts were, in certain cases, convenient. I have held both of these things simultaneously because both are true, and because holding only one of them at a time produces a picture of Ukraine that is flattering but not accurate.²⁵

[Day 1577](https://abdymok.substack.com/p/day-1577) (June 19) — the last post before this guide was written — holds together two things that resist being held together. Timothy Snyder has observed that Z’s decision to remain in Kyiv after the invasion was structural rather than personal courage — the act of a president who understood what his departure would mean for the country’s survival. This is true. It is also true that this same president, before the invasion, had an approval rating approaching zero, was constructing a residence in Kozyn while ignoring five months of explicit, specific warnings that Russia would invade, and had presided over the cover-up of a security failure that cost Ukraine its best opportunity to arrest the men who would later help run the war against it. Both of these things are in the record. The record is public. I did not rewrite it.²⁶

\* \* \*

### A Note on Method

This archive does not retrospectively adjust what it said. The Phase 3 prediction from June 2022 is still there, in the form it was written. The Wagnergate posts from January 2021 are still there. The posts documenting Ukraine’s Defense Minister calling the invasion risk “low” five days before it arrived are still there.

Most commentary about Ukraine — from Western analysts, Kyiv correspondents, think tanks whose institutional positions required certain forms of optimism — has been quietly revised in memory if not in archive. What people thought at the time has been substantially rewritten to match what they now believe they should have thought. I have not done this. I cannot do it. The posts are dated, public, and unchanged.

I have been wrong in public. The record shows this too. What it does not show is the systematic conversion of prior errors into subsequent authority — the professional habit, common among analysts, of treating the last wrong prediction as the foundation for the next confident one.

The wolf in the Botanical Garden was never found. The trackers eventually went home. The garden is 117 hectares, which is a great deal of ground to cover with heat-vision goggles at five in the morning, and some things, it turns out, do not want to be found.
