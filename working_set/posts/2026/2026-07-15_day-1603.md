The poster was torn down minutes after I took this picture. Someone had given Parliament Speaker Ruslan Stefanchuk horns, wedged a megaphone in a glass box between his hands, and declared Draft Civil Code No. 15150 a threat to freedom of speech and journalistic investigations — an unusual accusation to level at a civil code, the driest branch of law ever invented, the one concerned with contracts, property and who inherits your dacha. Strangeness has never been an obstacle to political advertising here. By the time I’d looked back, someone else was already peeling it off the wall.

The new Civil Code has acquired the familiar dignity of every grand legal reform: everyone agrees Ukraine needs one, and almost everyone dislikes it for a different reason. It is said to be too European, too academic, too theoretical, too liberal, too cautious, too long, too complicated and, perhaps most unforgivably, too optimistic about the amount of free time available to Ukrainian judges. Family lawyers fear it swallows the Family Code. Business lawyers complain it leaves too much uncertainty. Human-rights advocates find gaps where they expected guarantees. Technologists see yesterday’s answers to tomorrow’s questions.

And hanging over all of it is the oldest Ukrainian objection of all: before rebuilding the house, perhaps we should first make sure the roof has stopped exploding.

So the Code is controversial, and almost nobody likes Stefanchuk.

But he is the wrong villain for this particular week.

The poster made him look like the architect of the country’s troubles. He isn’t.

Horns painted on the man rewriting inheritance law is the sort of scandal a country produces when the real architecture of power is operating exactly as designed.

**That architecture has an address.**

**It is two floors above the President’s Office.**

Start with the arithmetic, because it is the least controversial part.

Z has never really had a chief of staff. The formal post has long functioned as the centre of executive power itself, with appointments, budgets, judges and political discipline flowing through a single office that exists largely outside **a constitution on pause**.

"Усі ті права, які гарантує Конституція України, для нас із вами зараз особисто як для представників нашої держави – вони на паузі". - [Z](https://www.president.gov.ua/news/shodnya-vi-mayete-vidchuvati-sho-vid-vashoyi-roboti-zalezhit-80041) (December 23, 2022)

Over time, the judiciary acquired a reputation for responding less to statute than to informal pressure, while lawyers learned there were clients best left unrepresented if they valued their future careers. Whatever one calls it — state capture, informal governance or simply survival — it is difficult to describe it as an independent rule of law.

Members of parliament elected in 2019 remained remarkably disciplined for years. **Only after the envelopes stopped arriving did they rediscover independent judgment.** New parliamentary groupings are now forming, but this looks less like a democratic awakening than a change of landlord.

Not incompetence.

Policy.

**Ukraine’s tragedy is not simply corruption. It is a state that reallocates its enemies rather than defeats them.**

That is why Mykhailo Fedorov’s predicament matters.

The Economist’s [profile](https://www.economist.com/europe/2026/07/13/ukraines-reforming-defence-minister-is-under-fire) this week is sympathetic, yet inadvertently describes the structural trap facing anyone who threatens an entrenched revenue stream without possessing an independent political base. Fedorov introduced competitive procurement and reportedly reduced artillery-shell prices by sixteen percent almost immediately.

**The saving tells us less about Fedorov than about the system that preceded him.**

He has no faction.

No institutional base.

No independent constituency.

One patron.

Fedorov himself has spoken publicly about pressure from corruption networks surrounding defence procurement. **Yet many of those associated with the old system remain among the president’s most trusted advisers. There has been no public accounting. Only continuity.**

That is the mechanism in miniature.

Failure is rarely punished.

It is redistributed.

The pattern appears elsewhere. A senior general dismisses criticism by asking whether passengers would board an aircraft flown by a shopkeeper. Contempt substitutes for argument. Senior officers delayed manpower reforms for months before finally accepting them. Delay itself becomes a political instrument.

Fedorov’s vulnerability is therefore structural, not personal. His problem is not a lack of military credentials. It is that he is attempting to reform institutions whose defenders remain politically entrenched while possessing little protection beyond Z’s favour, at precisely the moment that favour appears least capable of enforcing itself.
