February 2022 was a busy time for this Substack, at least judging by the titles of our posts. We were writing about separate realities, air drums over Kyiv, the roasting of chicken—topics that ping-ponged between the philosophical and the utterly trivial. Looking back now, I’m struck by how *precisely* we were capturing the moment: the acceleration of events, the way thought splinters under pressure, the comedy of people still discussing things that suddenly don’t matter.

[Image]

*Specialized Anti-corruption Bureau and Specialized Anti-corruption Prosecutor’s Office - Investigation of R2 (allegedly Andriy Yermak) May 11, 2026*

What this convo, dated February 16, 2022, allegedly between Yermak (R2) and his house designer, sounded like in English:

> **Designer:** Good day. We have fresh visualizations of your house. Ready to meet during the week. Please let us know when and what time would be convenient for you to meet.
>
> \[Later, during communication between R2 and the person he brought in to organize the construction process, there is an exchange of images of the design of R2’s residence.\]

What we didn’t know then, or perhaps what we were trying not to know, was that Andriy Yermak—the head of the President’s Office, the man presumably orchestrating some kind of response to the gathering storm—was holding meetings about the first-floor layout of his mansion outside the city. **Before the invasion. During. After.** Scheduling design consultations and wardrobe sketches with the same methodical attention he presumably brought to, well, everything else. The way you might discuss paint colors while the house itself is burning—or, more precisely, while it’s already burning, has burned, and will continue burning.

The moral vertigo of the moment keeps pulling me back to a specific image: Tarkovsky’s *[The Sacrifice](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=sI5oM0cJ2l4)*, a house burning at the edge of apocalypse.

In Tarkovsky’s *The Sacrifice*, a house burns as redemption—the protagonist destroys what he loves most to transcend the material world and touch something divine. The fire is voluntary, meaningful, a prayer for salvation amid apocalypse.

Yermak’s mansion burns differently. It represents wealth extracted through corruption and money-laundering, its construction continuing even as the country ignites. When it finally burns—through investigation and seizure—there is no spiritual leap, no grace. Only exposure.

Both are houses consumed at the moment of apocalypse. But one man chooses to lose everything to transcend the material. The other desperately builds material monuments while apocalypse rages, then loses them anyway, without redemption.

Tarkovsky’s protagonist learns possessions mean nothing. Yermak seems to have learned nothing. He negotiated wardrobe sketches while Burns warned of decapitation strikes. The mansion was meant to prove arrival, power, victory. Instead it became evidence of complete disconnection from reality—a bureaucrat optimizing closet space while the country mobilized to survive.
