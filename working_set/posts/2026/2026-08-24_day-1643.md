There is a particular kind of vertigo in being asked, by a machine, what you were doing thirty-five years ago today, and having the machine answer with more confidence than your own memory can muster — and being wrong about almost all of it. I did not need an AI to tell me I was working out of Kyiv in the summer of 1991. I needed it, apparently, to remind me that memory is a poor archivist and a worse editor: it keeps the smell of a place and loses the org chart.

The org chart, for the record, went like this: before Kyiv, Joe Filner and Project Development International, a firm whose name was so aggressively generic it could have doubled as a Soviet ministry, which in 1991 was often the more interesting proposition. Ted worked for PDI too — my colleague, occasional partner in bad decisions, the kind of man you sent a telegram to and trusted was already three steps ahead of you on the actual, non-notional version of the deal.

I came to Kyiv itself in 1990, on a trading firm’s business, not yet the Kyiv I would return to for good in 1998. This first stretch was more like a swallow deciding whether the nest was worth building.

Some of that year’s business took me back and forth to Moscow, changing money — the actual, physical business of holding a stack of one currency and needing, urgently, a stack of another, back when the exchange rate between the two was less a number than a rumor with a half-life measured in hours. On one of those trips I stayed at the Hotel Moskva, that Stalinist wedding cake with two mismatched facades. Legend has it the architects submitted two designs and Stalin signed both without noticing, and no one dared ask him which he meant, so they built both.

I looked up Ted, who was in town on PDI business of his own. I don’t remember what we discussed. I remember the currency changing, and I remember that everyone in Moscow that summer had the look of people watching a clock they couldn’t read.

That was the atmosphere of the whole year, really: representative democracy being invented and threatened in the same rooms, sometimes the same afternoon, and the only law that reliably held was whoever was standing closest to you with a firearm.

Not every memory from that stretch carries this much freight. There was a night in Moshchun, the village outside Kyiv where people went to swim and drink and briefly forget they were living through the unwinding of an empire, and I lost a pair of custom silver-framed glasses, made in New York, in a deep swimming hole during what I described at the time as the very hot summer of 1991.

I woke up the next morning under a tree in Kyiv with no clear account of how I’d gotten there.

For thirty-one years this was simply a good story, the kind you tell to convince younger colleagues that the past was more fun than the present. Then, in the spring of 2022, Russian armor came through Moshchun and the swimming hole became a firing position. The story did not stop being funny, exactly, but it acquired a weight it was never built to carry.

A place absorbs your trivial history and then, decades later, absorbs something unforgivable, and asks you to hold both at once.

I still have — boxed up somewhere, partially resurfaced from an old video I shot and posted years later — the situation reports I filed from the PDI office in Kyiv that August, typed on onionskin under my own letterhead: Vice President, care of Joe Filner.

The one dated August 19, 1991, the morning the plotters in Moscow announced that Gorbachev was too ill to govern, records the mood in the office as calm, “although most are in a semi-state of shock in light of this morning’s announcement.” Kyiv at large, I wrote, was “equally disturbed.”

[Image]

I noted that I’d heard no official word from local leaders on what had actually transpired, and that our office, cultivating a closer relationship with the consulate, had extended an invitation for consular staff to drop by that evening — foreign correspondents having, as I put it, “graciously accepted” one of their own the day before.

Five days later, on August 24, 1991, Ukraine declared its independence — a sovereign state coughed out of a dying empire over the course of an afternoon, greeted with the kind of speeches that assume history is listening and taking careful notes in the nation’s favor.

I was still in that office, still filing reports, watching the same room that had been rattled by a coup plotter’s announcement adjust, in less than a week, to the idea that it now belonged to a country that hadn’t existed the week before.

I have spent the twelve years since 2014 cataloguing, in this space, what that declaration actually grew into: a sham democracy wearing the coat of a real one, a feudal patronage system that periodically remembers it is supposed function as a republic.

I don’t take that back today. But thirty-five is an age at which a person — and maybe a country — stops being explained by its birth and starts being explained by what it has refused to die of.

Ukraine has lost land, lost people, lost the glasses in the swimming hole and eventually the swimming hole itself. It has not lost the afternoon in 1991 when a room full of people decided, against most of the available evidence, that a country was worth declaring into being anyway — and it has not, so far, lost the harder and more expensive habit of insisting, year after year, that the declaration wasn’t a mistake.

That’s the part I keep circling back to, thirty-five years on, still unsentimental about nearly everything else: not the birth, which was mostly luck and imperial exhaustion, but the refusal — repeated, costly, and ongoing — to treat the birth as one.
