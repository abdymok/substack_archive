In 1997 it was audits. Not indictments—audits. Tax inspectors, fire marshals, and sanitary inspectors would arrive at the same NGOs within days of one another, citing different regulations while serving the same political purpose. Lukashenko didn’t need to outlaw independent civil society. He only had to make compliance impossible and let bureaucracy accomplish what repression would have announced more loudly. We wrote about this then.¹

**The same administrative instinct is increasingly visible in Kyiv.**

Lukashenko’s real innovation wasn’t ideological. Belarus in the 1990s had very little ideology worth exporting. It was administrative. The important decisions quietly migrated out of institutions and into an informal circle around the president. Parliament continued to meet. Courts continued to function. Ministries continued to exist. Increasingly, however, they existed to formalize decisions already made elsewhere.

Bankova now operates in much the same way. The archive has documented the gradual transfer of authority into Z’s inner circle² and the parallel evolution of the security services into instruments of that circle rather than independent institutions.³ Parliament still votes. It mostly ratifies.

The legal mechanism differs, but the logic is familiar. Lukashenko used constitutional referendums to normalize permanent presidential authority. Ukraine doesn’t require referendums. War performs the same function. “Wartime necessity” has gradually replaced anti-corruption reform as the universal justification for concentrating power, while courts increasingly endorse those decisions rather than independently review them.⁴⁵

The institutions remain standing, but much of their load-bearing function has been removed. Independent oversight bodies need not be abolished if they can be weakened, restaffed, or deprived of meaningful authority. That is precisely what has happened to Ukraine’s anti-corruption architecture through the campaign against its agencies’ independence.⁶

The same migration is visible in procurement. Decisions that should pass through transparent institutions increasingly emerge from private networks instead. Multi-billion-dollar defense contracts are discussed in apartments rather than boardrooms. The roles now under investigation involving figures such as Mindich and Yermak illustrate how political authority migrates into relationships that exist largely outside formal accountability.⁷

The pattern extends to the press. Modern authoritarian systems rarely require overt censorship. Prior restraint is quieter and far more effective: legal pressure, confidential court orders, and informal warnings ensure that stories disappear before publication, leaving no visible act of censorship to protest. The tactics now facing investigative journalists in Kyiv bear a striking resemblance to those used against independent reporting in Minsk decades earlier.⁸

Appearances matter. So do institutions. They are not always telling the same story.

Meanwhile, in Washington, Trump smiles beside Z. Sanctions advance. Republicans again speak openly about helping Ukraine win. Those developments matter. They may prove consequential.

They are also largely disconnected from the institutional trajectory inside Ukraine.

NABU. SAPO. Mindich. Yermak. Vova.

Those belong to a different conversation altogether.

One country. Two narratives. They increasingly pass one another without making contact.

Washington has understandable reasons for emphasizing battlefield success over constitutional architecture. Wars are measured in territory gained, sanctions imposed, weapons delivered, alliances reaffirmed. Institutions are slower. Administrative decay rarely photographs well.

That is what Belarus demonstrated three decades ago. Democratic backsliding seldom begins with tanks outside parliament. It begins when institutions continue to function in public while decisions quietly migrate elsewhere.

None of this requires Ukraine to imitate Belarus. Post-Soviet presidential systems drift toward the same equilibrium when institutions cease restraining power. Lukashenko justified it through constitutional engineering. Z justifies it through wartime necessity.

Keeping up appearances is easy. Keeping institutions independent is harder.
