[George Bernard Shaw](https://www.epedagogia.com.br/materialbibliotecaonine/2874Common-Sense-About-the-War.pdf), writing in November 1914 while the mud at Mons was still fresh, noticed something that has not stopped being true. Both nations, he observed, had been duped — but not quite unwillingly duped — by their militarists into fighting a war that served neither of them. He was considered impolite for saying so. He said so anyway. This is the first thing to understand about Shaw: he treated impoliteness as a civic duty, and performed it with considerable pleasure.

The second thing to understand is that he was right.

The photograph above this piece was taken on Saturday, directly opposite to a café once called Trump Pizza Station. The café has since changed its name, then closed entirely. The building in the photograph has not been renamed. It simply stands, or most of it does, in the way that things stand when they have been partially disaggregated by a Shahed drone — Iranian, as it happens, now that Iran is on the other end of a different war’s paperwork. The street is otherwise unremarkable. This is the thirteenth year of the war, and remarkability has been rationed.

At the [Imperial War Museum](https://www.iwm.org.uk/) in London, the exhibits are arranged chronologically. The arguments are not. Walk through the galleries and you will find that each war arrives with fresh justifications and familiar shapes — necessary, limited, preventive, decisive. [David Kaye, writing in the Times this week](https://www.nytimes.com/2026/03/22/opinion/trump-iran-war-memes.html), notices that the purposes of the Iran war multiply precisely where clarity is absent. The war, like a well-trained actor, adjusts its role to the audience. This too is a tradition with deep institutional support.

[Bertrand Russell](https://users.drew.edu/~jlenz/br-ethics-of-war.html) had a category for what follows: the war of prestige. It begins in abstraction — credibility, resolve, the necessity of being seen to act — and ends in arithmetic. The dead. The displaced. The quietly surprised. Nations, he wrote, rarely ask what good a war will actually produce. They ask only whether it can be made to look proper. The paperwork is not a reason for war but a decoration. Nations go to war and then, in the hours after, discover their reasons, the way a man who has already jumped from a window discovers, on the way down, that he was pushed.

Shaw would have found this insufficiently comic. He preferred the version in which everyone knew perfectly well what they were doing and chose, collectively, to pretend otherwise. His militarists — English and German alike — had been drinking to The Day for a generation. When The Day arrived, they expressed shock. Shaw found this shock rather difficult to take seriously. His remedy was recrimination, which he considered not only appropriate but practical: you cannot negotiate what you will not name.

[Valeriy Zaluzhny](https://english.nv.ua/opinion/zaluzhnyi-why-wars-repeat-and-global-leaders-fail-to-stop-them-50593006.html), writing over the weekend with the patience of a man who has used up his patience, adds the operational dimension. Wars repeat, he argues, not from passion or miscalculation, but from the systematic failure to close one unresolved conflict before opening the next. He does not use Shaw’s language, but he is describing Shaw’s mechanism. The war of prestige generates its own continuation. Each inconclusive end becomes the beginning of something that has not yet been named, and will not be named until it is too late to matter.

Outside, Kyiv is managing. This is what Kyiv does — it manages. Russia sent more than 60,000 drones at Ukrainian civilians last year, nearly five times the number of the year before, which was itself a record. The negotiations [continue](https://x.com/SEPeaceMissions/status/2035794441861259293) in Miami. The purposes multiply.

What Shaw asked for in 1914 — what Russell asked for, what Zaluzhny is asking for now, with considerably less patience for irony — is modest enough to be embarrassing: that we decide what a war is for before it begins, rather than after it ends. That the purposes survive contact with reality. That the language retain some memory of what killing is.

The museum in London is prepared for our failure to manage this. It has been quietly expanding its collection for a hundred and twelve years.

The building opposite Trump Pizza Station is also, in its way, prepared. It has stopped waiting to be repaired.
