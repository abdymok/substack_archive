The weather has grown warm, and winter — like an unpleasant relative who overstayed his welcome — has been shown to the door. People are sitting outside again. Children are eating ice cream without guilt. It is the season when a man naturally forgets that he was ever cold.

And it is precisely this season that T.J. of *[The New York Times](http://nyt.com)* [](http://nyt.com) chose to publish his great investigation into Russia’s use of cold as a weapon[1](#footnote-1).

The article ran to some four thousand five hundred words. I counted. Or rather, I felt them — the way you feel a long journey on a bad road. In all those words, there was no room — not a single comfortable paragraph, not even a footnote’s worth of space — for the observation that perhaps the pipes had burst not because of geopolitical cunning, but because of something more ancient and universal: the theft of the money meant to insulate them, and the profound human capacity to avoid thinking about 2026 when it was 2022.

But then, T.J. is an old acquaintance of ours. We knew him in 2014, when he was a younger man — or at least a man with younger ambitions — and he joined forces with colleagues (Noah, Sabrina and Andrew come to mind) to tell the world a very wacko, flawed story about eastern Ukraine. The rebels there, we were given to understand, had risen from the earth like mushrooms after rain, spontaneously, organically, full of local grievance and native passion. It was a beautiful story. The kind of story that a very talented person tells when he has already decided, in some quiet room before he sat down to write, what the story will be.

There is a certain kind of journalist — and I say this with the respect one reserves for a skilled craftsman — who approaches the truth the way a man approaches a large dog he does not own: carefully, from a specific angle, making no sudden movements, and always keeping the exit in view.

On the Ukraine jacket, the big news is Z sucking up to Bibi, or trying to suck up, or vice versa.

Spring has arrived, and with it comes love. Or at least, the diplomatic approximation of love — which is to say, two men who have not spoken in two years discovering, simultaneously, that each possesses exactly what the other needs.

Z, returning from a tour of the Middle East with the satisfied air of a man who has just remembered he has friends, sat down with [Barak Ravid of Axios](https://archive.ph/pFNip#selection-659.0-659.182) — a journalist who has made something of a specialty of sitting down with Z — and spoke warmly of Bibi. He has not spoken to the man himself in two years, you understand. But he spoke warmly *about* him, which in diplomacy is considered a promising first step, like leaving flowers outside a door you are not yet certain will open.

“We have what he needs,” Z said, with the quiet confidence of a man at a bazaar who knows the other man’s pockets. “And he has what we need.” One searches, in this formulation, for any difference from an ordinary business transaction, and finds mostly that the goods in question are weapons, and the currency is the mutual company of powerful friends — which has always been the true currency of the Middle East.

Bibi, for his part, has spent these two years “balancing.” This is the word Z used, generously, for what a less diplomatic observer might describe as a man standing on a fence so long that he has begun to regard the fence as his natural habitat. Russia is helping Iran — this Z knows, has shared the intelligence, has presented the satellite images. And Bibi balances. One almost admires the athleticism of it.

What makes the courtship additionally fragrant is that Israel has not been merely a passive admirer from afar. It has, in the generous tradition of a country that takes hospitality seriously, been housing a remarkable collection of Z’s former associates. There is Tymur Mindich, Z’s old friend, a man of considerable ingenuity whose involvement in the affair known as Operation Midas has made him, shall we say, temporarily inconvenient to have around Kyiv. There is Kolomoisky and Boholiubov. There is Vadym Rabinovich. There are, by all accounts, dozens of others — a small colony of disgraced Ukrainian politicians, each one suspected of crimes that would require a separate and lengthy article to enumerate, most of them enjoying the Mediterranean climate and the certain comfort of knowing that extradition is a concept Israel has always approached with the same philosophical detachment that Bibi brings to fence-sitting. The residents of this colony are not, it should be said, sedentary by nature. They commute — between Tel Aviv, Monaco, and Miami, principally — with the easy frequency of men who have solved the problem of borders that so vexes ordinary people. One begins to understand that the relationship between the two countries, even during these two years of official silence, has never really been cold. It has simply been conducted at a different level.

The Passover season lends the whole affair a certain poignancy. Z, who did not visit Israel on his Middle Eastern tour — Israel, notably, did not ask — nonetheless wished all Jews worldwide “peace,” calling it the greatest deficit in the world, ahead of energy. This is a generous and also carefully sequenced hierarchy. A man who supplies air defense systems understands that the wish for peace and the trade in the instruments of war are not contradictions. They are, in fact, the same industry, viewed from different ends of the transaction. And a man whose friends are warmly received in a country he is publicly courting understands that diplomacy, like most human arrangements, operates on several floors simultaneously, not all of them visible from the street.

What is touching, in the manner of a story that knows it is being watched, is the courtship ritual itself. Ukraine has sent military experts to Jordan, Saudi Arabia, the UAE — countries that did ask — to demonstrate the elegant Ukrainian system of combining defensive layers so that a sky full of Iranian drones becomes, eventually, a quieter sky. Israel observed this from a distance, as a reserved suitor observes a dance. It has not yet asked to be taught the steps.

But Z is patient. He is a man who has learned, over several years of war, the difference between those who call and those who need to be called. He waits. The deficit in air defense is real. The deficit in peace is larger. And somewhere in Jerusalem, a very experienced balancer is looking at his fence and quietly reconsidering his footing — while, in a nearby café, several of Kyiv’s most wanted are ordering their second coffee of the morning, unhurried, unextradited, and perfectly at ease.
