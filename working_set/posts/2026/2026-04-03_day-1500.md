As of this writing, around 0830, there’s a lot flying around. Not on the map are airborne Russian strategic bombers with cruise missiles, 2× Tu-160 (from Ukrainka airbase) 3× Tu-95MS (2 from Olenya, 1 from Engels-2).

Paid-up subscribers to this Substack will no doubt recall the scene in Kyiv on Orthodox Easter in 2022 and the wild wheels-up party at [Tootsie Club](https://uk.tootsie.kiev.ua/) after Randy George and our hungover American friends departed the capital by train.

The intro to that post went something like this:

> *There is no consecution* (последовательность) *in madness. We exit Ward No. 6 and make strides towards the office belonging to the landlord’s sister — when out of nowhere, the smell of a kitchen punches through the corridor. We spin around and plow directly towards it, bedpan in hand. Someone asks what we’re holding. A bedpan, we say. Don’t be absurd, they say. That’s a corridor to Transdniester.*

------------------------------------------------------------------------

That was Day 59[1](#footnote-1).

The Americans had landed. Randy, Tony and Lloyd, disembarking in a city still blinking at itself in the mirror — monument-polished, swept, barricaded, braced. Z held his press conference from an undisclosed metro station, apologizing for the sound of wagons.

It was, briefly, a good day. If memory serves, I took [Triple C](https://www.magnumphotos.com/photographer/chien-chi-chang/) on a hike.

There is a particular American habit, visible in moments of institutional stress, of confusing motion with control. A man removes another man from his post and calls it strategy. The act itself becomes the argument. And so Pete Hegseth has decided to sack General Randy A. George, and in doing so has offered us not merely a personnel change, but a small window into how this war — the one we discussed yesterday, the one made of drones and arithmetic — is being misunderstood at the highest levels.[2](#footnote-2).

[Image]

*The Fast Clock and the Cheap Kill. How Ukraine solved the drone interceptor problem while everyone else held a meeting about it. (p. 8, c/o adymok , April 2, 2026)*

General George’s offense, if it can be called that, was not failure. It was attention. He had been looking, with increasing seriousness, at Ukraine.

Not Ukraine as metaphor, or talking point, or fundraising device — but Ukraine as laboratory. A place where the cost curve of war has broken in half. Where a quadcopter assembled in a garage can hunt a tank. Where the side that learns faster survives longer. He understood, perhaps too clearly, that the old grammar of force — armor, mass, time — is dissolving into something else: speed, iteration, and volume.

This is not a comfortable realization for a large institution. It is even less comfortable for men for whom a war, to be real, must be describable to a taxi driver.

Yesterday’s post made a simple claim: that the future of drone warfare is not brilliance, but scale. Not the exquisite system, but the abundant one. The \$10,000 interceptor, not the \$400,000 missile. We did not arrive at this conclusion through theory. We arrived there through necessity, which is the most persuasive teacher available to man.

George was listening to that teacher.

[Video: https://youtu.be/eXSZH-pNK\_g]

He spoke, repeatedly, about a battlefield that had become transparent. About units that could no longer hide. About the need to distribute drones down to the smallest formations, to accept that every soldier would operate within a mesh of sensors and signals. He was, in other words, attempting to translate a Ukrainian reality into an American doctrine.

This is not the same thing as winning a war. It is, however, the beginning of not losing the next one.

Hegseth’s decision suggests a different instinct. Not adaptation, but reassertion. Not learning from the periphery, but restoring the center. It is the belief — deeply American, deeply persistent — that complexity can be managed by replacing the man in charge of it.

But the problem George was describing does not belong to him. It does not disappear with his departure. The drone does not become less cheap because a general has been dismissed. The kill chain does not slow down out of respect for institutional hierarchy.

If anything, the timing is instructive. At the very moment when private capital — the Witkoffs, the Luckeys, the Princes, the Schmidts — is reorganizing itself around the economics of drone warfare, the man most responsible for executing war is demonstrating his discomfort with the same lesson.

There is, in this, a kind of symmetry.

Yesterday we wrote about men in warm climates discovering that war can be rendered as a balance sheet. Today we observe a colder reality: that institutions, when confronted with the implications of that balance sheet, may choose denial over adaptation.

On the beaches of Boca Raton, we used to say that a man who cannot change his environment will change the subject. In Washington, the method is more direct. You change the general.

The environment remains.

Have a great weekend!
