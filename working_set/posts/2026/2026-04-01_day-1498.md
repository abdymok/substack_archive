There is a particular kind of distance that cannot be measured in miles. Ukraine, in the past month, destroyed [37,804 targets](https://t.me/usf_army/1720) using unmanned systems — including, yesterday, 203 enemy personnel, 61 drone launch points, and 324 hostile drones, dispatched before lunch. The United States, in the same period, issued [a formal request](https://www.shephardmedia.com/news/air-warfare/us-air-force-automated-low-cost-cuas-expeditionary-solutions/#:~:text=small%20drone%20attacks.-,US%20Air%20Force%20is%20eyeing%20cost%2Deffective%20automated%20counter%2Ddrone,fixed%20and%20semi%2Dfixed%20sites.) asking contractors to please submit, by April 7th, any thoughts they might have about automated drone defense. The gap between these two sentences is not geographical. It is civilizational.

The list of requirements reads, with only mild exaggeration, like a description of what Ukraine has been cobbling together since 2022: acoustic sensors that hear before you see, cameras that see before you think, radios that listen to what should not be there, and software that ties it all together quickly enough that a man can still finish his cigarette before the engagement is over. Two operators. Seconds, not minutes. Cheap enough to buy in quantity.

“Current solutions,” the notice observes, in the tone of a man discovering that water is wet, “are often expensive, require significant operator training, or lack the automation needed to counter multiple simultaneous threats.”

Iran has launched more than two thousand drones since late February — at an estimated cost to Tehran of somewhere between \$40 and \$100 million. The United States, intercepting a fraction of them with missiles costing between \$2 and \$4 million each, has likely spent more doing so than the \$820 million it has set aside this year to think about the problem. America says it already has the best drones in the world. One imagines Hegseth says this with a straight face.

Men have been leaving certain countries in a hurry and arriving in others at a leisurely pace. They travel lightly, but not without consequence. Their relationship to Ukrainian sovereignty resembles that of a landlord to a building he has insured and then, by coincidence, set on fire. They move between Tel Aviv and Monaco and Florida as men of means do — unhurried, untroubled, and, for the moment, uncollected by the law. Before leaving, one of them appears — on surveillance tapes, in the company of men with names like characters from a provincial comedy — to have taken an interest in a drone company called Fire Point.

Fire Point did not exist before the invasion. Its founder’s previous screen credit was a romantic comedy. By 2025 it had become the largest recipient of Ukraine’s drone procurement budget — about a billion dollars — without ever undergoing the price negotiations required by law, an audit found. The formal owner, known on NABU tapes by a nickname suggesting technical competence, had previously lived in Russia. The man whose name keeps appearing says he only wanted to buy shares and was refused. This may even be true. In this ecosystem, formal ownership is the least interesting detail. What matters is that Fire Point occupied space — space where faster, cheaper, less entangled companies might have grown. Each inflated invoice was not just money lost but something not built: a sensor not deployed, a network left theoretical, a pilot training on yesterday’s machine[1](#footnote-1).

Corruption, in this sense, is not merely theft. It is delay. And delay, eventually, presents its bill.

Between March 9 and 15, small groups of drones — twelve, fifteen at a time — flew over Barksdale Air Force Base in Louisiana. They stayed for hours. They varied their routes. They appeared to be testing responses, like a man gently pressing on a door to see if it is locked. Barksdale houses the B-52 fleet and the headquarters of Air Force Global Strike Command. The flight line was shut down. Analysts concluded, with professional calm, that the incursions would [likely continue](https://abcnews.com/International/multiple-waves-unauthorized-drones-spotted-strategic-us-air/story?id=131245527).

The good news, it is said, is that no military learns faster than the American one. There is even [a center dedicated to lessons learned](https://www.army.mil/call). One notes, without insisting on the point, that there are people who have already learned these lessons. They have offered to share them.

What America has, more precisely, are very expensive drones and very confident contracts. Donald Trump Jr. holds stakes in Unusual Machines and Anduril. Eric Trump promotes an Israeli drone maker to his followers. A company counting both brothers as notable investors has announced a merger with a golf course operator, the combined entity promising dominance through strategic defense partnerships — the courses, a press release notes, may serve as proving grounds. Anduril’s loitering drones, when exposed to real electronic warfare in Ukraine, failed and were abandoned. The Army then awarded Anduril a contract worth up to twenty billion dollars. The ministry writes its conclusions carefully and files them.

Into this comes the market. Erik Prince holds a position in Swarmer Inc. — SWMR closed Monday at \$47.20, up nine percent, in a fifty-two week range running from \$11 to \$65, which is less a stock chart than a weather system. No earnings, no dividend, no PE ratio: pure expectation, trading on the idea that swarms are the future and that someone, somewhere, will pay for protection. A safe investment.

Ukraine updates its drone software every two weeks. The U.S. military's drone doctrine revision cycle runs fifteen to twenty months — and that is before the contract is awarded, the protest is filed, the oversight hearing is scheduled, and the press release is written. Between those two clocks — the fast one and the slow one — there is a gap.

In that gap sit many things: a base in Louisiana, a contract in Washington, a workshop in Dnipro, a man on a golf course in Boca Raton with time enough for a second coffee.

The deadline for responses is April 7th. There is, officially, urgency. In practice, there is still time.
