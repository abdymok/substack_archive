There is a particular quality of ambition that only flourishes in warm climates. On the beaches of Boca Raton we used to say that a man who has everything will eventually want the thing he cannot name. At Mar-a-Lago, apparently, that thing is Ukrainian drone technology.

Alex and Zach Witkoff — sons of Steve, the special envoy, the man who negotiates with the world on behalf of his golf partner — have discovered what every war eventually produces besides casualties: an investment thesis. They call their approach “Peace Through Profit,” which is the kind of phrase that sounds better the less carefully you examine it.

The thesis is not foolish. In fact it is rather elegant, in the way that a well-designed weapon is elegant. The problem they have identified is real: expensive missiles chasing cheap drones is a losing arithmetic. A Sidewinder costs four hundred thousand dollars. The drone it intercepts costs eight hundred. Somewhere in that gap lives either catastrophe or opportunity, depending on which side of the transaction you occupy.

> *Their father negotiates peace. They invest in the infrastructure of the next war. This is not a contradiction. This is, in a certain light, a complete family portrait.*

They are not alone. This is, it turns out, also a map of people — and the people are worth naming.

Palmer Luckey, who sold one company to Facebook and built another to arm the Pentagon, is here. Donald Trump Jr. is here, attached to a drone manufacturer called Unusual Machines with the enthusiasm of a man who has discovered a new hobby. Erik Prince — who has always been here, in every room where private violence meets public money — is here, this time with something called Swarmer. Eric Schmidt, who left Google to become the invisible architect of American military technology, is present in two forms simultaneously: Istari and Rebellion Defense, the brain without the body. Eric Trump has attached himself to the Israeli drone sector, which requires less explanation than it receives. Elon Musk circles the entire enterprise from a greater altitude — SpaceX, xAI, Tesla — a one-man stack that could, if the mood struck him, collapse the cost of the entire kill chain before breakfast.

And then there is Selçuk Bayraktar, the Turkish engineer who married the president’s daughter and built the Bayraktar TB2, the drone that made the world pay attention. He is the only one in this room who has actually changed a war. He stands slightly apart, as people who have done the real thing tend to do.

What Ukraine learned from all of this is simple enough: the future of drone defense is not sophistication. It is inventory. It is the kill loop running in seconds. It is the \$10,000 interceptor that can be manufactured in the same week it is designed, tested in the field, and redesigned by Tuesday.

Whether the Witkoff sons — or any of the men named above — have learned this lesson, or merely learned to describe it fluently at dinner parties in Palm Beach, remains a private matter. Their portfolios are not public. Their results are not public. Only the philosophy is public, which is itself a kind of investment strategy.

On the beaches of Boca Raton we had a saying about men who stood at the border between two worlds and called it a position. We admired them, a little. We also kept count of which side they ran to when the shooting started.
