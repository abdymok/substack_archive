In 1997, during the full collapse of Belarusian institutions, I made a simple argument in a newspaper interview: *principles do not depend on circumstances***[1](#footnote-1).**

Duties bind absolutely. Political chaos does not suspend truth-telling. Institutional weakness does not relieve moral obligation.

If corruption exists, you name it.

I no longer fully believe that argument in its pure deontological form. Over time, I moved toward utilitarianism. Consequences matter. Outcomes matter. Preventing suffering matters.

But here is what is striking about corruption during wartime: both frameworks lead to the same conclusion.

The deontological position says truth-telling is a duty.

The utilitarian position says exposing corruption prevents suffering.

In wartime procurement scandals, these are not competing moral systems. They converge.

Expose corruption. Force accountability. Prevent repetition.

Yet there is a recurring type of public figure who cannot tolerate this convergence.

They are often articulate critics of dysfunction. They can describe institutional rot with remarkable clarity: the Soviet management habits, the procurement failures, the vanity projects, the bureaucratic chaos, the systems that fail soldiers in predictable ways.

They understand exactly how corruption works.

But when specific evidence emerges—recordings, financial trails, procurement irregularities, insider favoritism—their certainty evaporates.

Suddenly they become proceduralists.

Let the courts decide.

Let investigators investigate.

We must wait for formal findings.

We should avoid premature judgment.

This would be defensible if these same people believed institutions functioned well.

They do not.

They openly acknowledge that courts are compromised, prosecutors are politically constrained, and powerful offices routinely make scandals disappear.

They know this.

And yet they retreat into procedural language precisely when procedural mechanisms are least trustworthy.

Why?

Because procedural deference offers something extremely valuable: moral distance.

It allows someone to preserve the appearance of principle while avoiding the risks that come with speaking plainly.

They can continue condemning corruption in theory while refusing to identify it in practice.

They can maintain reputations for seriousness without confronting power directly.

This is not neutrality.

It is moral evasion.

And the consequences are not abstract.

When wartime procurement fails, evacuation systems fail.

Medical supplies arrive late.

Equipment shortages compound.

Soldiers wait longer for help that should already exist.

Some die who did not need to die.

Meanwhile others profit from chaos.

This pattern survives because enough respectable people insist on waiting for institutions they know are unreliable.

And every time accountability fails, the system learns the same lesson:

wait long enough, and public outrage fades.

In actual institutional collapse, insisting on principles is difficult but necessary.

In partially functioning democracies, the temptation is different: outsourcing conscience to institutions that exist just enough to provide cover.

That may be the more sophisticated moral failure.

Not abandoning principle outright.

Not rejecting consequences outright.

But selectively invoking whichever framework demands less courage in the moment.

And that is how corruption survives war—protected not only by thieves, but by people who know better.
