I sit in Kyiv on May 7th reading and listening to pronouncements and announcements with the strange sensation of watching exhausted incompetent politicians argue over a future none of them can control.

This was not the future anyone designed.

It lacks grandeur. It lacks even strategic elegance.

It is simply what happens when the old arithmetic collapses and nobody has learned the new one yet.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

------------------------------------------------------------------------

On Wednesday, May 6th, Maria Zakharova appeared on Telegram—because of course she did, because this is how empires communicate now, through the apps of scam empires—to warn the diplomatic corps that they should evacuate Kyiv promptly. Not eventually. Promptly. She used the word “inevitability” in connection with a retaliatory strike. This is the language of physics, not politics. As if a bomb were a law of thermodynamics.

The irony, of course, is that the American embassy is already operating on fumes. Julie Davis, the acting ambassador, resigned in late April after less than a year in the job. The State Department denied that she had grown frustrated with Trump over his lack of support for Ukraine, which is exactly what you say when someone has grown frustrated with Trump over his lack of support for Ukraine. By June, she will be gone. What remains is a skeleton crew—a couple of Marines, perhaps an ISA team if we’re generous with our accounting. The Americans, it seems, had already begun their evacuation before Zakharova’s warning made it official.

The reasoning, you understand, was almost elegant in its circularity. Ukraine’s president, speaking in Armenia on Monday, had made what the Russian Foreign Ministry termed “aggressive and threatening statements” about disrupting Russia’s Victory Day parade. In response, Russia was announcing in advance that it would bomb the city where I live. This is not a paradox, exactly. It is bureaucracy achieving a kind of purity—the perfect expression of how modern states make threats. They announce them. They give you time to leave. They preserve, at the level of form, the notion that this is regrettable, that you have options, that you could have chosen differently.

Several EU countries were present at Z’s remarks in Armenia, Zakharova noted. None of them reprimanded him. You can hear her thinking: this is what happens when the grown-ups stop enforcing the rules. The children run wild. One child threatens a parade. Another child, having announced the rules, now announces the punishment in advance, like a schoolmaster who has lost all authority and retained only the capacity to document his own impotence.

But there is something genuinely revealing in this moment, something that the earlier pieces—the drone economics, the Witkoff boys calculating profit margins on the infrastructure of the next war—were circling around without quite naming it. We are watching the moment when military force stops being a tool and becomes a language game. Russia cannot actually prevent Ukraine from doing anything. Russia can only announce that it will respond. And then it responds, or it doesn’t, and either way the script continues.

What makes this particularly interesting is that the American president did not see this coming. Not because it was invisible—Ukraine has been teaching this lesson for years now, in real time, with actual casualties. But because he was not interested in learning it. One of his advisors noted, with the kind of careful understatement that suggests deep frustration, that Trump “stubbornly refused” to pay attention to what was happening in Ukraine. The nature of modern warfare was changing on the battlefield in front of him, and he was looking somewhere else.

And now, even as Russia announces the inevitability of strikes on Kyiv, the American attention is still elsewhere. Rustem Umerov, Ukraine’s National Security and Defense Council secretary, is scheduled to meet Steve Witkoff—Trump’s special envoy, whose sons are busy calculating investment opportunities in drone technology—in Miami sometime this week. The meeting, the first high-level contact between Kyiv and Washington in weeks, comes as negotiations have stalled. No one will say what they’ll discuss. Ceasefire proposals? Peace terms? Military assistance? The vagueness is itself the message. Even the meeting itself feels provisional, uncertain, like something that might or might not happen depending on what the president decided to have for breakfast.

Meanwhile, Trump held a 90-minute phone call with Putin on April 29th, which he described as “very good.” One does not need to imagine what was discussed.

The Iranians began deploying Shahed drones—built by themselves, launched by the Russians—against Ukraine in 2022. Years before that, Nagorno-Karabakh had already made clear that drones were no longer supplementary to modern warfare but central to it. The lesson was available. It was public. It required only attention.

The American military still has not come to grips with it. When confronted with the reality, they discovered something alarming: cheap drones cost millions of dollars to shoot down. A ten-thousand-dollar aircraft, and you’re spending millions in missiles to neutralize it. A smaller nation can simply bleed a larger one dry, very easily, very cheaply.

So when Trump stood up in the White House and announced that the Strait of Hormuz wasn’t his problem—the Europeans could figure it out—he was making a statement based on a world that no longer existed. The Iranians, with their cheap drones, had already figured out how to hold the Americans hostage in that same strait. You cannot simply walk away from a problem when the problem has drones.

Z’s comment about the drones was almost offhand. The Russians, he noted, cannot afford military equipment. They fear drones buzzing over Red Square. This is telling, he said—and it is. Because what it tells us is that the expensive architecture of deterrence, the missiles and the doctrine and the carefully maintained threat, has been made obsolete by a ten-thousand-dollar aircraft that you can manufacture in a week. And the president of the United States spent months failing to notice.

So now we have this perfect inversion: Russia announces it will bomb Kyiv to prevent Ukraine from disrupting a parade. Ukraine notes that Russia cannot even protect its own parade.

The announcement stands. The threat is real. The drones are cheaper than the missiles. And all of us, it seems, are waiting to discover which laws of physics still apply.
