There is a famous problem in the science of causality. A rooster crows every morning just before sunrise. Day after day the correlation is perfect — more reliable, frankly, than most things in this world.

And yet we do not say the rooster causes the sun to rise.

We understand, somehow, that the rooster is merely listening to the light gathering on the horizon. He is downstream of events he did not author.

Judea Pearl, who has spent much of his life teaching machines how to reason about cause and effect, says the real question is not what correlates with what. The real question is: **what listens to what[1](#footnote-1)**.

It sounds simple. It is not simple. Most of the world’s institutions — governments, militaries, morning television — are organized around the confusion between these two things.

Which brings us, naturally, to Pete Hegseth.

Here is a man who observed, over many years, a reliable correlation: appearing on Fox News in a state of theatrical agitation produced, reliably, the approval of one particular viewer in Florida.

The rooster crowed.
The sun rose.
The rooster concluded he was in command of the heavens.

Now this rooster commands the heavens.

What has gone wrong with Team USA’s adventure in Iran is precisely a failure of causal reasoning at the highest level. There is a difference, Pearl would remind us, between observing that something happens and understanding what will happen if you intervene.

The second is harder.

It requires a diagram — arrows pointing from causes to effects — and the humility to ask: if I do this thing, what will the world look like afterward? Not in my imagination. In the world.

The performance at the Pentagon podium — “toast,” “punching them while they’re down,” the social-media reel scored with music from Gladiator — this is not strategic communication.

It is a man who learned that a certain kind of noise produces a certain kind of applause, and who has not yet discovered that the variables have changed.

He is running an old experiment in a new laboratory.

The barometer has been moved indoors, but he is still predicting rain.

Hegseth’s tattoos say *Deus vult* — God wills it[2](#footnote-2).

This is, in its way, the oldest and most confident causal claim in human history: the outcome is predetermined, the arrow points only forward, the diagram needs no revision.

The crusaders believed this too.

The diagram did not go especially well for them either — though the end took a long time, which is the other problem with confident causal diagrams.

Pearl also tells us that babies in their cribs are not pacified until they understand **why** the toy makes noise. They shake it, drop it, throw it. They are running experiments.

Human beings, he argues, are born with a craving for causal understanding. It is what distinguishes us from other animals, which are driven more simply by reward.

It is a charming theory.

Looking at Washington this week, one wonders about the sample size.

### The Ukraine Jacket

The temptation to confuse correlation with causation is not confined to Washington.

Z has spent years discovering a reliable correlation: blunt public appeals and chest thumping are often followed, sooner or later, by weapons shipments and financial aid.

It is an understandable inference.

Last week, addressing the slow movement of a European support package reportedly worth around EUR 90 billion, Z warned that if a certain obstruction in Europe continued — widely understood to mean Hungarian Prime Minister Viktor Orbán — Ukrainian soldiers might be given the man’s address and encouraged to “call him and speak in their own language.[3](#footnote-3)”

It was meant as pressure.

But here again the arrows matter.

European aid is not produced by rhetoric alone. It emerges from a system of parliaments, coalitions, vetoes, and domestic politics — a machine considerably more complicated than a television audience.

When the rooster crows at the sun, the sun does not feel threatened.

Diplomacy is a slightly more delicate causal system.

Sometimes the rooster crows and the dawn arrives.

And sometimes the rooster discovers he was only ever listening.
