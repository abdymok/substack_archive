Yesterday, at Pechersky Market, I paid 300 hryvnia — about seven dollars — for a haircut.

The woman who cut my hair did not hurry. She was middle-aged, precise, and entirely indifferent to the idea that anything in the world might be urgent. Around us, the market moved in its usual way: vegetables arranged with quiet optimism, meat displayed with a kind of resigned honesty, people buying what they could and not what they wanted. War, like the weather, had been incorporated into the price of things.

She asked me, without curiosity, how short I wanted it. I told her. She nodded, as if confirming not a preference but a fact about the world that had always been true.

At some point, the conversation turned — not toward the war itself, which would have been too large and therefore impolite — but toward electricity, prices, the small arithmetic of survival. She spoke about outages the way one speaks about an unreliable relative: with irritation, but without surprise. There was no drama in it. Only adjustment.

When she finished, she brushed the hair from my shoulders with a quick, almost affectionate motion, as though correcting a minor administrative error. Then, as an afterthought — though in fact it was the final act — she trimmed my eyebrows with careful attention, restoring a kind of order that no larger authority seemed capable of providing.

I paid. She gave change. Neither of us mentioned that somewhere not very far away, men were attempting to alter the map of Europe with artillery.

Outside, the market continued. It occurred to me that whatever else this country lacks, it does not lack for systems that function in the absence of permission.

This is a detail that does not travel well.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

There is a species of Washington analysis that resembles an expensive suit worn without shoes. [A recent Washington Post editorial](https://www.washingtonpost.com/opinions/2026/03/29/trump-iran-russia-ukraine-united-states/) attempts such tailoring: Russia’s war in Ukraine, it argues, resembles America’s war with Iran. Missiles, proxies, waterways — the parallels align neatly from altitude, where detail disappears and everything resembles everything else.

A single sentence notes that Iran is not Ukraine. The sentence is correct and immediately abandoned.

It is worth not abandoning it.

Iran is not an unfinished state navigating its sovereignty between great powers. It is Persia — a civilization that has buried Greeks, Arabs, Mongols, and British imperialists without ceremony, and with something approaching aristocratic indifference. Ukraine is a state still learning which parts of itself are permanent. The comparison works only from very high up, and the author has clearly never been lower.

Then there is leadership, which the piece dispenses with in one exonerating clause and never revisits.

Ukraine is governed by a former entertainer who confuses visibility with control. Around him, the state developed a parallel economy of extraction. Operation Midas — named with an irony that would have delighted Gogol — revealed that Energoatom functioned less as national infrastructure than as personal resource. Timur Mindich, Z’s old comedy partner, call sign “Karlsson,” allegedly collected percentages while missiles fell on the power grid above. Ministers were fired. One fled on the Kyiv-Warsaw train.

More instructive is Andriy Yermak — “Ali Baba” in NABU recordings — Z’s former chief of staff, who allegedly instructed law enforcement to obstruct the anti-corruption bodies investigating his associates. He was eventually dismissed, publicly thanked for his patriotic service, and replaced on 2 January 2026.

He did not leave. He redistributed himself.

By February, Ukrainska Pravda had located Ali Baba in a private Kyiv office — “the Godfather working remotely.” Among his visitors: Oleksandr Kamyshin, former Minister of Strategic Industries, current strategic adviser to Z, who arrived carrying a notebook, stayed an hour, and subsequently told journalists he could not recall what they had discussed. This is not amnesia. It is a method.

While this was happening, the war itself quietly revealed something important.

Ukrainian drone manufacturers — funded by the state, distrustful of its procurement apparatus — built ten factories abroad in secret, exporting interceptors and keeping the core technology beyond administrative reach. Z [called this harmful](https://www.pravda.com.ua/eng/news/2026/03/28/8027604/) to Ukrainian exports. The manufacturers called it survival. Both were correct. Under Kamyshin’s ministerial watch, by his own admission, of 35 drone types developed with state funds, only three produced concrete results — the remainder existing primarily to extract grants, like sunflowers arranged to face the money.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

[Simon Shuster, writing in The Atlantic](https://archive.ph/cM9kn#selection-607.0-1191.38), sees the innovation. He has heard the sirens; his prose carries their specific gravity. His portrait of Armin Papperger — Rheinmetall’s CEO, who dismisses Ukrainian drones as kitchen craft and “Legos” while his order backlog approaches 135 billion euros — is accurate and sharp. Papperger cannot perceive the revolution because it invalidates his scale. Fair enough. Industrialists are not required to welcome their own obsolescence.

But Shuster has a blindspot of his own, and it is not accidental. His celebrated book on Z presents the invasion as, among other things, a story of remarkable wartime leadership. This framing does not merely linger in his Atlantic piece — it selects his sources. Kamyshin explains strategy with elegance and forgets conversations with precision. He is a superb interview subject. Shuster quotes him admiringly and moves on. The factories built abroad in deliberate secrecy are not an anecdote in this telling. They are the conclusion — and they are omitted.

Shuster’s job, it seems, is to make Z and his managers look good. He performs it with considerable literary grace.

Meanwhile, as this piece goes to press, Z has completed a Gulf tour — Qatar, Saudi Arabia, UAE, Jordan — selling Ukrainian drone expertise to buyers who do not inquire about Ali Baba or Karlsson, and who understand perfectly well that outcomes are separable from the systems that produced them. At home, an increasing number of Ukrainians feel distinctly unwell whenever he boards a plane on their behalf. They have learned what it means when the showman goes on tour.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

And here is what all three miss — Willick with his Washington frameworks, Papperger with his balance sheets, Shuster with his hagiographer’s brief.

In both Iran and Ukraine, the population has learned to route around its leadership. Not from ideology, but from experience. Civil networks in Iran. Autonomous war production in Ukraine. In both cases, resilience lives outside authority — because authority has repeatedly demonstrated what it does with the inside.

This is the similarity that matters. Not missiles. Not maps. Not metaphors.

Willick sees structure and misses agency. Papperger sees industry and misses revolution. Shuster sees revolution and misses betrayal. The Ukrainians, characteristically, see all of it — and get on with the work anyway.

They build accordingly. They always have.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

*Footnote on method: the Washington Post piece reflects a familiar condition — analysis conducted at altitude. This is not automatically fatal. Tocqueville also wrote from partial distance. But Tocqueville traveled. He dirtied his boots. He sat with people whose lives were the subject of his sentences. There is a specific confidence that grows in clean rooms. It produces elegant prose, stable abstractions, and, at the edges, the serene blindness of a man explaining rain while remaining perfectly dry.*
