*c/o 157th Mechanized Brigade (March 18, 2026)*

When Sean Penn skipped the Academy Awards and flew to Ukraine, it suggested seriousness. A man declining applause in favor of history. One imagines a quiet decision, the sort that improves in the retelling.

History, however, has its own casting director.

The photographs appeared. Penn near Sloviansk — close enough to borrow the front’s authority, not so close as to require its obligations. Soldiers arranged themselves with the courtesy reserved for guests and funerals. Beside him, positioned with the ease of long practice, sat Andriy Yermak: recently dismissed, recently investigated, recently in need of different lighting.

Yermak had, before his departure from office, attempted to weaken the institutions investigating him. This produced protests — not the theatrical kind, but the stubborn kind, where people insist on remembering what happened and who did it. The EU weighed in. Eventually even the system he had tried to rearrange found it necessary to rearrange him.

There was also the smaller matter, noted with restraint by Ukrainian outlets, that Yermak had shown little enthusiasm for military service. Yet here he was at the front, demonstrating sudden precision of movement. He did not come to fight. He came to stand beside someone who had come to witness fighting. An efficient division of labor.

Penn has made several such journeys. In 2023 he released *Superpower*, a documentary about Z that premiered at the Berlinale, where Penn introduced a live video address by the Ukrainian president with the solemnity of a man who has recently discovered that solemnity suits him. At a press conference, Penn described the film as “the idiot’s guide” to the conflict — an admission of such frank self-awareness that one wishes it had extended further. He also acknowledged making “an unapologetically biased film, because that is the truth we found.” The truth, in this case, arriving pre-packaged and camera-ready.

The film largely follows Penn following the story — cigarette-and-vodka-filled scenes, advocacy work, a shallow American’s journey to base-level understanding. Those expecting insight were advised to adjust expectations. What they received instead was Penn, convinced, after a brief bunker interview on the first day of the invasion, that Z possessed a superpower. “I believe with everything in my heart,” he told a journalist shortly afterward, “that this is a man of love, of intelligence and courage.” One notes that everything in Penn’s heart was formed over the course of a single afternoon, under conditions not optimally suited to biographical assessment.

He is not alone in this habit. Bernard-Henri Lévy, writing today in the [Wall Street Journal](https://archive.ph/YiVNL#selection-569.0-853.187), informs us that Z now “holds aces.” The metaphor arrives already worn smooth. Lévy’s Ukraine is the dealer, the Middle East the table, history a sequence of hands changing possession at decisive moments. What is less clear is where, in this well-lit arrangement, one places the dismissed official, the resisted investigation, the small persistent facts that decline to be dealt away. They do not appear — not because they are denied, but because they are unnecessary to the game being described.

This style of thinking prefers large metaphors to small realities. It does not stumble, because there is nothing on the floor.

It reminds me of a certain season of confident explanations in post-Soviet hotel lobbies — beneath soft lighting, among expatriates still uncertain to which story they belonged, grand interpretations offered with ease. The conclusions were large. The setting did not interfere — until you wandered outside to the nearest railway underpass, populated by people the metaphors had forgotten to account for.

Near Sloviansk, the setting is less accommodating. Mud has no interest in metaphors. The soldiers there understand something visitors often do not: reality is not arranged in advance and does not improve when described at altitude.

They pose for photographs nonetheless. This too is part of the work — to provide images that others will interpret later, in cleaner rooms, with better lighting.

In one version, a famous actor stands with a nation. In another, a compromised insider stands with a famous actor. In a third, less circulated version, both stand briefly with men who will remain after the image has moved on.

The soldier is not using anyone. He is being used — politely, almost imperceptibly — as part of a composition that requires his presence but not his circumstances.

The photograph tells the truth, as photographs do.

It shows who was there.

It does not show who is holding what.

The hearing proceeded with admirable thoroughness. Senators pressed Tulsi on what was imminent, what was omitted, what was truncated and why. She answered carefully, which is to say she answered other questions than the ones posed.

Questions asked, but not answered: how Russian targeting intelligence reaches Iranian operators. How Chinese components find their way into the weapons currently killing American servicemen. These would have required a different kind of hearing — one where the answers were not already known in advance and deemed inconvenient.
