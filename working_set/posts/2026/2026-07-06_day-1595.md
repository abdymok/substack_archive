Scenes of death and destruction not unlike last week. The pattern this morning was the same. First ballistic missiles, then jet-propelled Kamikaze drones, followed by cruise missiles and more drones. At least 11 reported dead and dozens injured as of this writing.

Kyiv is the main target not by accident. Z warns people in advance, as he did before last week’s strike, telling them to be careful, to heed the sirens, because intelligence suggested something large is coming. The population, dutifully, obeys — packs into the metro with fresh foam mats, sleeping bags, does what a warned population is supposed to do. And in the morning the death toll is announced anyway. The warning system functions perfectly, and it changes almost nothing, and everyone involved — the mayor, the air force spokesman, the woman being pulled from the rubble of a nine-story building she was told to shelter from — understands this and continues anyway, because the alternative to the ritual is no ritual at all, which is worse.

Russian missiles are not aimed at military installations, which are hundreds of kilometers away. What gets hit here is an apartment block in Podil, a hotel, an ambulance station, a monastery with a UNESCO plaque — an inventory of things that have no front line to speak of. The message, if we’re going to be honest about there being one, is addressed elsewhere: to the people of this city, who are meant to understand that warning is not the same as safety, and being warned changes nothing about who gets carried out on a stretcher. And it’s addressed to the people who keep being asked for more interceptors and keep sending fewer than asked: a Patriot battery without enough missiles is worthless. It is not a demonstration anymore, in the sense of a single dramatic gesture. It’s a rehearsal that has been running long enough to become policy, performed for an audience that keeps watching and keeps not quite doing the one thing that would end the performance.

In other news, heavy rains washed out hundreds of newly dug graves at the National Military Memorial Cemetery near Vita-Poshtova in the Hatne community of Kyiv region, thirty minutes south of the capital. The cemetery’s administration moved quickly to correct the record: [this was natural soil compaction in burial places,](https://sud.ua/en/news/ukraine/365766-u-natsionalnomu-viiskovomu-memorialnomu-kladovyshchi-poiasnyly-poiavu-provaliv-pislia-zlyv) not the washing away of graves or the influence of groundwater. Compaction, not collapse. A process, not an event.

What the statement declined to mention — because statements like this never mention it, that’s the whole genre — is that the site was contested well before the first grave was dug. Hydrologists and ecologists **had warned for years** that the chosen location sat on nature-reserve land with a high water table, unsuited to burials at scale. A district administrative court had apparently ruled the land’s use for a cemetery unlawful, a ruling the Verkhovna Rada is said to have legislated around with 267 votes; and the contractor entangled in the construction is linked, to a sanctioned businessman more recently in the news for an alleged assassination attempt in Monaco. Whether all of that survives scrutiny is a separate question from whether it’s the kind of thing anyone wants to believe, and in Ukraine those two questions rarely get to stay separate for long.

This is the feudal autocracy running its most efficient process of all: the conversion of consequence into vocabulary. Land-use law inconvenient to Z and his top managers gets a legislative workaround. Warnings from people who actually understand water tables get filed under noise, the same folder Z kept Western intelligence’s invasion warnings in back when the state was busy prioritizing the May holidays over the border. And when the mud finally, predictably, arrives — not as tragedy but as the fully anticipated output of a fully anticipated failure — the state doesn’t apologize for the decision. It apologizes for the footage.

Sorry for your concern, not sorry for the swamp.

Here is the arrangement in its complete, closed form: the state that cannot keep the front line supplied without laundering procurement contracts through Miami real estate is the same state that cannot bury its dead without laundering hydrology through a press release. Nothing has been built here — not the drones, not the cemetery — that will still exist once the money and the war both stop being useful reasons not to ask who built it and on whose water table. The soldiers get the compaction. Somebody else, eventually, gets the audit. The families get told, in the meantime, to trust the state’s account of the ground over the state’s own photographs of the ground opening.
