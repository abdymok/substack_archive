Yesterday's post put words in Z's mouth before he'd said them: no interceptors, everyone's on their own, things will be bad. It looked like a rhetorical flourish. A few hours later it became the headline.¹

# [Ukraine fails to halt Russian barrage as Z blames partners for ‘horrific casualties’](https://www.washingtonpost.com/world/2026/08/05/ukraine-fails-stop-russian-missile-barrage-after-zelensky-says-interceptors-gone/)

*U.S.-made systems are Kyiv’s only means to shoot down ballistic missiles, and Ukraine says it has run out of interceptors. Seventeen Ukrainians died overnight. - [The Washington Post](http://wapo.com), August 6, 2026*

Z’s actual language, after the August 1 strike, was that partners needed to understand anti-ballistic systems aren’t meant to sit in warehouses. By evening he was demanding “real answers” to what he called Russian terror. Four days later nothing had changed except the casualty count. Yesterday’s barrage killed seventeen and wounded dozens. Kyiv’s depleted air defenses failed to stop a single incoming missile, and Z went back to Telegram to blame everyone except the people whose job it is to prepare for exactly this.

The appeal abroad has become the policy. Everything else now follows it around carrying the bags.

Kyiv did not become collateral in the forty-day campaign by accident, and it did not become collateral without warning. The cost of answering escalation with escalation was calculable before the campaign began because Russia had very few ways left to raise the stakes. One of them was always obvious.

Russia banks part of its ballistic inventory for winter. It still intends to freeze Ukraine again, and that requires a reserve. The missiles left over are divided between military targets and Kyiv. Kyiv is the one asymmetric answer available to a country that cannot yet strike Russia in kind with ballistic weapons. This isn’t mysterious. It’s bookkeeping. Kyiv is simply where the remainder gets entered.

The problem was never dependence on partners. The problem was turning the partners’ delivery schedule into the strategy instead of treating it as something a strategy should gradually make less decisive.

What’s worth noticing isn’t that Z repeats himself. Politicians do that. It’s that responsibility keeps migrating away from him.

On Monday, before this latest strike, he was talking about seventeen companies exploiting sanctions loopholes to keep producing the missiles landing in Kyiv. That is a real problem. It is also an unusually convenient one because sanctions are enforced by somebody else’s government in somebody else’s capital. Trump, meanwhile, had spent the previous week retreating from his own promise on domestic Patriot production, providing another convenient somebody else right on cue.

There is another story running underneath this one, and it is aimed not at foreign governments but at Ukrainians.

Ukraine’s long-range strikes on Russian refineries and logistics are presented as precise military operations bringing peace closer. Russia’s strikes on Ukraine are presented as something that exists outside cause and effect, as though describing them as retaliation would somehow justify them. Both stories lean in the same direction. Ukrainian strikes are real. Russian strikes are real. Both shape the battlefield. Both alter the next day’s decisions.

Yet a population that increasingly wants the war to end is being sold not the end of the war but its next chapter, wrapped in the language of peace.

None of this requires unusual insight. It requires only paying attention to four years of these posts,² where every major strike follows the same sequence: casualties, appeal abroad, responsibility reassigned to whoever is least able to answer back.

A pattern this stable isn’t prediction. It’s arithmetic. You don’t predict the answer to two plus two.

You already know it.

Nobody upstairs has gone to turn off the tap.

Zero of the missiles fired at Kyiv on Wednesday were intercepted. On Saturday it was one out of twenty-seven.

Progress, if you’re grading on the wrong curve.

The same abdication scales down as easily as it scales up. In Kherson, Russian drone operators have spent weeks hunting civilians one at a time, a slower and more personal cruelty than a ballistic barrage but the same permission structure underneath it — nobody upstream decided [this](https://archive.ph/8JtWx#selection-613.0-898.0) was worth stopping, so somebody with a drone and no oversight decided what came next.
