We thought about *Dr. Strangelove* this morning at dawn celebrating the end of the first month of this year’s running season. *Dr. Strangelove*, or *Red Alert*, because I fell asleep last night listening to Trump’s inane Cabinet meeting. The movie bubbled up this morning, specifically the War Room scene where Dr. Strangelove himself keeps losing control of his own arm. His right hand, apparently with a will of its own, keeps lurching toward a Nazi salute, which he has to wrestle back down with his left hand — at one point nearly strangling himself in the process.

Before setting out I listened to two [old white aspiring Russian philosophers](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=rQsQ3lTz2lA&t=504s) invoke Strugatsky’s *Hard to be a God* describing Team USA’s Iran folly, but the interlocutors have zero stake in the outcome of the disaster beyond moral discomfort.

Where the Strugatskys gave us the paralysis of the witness — the observer from a more advanced civilization, watching catastrophe accumulate, forbidden to act — *Red Alert* — and especially its Kubrick incarnation — is about what happens when the machinery of intervention runs ahead of the humans nominally in charge of it. Trump’s golf buddy Witkoff saying "it all smelled fishy" and Trump launching the strike the next morning sits uncomfortably close to the Strangelove universe[1](#footnote-1).

A *New York Times* [dispatch](https://www.nytimes.com/2026/03/26/us/politics/trump-iran-talks.html) from Washington, dated the day after our Thursday broadcast, fills in the details with a specificity that is almost novelistic in its bleakness. We learn that a senior American official attended the critical Geneva meeting — the last chance, apparently, to avoid war — without technical experts, and emerged surprised to discover that Iran considered uranium enrichment a sovereign right it had been publicly asserting for decades. We learn that this same official repeatedly mispronounced the name of the international agency that has monitored Iranian nuclear activity for years. The president launched his attack the following night. General Ripper, one feels, would have recognized the epistemology.

The Strugatskys during the 1960s weren’t writing a detached philosophical puzzle about when intervention is justified — they were writing in moral anguish, from inside a repressive system, about the unbearable cost of being forced to watch and do nothing.
