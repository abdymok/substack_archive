A brief note to anyone arriving here for the first time: I plan to start corresponding with people again once I get over feeling guilty about not corresponding with people for the last several years.

Call it a Samsa moment. I went to sleep as someone who got away with pretending the rest of the world didn’t exist, and woke up as someone who could not.

For years, I have been trying to work out what to do with [ukraine@war](https://abdymok.substack.com).

It has grown into something rather larger than the website on which it appears: more than 1,700 posts, roughly 700,000 words, and twelve years of trying to make sense of what has happened in Ukraine, what I thought was happening at the time, and the way the war looks from Kyiv.

A normal archive lets you find things.

A search engine lets you search for things.

I wanted something that would let people ask questions of the archive.

Not just:

Find every mention of Fire Point.

But:

When did Fire Point first appear in ukraine@war, what did I think the company was doing at the time, and how did that understanding change over the following three years?

Or:

What did ukraine@war get right before it became conventional wisdom?

Or:

What has ukraine@war written about running long distances in Kyiv?[1](#footnote-1)

I did not expect a machine to notice that the running and the writing are the same argument.

So I donated the archive to Gemini.

Google has just renamed NotebookLM to Gemini Notebook. Apparently, the world did not yet have enough things called Gemini.

The archive itself is a separate PHP/MySQL project, built specifically for searching and interrogating the text of the posts. It isn’t simply this website with an AI bolted onto the side.

Run entirely through the browser—no FTP mucky-muck—it is intended to make it possible to interrogate the archive and quickly obtain detailed information about the war, the people and institutions involved in it, and the way all of this has looked from Kyiv.

**[INTERROGATE THE UKRAINE@WAR ARCHIVE](https://notebooklm.google.com/notebook/0c30d4e5-3bba-4d3c-a488-01ee2444e3bd).** It’s free. It’s fun!

The current archive covers the period from mid-2021 through the end of June 2026.

I have forgotten a great deal of what I wrote.

The words have not.

**What it’s supposed to do**

You can ask it to:

Find every prediction made in the archive about the future of the war. Which predictions were correct, which were wrong and which remain unresolved?

I am particularly interested in the last category.

It is always comforting to discover that one was neither right nor wrong.

One is merely awaiting further developments.

You can ask:

Which people, weapons, companies and ideas were once discussed frequently in ukraine@war but have since disappeared?

Or:

Find stories that seemed minor when they were written but later became historically important.

Or:

Which people first appeared as minor figures and later became important?

Or perhaps:

What is the central question ukraine@war has been trying to answer since 2014, even when the author did not know he was asking it?

You can ask what the archive says about the weather in Kyiv for the past five years.

**The experiment**

The point is not to ask the AI to summarize ukraine@war. That would be boring.

The point is to interrogate the archive.

Ask it what disappeared. Ask it what I got wrong. Ask it which arguments survived twelve years of war — and which ones should have been taken out behind the shed and quietly disposed of.

Go ahead. Ask it anything.

Ask it something you have always wondered about the war.

Ask it something you remember reading here years ago.

Ask it to find a person, an idea, a prediction or a story.

Then ask it what happened next.

And if you find something interesting, send it to me.

**There is one important thing to know about the archive.**

It does not have the photographs, maps, illustrations, screen grabs of leaked recordings, bad drawings or satellite imagery that accompany the posts.

And those are half the weight of the archive.

They have recorded what the war looked like from where I happened to be standing: Kyiv under attack, smoke on the horizon, blackouts, the metro, ruined buildings, soldiers, streets, sunsets, breakfasts and all the strange, stubborn fragments of ordinary life that continue even when Russia is trying very hard to make ordinary life impossible.

Many of the photographs have become wildly popular with readers.

Some have been viewed more often than the posts they accompanied.

The AI, at least, can tell you what I wrote about.

**Subscribers can see and hear what I’m talking about.**

If you are already a subscriber, thank you. You have helped keep *ukraine@war* alive—and helped finance the ongoing attempt to understand the horrible bloody mess we are all currently living through.

If you are not a subscriber, you are welcome to rummage through the archive.

If you enjoy the experiment, consider subscribing.

You will get the continuing dispatches.

You will get the photographs.

**And you will help keep me alive for whatever comes next.**
