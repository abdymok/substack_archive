Today is the exact one-year anniversary of the day Z signed the law gutting the independence of the country’s anti-corruption agencies — triggering the biggest wartime protests Ukraine had seen, until this week.

Not one of the outlets covering Tuesday’s reshuffle seems to have noticed the date.

We did, at the time, in a [dispatch](https://abdymok.substack.com/p/day-1246?utm_source=publication-search) now tucked behind a paywall that was considerably less composed about it than we’re managing to be now.

[Image]

With less than two weeks left in Z’s 40-day influence campaign against Russia, he has named a new commander-in-chief.

This is not the same as appointing one.

The distinction is doing a great deal of quiet work.

After several days of protest in Kyiv and elsewhere, Z announced that Maj. Gen. Mykhailo Drapaty, commander of the Joint Forces, would take over from Oleksandr Syrsky.

He asked Syrsky, with the courtesy one extends to a man being removed, to hand over his affairs to his successor in a civilized manner.

It was a gracious sentence built around an event that has not happened.

Here is how it read from Washington, in the Institute for the Study of War’s nightly assessment:

Z “dismissed ... Syrsky and appointed ... Drapaty as his replacement late on July 21.” — I[nstitute for the Study of War](https://understandingwar.org/research/russia-ukraine/russian-offensive-campaign-assessment-july-21-2026/), July 21, 2026

Note the tense.

Not designated.

Appointed.

Full stop. Past and completed. The way an institute of record has to write it when choosing between the categories available and none of them is:

**“Pending a signature that depends on a minister who does not yet exist.”**

That is not a criticism of the institute.

It is a comment on how much information a clean sentence can lose.

Ukrainian procedure requires a sitting defense minister to submit the candidacy of a new commander-in-chief before the president can appoint one.

Ukraine does not currently have a sitting defense minister.

It has an acting one, Yevhen Khmara, who is expected in time to be confirmed by the Rada. After that, he will formally propose the man everyone has already been told is the new commander-in-chief.

Pavlo Palisa is understood to be the next chief of the general staff.

None of this is official.

All of it is settled.

The country has become fluent in the grammar of the fait accompli.

What it has not become fluent in is patience with the one demand the protests actually made: the reinstatement of Mykhailo Fedorov to the defense ministry he was pushed out of.

That will not happen.

Z has offered him, by his own description, a worthy post overseeing the state’s technological development — worthy, but not the ministry, and not adjacent to it.

So the arithmetic of the week is this:

The commander everyone objected to is gone.

The minister whose return everyone demanded is not coming back.

And the crowds that produced both outcomes are left to work out for themselves which of their two grievances was actually addressed.

A protest can be satisfied without being answered.

This appears to be such a case.

Syrsky, for what it’s worth, has not conceded anything.

The day before this designation, he wrote that he had no intention of resigning.

A general who intends to fight for his position does not necessarily change his intentions because the position has been given, on paper that isn’t yet paper, to someone else.

His response to his own removal, whenever it arrives, will tell us more about how settled this settlement is than anything Z’s office has announced so far.

The shake-up arrives on the anniversary of the crowds that forced Z, eventually, to abandon the law folding the country’s anti-corruption agencies into a structure he controlled.

The comparison is obvious enough that it’s already being made.

What’s less obvious is whether it holds.

The timeline is worth getting right before deciding.

Franko worked because the thing the crowd wanted was simple and binary:

Kill the bill or don’t.

But “worked” took longer than the shorthand suggests.

Z signed the original law.

Protests erupted the same day.

It was two days before he proposed reversing it and more than a week before parliament actually passed the reversal.

This week’s crowd wanted two things and got a partial and an ambiguity within roughly the same span.

Syrsky is gone in substance but not yet in paper.

Fedorov is not coming back, in any form the protesters would recognize as the demand being met.

So before anyone files this alongside last July as a second instance of the street functioning as an unelected fourth branch of government, it’s worth asking the plainer question:

Did it actually work?

Or did it merely look, for one news cycle, like the same trick performed twice?

So:

A commander has been named, not installed.

A minister has been designated, not confirmed.

A dismissed reformer has been offered a consolation that isn’t the thing he was promised he’d get back.

A general who was supposedly eased out with civility hasn’t said he’s leaving.

Four sentences.

Four different tenses.

None of them finished.
