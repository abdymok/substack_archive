*Puhleaze*

Read it against [Day 1511](https://abdymok.substack.com/p/day-1511) and the irony becomes almost architectural.

[Bloomberg](http://bloomberg.com) gives you Budanov admiring Napoleon at his tomb in Paris — his first international trip as chief of staff. The chess set. The katana. The frog named Petro. The sitcom Friends playing in reception. The adviser who profiles Russian negotiators psychologically. The man who “buries many people” and therefore understands the true cost of war.

It is a masterpiece of profile-writing in the service of myth-making. Every detail is chosen to produce a specific figure: the warrior-intellectual, the reluctant statesman, the realist who can end the war. The piece even provides its own disclaimer — “it’s too early to think about that now” — which is the oldest way of thinking about it.

What the piece does not contain: Wagner. Navalny. The invasion briefings. The daily intelligence reports that did not name the routes. None of it. Not one word.

And there, in the window of Budanov’s new office, if you look carefully at the Bloomberg piece: a pysanka behind the secretary’s desk. A painted Easter egg — the same icon that surfaces in every register of this war, for every audience, in every language.

The Paris tag. The paska. The cat. Everything rhymes. The Z system is very good at this. The details change. The omissions don’t.

Meanwhile, Z is zipping around Europen capitals signing bilateral drone framework agreements — the latest iteration of a habit that never quite became a strategy.

There was a time when nothing had a name yet, and therefore nothing was officially happening. Only the usual confusion of volunteers, engineers, and officers trying to explain to one another — across different languages of urgency — why paper maps were no longer sufficient for a war that had already become digital without permission.

In those early years, groups like Aerorozvidka appeared not as institutions but as habits of mind. They were what happened when people who knew how systems should behave met a system that did not yet agree. Drones were assembled in workshops that still smelled faintly of domestic life. Reconnaissance reports travelled faster than the bureaucracy could file objections. It was not revolution, exactly. It was improvisation that refused to stay temporary.

From this atmosphere, a new idea slowly formed: that the battlefield could be seen, not in fragments, but as a continuous field of information. The system later called Delta did not arrive like a discovery. It accreted. It passed through committees, field tests, wartime necessity, and institutional hesitation. It became official in the way things become official in wartime Ukraine — not by declaration, but by exhaustion of alternatives.

There was a moment when this accumulation crossed a threshold. Systems that had once lived in parallel — military, volunteer, experimental — began to align. Not perfectly, not cleanly, but sufficiently to function under pressure. NATO interoperability entered the vocabulary, not as aspiration, but as grammar. The language of war itself began to change its syntax.

Outside this process, or perhaps inside it in another form, the new defense economy began to take shape. Startups appeared with names that sounded like weather phenomena or birds that had forgotten migration routes. Brave1 became a kind of administrative greenhouse where ideas were no longer asked whether they were serious, only whether they could survive contact with reality.

Western partners entered this ecosystem not as architects, but as suppliers of cash and components — analytics systems, mapping tools, integration layers. Palantir was among those present in this landscape, offering structures for making sense of data that already existed in overwhelming quantity. But the architecture remained, stubbornly, Ukrainian in its orientation: fragmented, redundant, adaptive. Nothing truly centralized survived long without being forced to justify itself again.

And then there are the people who move between levels — between ministries, industries, and presidential corridors — trying to keep the system from separating into incompatible realities. Not controllers, not symbols, but something closer to translators under pressure. Their task is not to impose order, but to prevent different kinds of order from cancelling each other out.

What emerges from all this is not a clean story of ownership. It is something more awkward and more durable: a hybrid structure in which the state is everywhere and yet not alone, in which private actors build what the state cannot specify in advance, and in which foreign systems attach themselves like scaffolding that may or may not be removed later.

People sometimes describe this as a loss of control. It is more precise to call it redistribution of authorship. The map is no longer drawn by one hand. It is revised continuously by necessity.

Wars, in the end, do not only destroy. They also assign vocabulary. And once a new vocabulary takes hold, it becomes difficult to remember the exact moment when it stopped being improvised speech and started becoming the only language available.
