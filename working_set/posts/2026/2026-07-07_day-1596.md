Vonnegut’s answer to trauma was to abandon linear narration. In *Slaughterhouse-Five*, Billy Pilgrim becomes unstuck in time because chronology can no longer contain Dresden. “So it goes” is less a catchphrase than emotional flatline: the only response left when horror has exhausted description.

Welcome to Vyshneve.

Houses burned to their foundations. So it goes.

Entire micro-districts treated simultaneously as crime scenes and cemeteries, pending confirmation of which. So it goes.

Six hundred evacuated. Fifty more by armored transport because the roads themselves had become targets. Five hundred relocated “temporarily,” a word that now means only *later*. So it goes.

The detonations continue at irregular intervals, as though the war has forgotten to turn itself off. Officials promise replacement passports within days—an admirable administrative reflex applied to a settlement with too few intact buildings left to issue them. So it goes.

This is not damage. Damage implies something remains against which loss can be measured. This is a village being informed, street by street, that it used to exist. Billy Pilgrim would recognize the tense.

Hours later, Z [sits down](https://archive.ph/jbqM9#selection-1765.0-2041.90) with a Tralfamadorian from *The* *Financial Times* and describes a war entering its decisive phase in the air. The land front is largely static. Russia has been denied the sea. The remaining contest is overhead, where, he argues, Ukraine’s principal weakness remains air defense. During Monday’s attack—the same attack that devastated Vyshneve—not one of the twenty-nine ballistic missiles was intercepted. Patriot interceptors, he says, sometimes arrive only a day before the barrage they are meant to stop.

Vyshneve is that sentence made concrete. So it goes.

A military analyst would make the same point more coldly. Patriots and SAMP/T batteries exist in meaningful numbers around Kyiv and almost nowhere else—not Dnipro, Zaporizhzhia, Odesa, Mykolaiv, Kharkiv or Sumy. Perfect air defense exists nowhere.

Billy Pilgrim and Z share the affliction, not the response. Billy drifts through catastrophe because sequence has ceased to matter. Z, briefed every thirty minutes as the war jumps from Kyiv rooftops to Omsk refineries to NATO summits without transition, refuses that passivity. His nightly address is not a shrug but an argument that the chaos still has shape—that it bends toward air defense, production lines, diplomacy, tomorrow.

Putin “vanquished” ordinary apartment blocks—Z’s word, repeated with the flat consistency of someone who has exhausted synonyms. Sixty-eight missiles. More than 350 drones. Twenty-two dead. Ninety injured. Kyiv again.

So it goes.

Vyshneve receives one clause in the speech: *“The situation is difficult... because of secondary detonation.”* The bureaucratic register compresses into twelve words what the smoking streets had already said all morning. The speech and the wreckage describe the same event from different altitudes.

Z [says](https://t.me/V_Zelenskiy_official/19817) he wants the Security Service to report what happened. Of course he already knows. Klymenko briefs him every half hour: fires, rubble, rescue. The Cabinet will release emergency funds because Vyshneve no longer possesses the resources to repair what was done to it overnight.

He thanks the rescuers, the mayors, the businesses, the partners.

So it goes.

Something that’s been bugging me lately are the [center-of-gravity arguments](https://abdymok.substack.com/p/2023-military-campaign-propects?utm_source=substack&utm_campaign=post_embed&utm_medium=web) of bona fide experts. A friend of mine, a deputy battalion commander on the zero line for more four years, put it to me in June with great tenderness: we have reached, he said, a durable balance. Modus vivendi.

I believed him for about three weeks, which is roughly how long it takes Moscow to reload.

Because here is the trouble with balance, durable or otherwise: it assumes both parties are weighing the same thing on the same scale. One side is counting kilometers. The other is counting how many Patriot interceptors the enemy has left, and when the answer is not enough, the correct military term for what follows is not “stalemate” — it is “Tuesday.” Twenty-nine ballistic missiles arrive over Kyiv in a single night, unintercepted, and somewhere a spokesman explains, patiently, like a doctor delivering a diagnosis to a child, that only American systems can catch these, and America’s next delivery is scheduled for 2027, which is to say: after the patient has already been discharged, in several senses of the word.

Meanwhile the same capital that cannot always intercept a missile aimed at itself managed, the same week, to set fire to a refinery two and a half thousand kilometers away — a small, embarrassing miracle that the Kremlin’s own television was instructed not to mention, on the theory that the Russian viewer should hear only about the missiles that land, never the drones that do. This is the durable balance we have reached: one side can reach Omsk, the other side can reach the fifth floor of an apartment building on any given night it feels like proving a point before a summit. A balance, yes. Durable, no. A seesaw is also, briefly, level — right before someone gets off.

So my friend is not wrong that the war has answered its two big questions, about whether Ukraine exists and whether Russia will collapse. He is only wrong about what comes next being arithmetic. It is not a ledger. It is a country waiting to see whether the world remembers, at the summit, that a balance held up entirely by someone else’s missiles is not a balance at all — it is a favor, renewed nightly, at the at the pleasure of western leaders whose convictions have the shelf life of a news cycle.
