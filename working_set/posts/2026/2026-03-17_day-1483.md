**What Are They Actually Optimizing For?**

This year at the World Economic Forum in Davos, Z explained Europe to itself.

This happens from time to time. Europe, being large and somewhat self-conscious, often appreciates an external perspective on its own deficiencies. Z spoke about courage, about competence, about the dangers of hesitation. Europe listened, as Europe does, with a look that combines interest with mild surprise at being addressed in this tone.

It was, all things considered, an unusual [lecture](https://www.president.gov.ua/en/news/zvernennya-prezidenta-do-uchasnikiv-specialnogo-zasidannya-v-102517).

Ukraine, at that very moment, remained a country in which institutions functioned the way old houses do during a storm: they hold, but they creak. Top officials appeared in corruption investigations with a regularity that suggested not scandal so much as scheduling. Mobilization worked, though visibly. Procurement existed, though not always in a way that invited inspection.

But Davos is not a place for creaking houses. It is a place for clean sentences.

And so the sentences were clean.

Another forum. Another audience. Another careful redirection outward — toward people who cannot vote in Ukrainian elections but can influence the flow of weapons and money. Z rarely speaks to Ukrainian journalists. He speaks to fawning ones and foreign actors. The conversation inside the country — the one conducted under sirens, in kitchens, on Telegram channels of uncertain origin — proceeds largely without him.

This, too, is a kind of policy.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

------------------------------------------------------------------------

There is a certain type of man — one meets him in provincial theaters, occasionally in politics — who steps onto the stage and, for a moment, becomes indistinguishable from the role he is playing. He speaks clearly. He gestures well. The audience understands him immediately, which is not a small achievement.

The difficulty begins later, when the set must be dismantled and something real must be built in its place.

Trump is this kind of performer. Z, in a different register, is also this kind of performer.

One lectures allies about loyalty while loosening the threads that make loyalty possible. The other lectures partners about competence while presiding over institutions that visibly struggle to produce it.

The circumstances are not the same. The stakes are not the same.

The method, however, has a family resemblance.

Performance.

Performance is efficient. It travels. It compresses complexity into something that fits between advertisements. It produces applause, which is measurable, and headlines, which are even more so.

Reality does not travel as well. It prefers to remain where it is, accumulating consequences.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

There is, in machine learning, a concept that would have been appreciated by certain Soviet administrators, had they encountered it at the right moment: the objective function.

It is not what the system claims to do. It is what the system, when observed patiently, actually does.

A factory may claim to produce shoes. If, over time, it produces only reports about shoes, then reports are its true output, and the rest is decoration.

Politics, too, produces outputs.

In Ukraine, official news is not optimized for the reader’s understanding. It is optimized for something simpler: the continued stability of Z’s position. Often these goals align, which allows everyone to proceed without discomfort. But alignment is not identity. When they diverge, one notices small absences. Then larger ones. Eventually one notices that certain questions no longer quite form in the mind.

This is not censorship in the theatrical sense. No one storms the stage. The script simply narrows.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

It is a useful exercise, then, to ask — of any system, in any country:

What is it actually optimizing for?

Not what it says. What does it produce?

This week, the answer became easier to observe.

It did not flatter anyone.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

Three weeks ago, the United States began striking Iran.

The operation was described, depending on the speaker, as limited, open-ended, decisive, precautionary, and, on one occasion, a “little excursion,” which has the advantage of sounding both temporary and optional.

Language, in such moments, becomes a kind of scaffolding. It allows one to move around the structure without quite touching it.

Money, however, has less patience for language. In two weeks, approximately \$12 billion was spent. The Strait of Hormuz — that narrow, inconvenient fact of geography — became less navigable. Oil prices, obedient to physics rather than rhetoric, began to rise.

To calm the markets, sanctions on Russian oil were briefly lifted.

This produced an elegant arrangement: a war partially financed by the alleviation of pressure on the same system funding another war, which the same administration was attempting, in parallel, to conclude.

It is possible to describe this as contradiction. It is perhaps more accurate to describe it as consistency of a different kind.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

At a certain point, one must listen not only to what is said, but to how often it changes.

Iran wants a deal. Iran does not want a deal. The terms are insufficient. The terms are unspecified. The leader may be alive. If he is alive, he should surrender.

Allies are invited to participate. Allies decline.

Officials promise timelines. Other officials, with the air of men who have seen timelines before, gently withdraw the promise.

At a press briefing, the defense secretary — a man who has spent years insisting on the primacy of war as both language and identity — described a particularly successful day of strikes. In the same conversation, he expressed a hope that a news network covering the war might soon come under more congenial ownership.

Not a complaint.

A preference.

In certain systems, preferences are more informative than statements.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

A few days earlier, Z had offered a different kind of statement.

Ukraine, he said, had definitive proof that Russia had transferred drone technology and targeting intelligence to Iran — the same drones now shaping the conflict in the Gulf.

This was not entirely new information. It had, as they say, circulated. But it had not been placed, so directly, into the American conversation.

Russia is helping to kill your soldiers.

With familiar tools.

In a familiar way.

The response, from Washington, was that this detail was not, at present, a focus of attention.

Focus, like language, is an allocation problem.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

Z went further. Ukraine, having spent several years learning how not to be destroyed by these drones, offered its expertise. Teams were dispatched. Techniques were demonstrated. The proposal was not dressed in sentiment.

Technology, in exchange for funding.

Experience, in exchange for relevance.

It was, in its way, refreshingly clear.

The response was also clear.

“We don’t need help.”

------------------------------------------------------------------------

If one were inclined toward geometry, one might sketch the situation.

A country under attack develops a method of survival. Another country, newly engaged with the same method of attack, declines the offered solution. Meanwhile, the source of the method receives temporary economic relief to stabilize a third variable affected by the second country’s actions.

The lines do not intersect.

They run, neatly, in parallel.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

This is not a disagreement in policy. It is not even, strictly speaking, a disagreement in priorities.

It is a set of systems, each pursuing its own objective function, producing outcomes that appear contradictory only if one assumes a shared goal.

Remove that assumption, and the contradictions resolve themselves.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

Once one begins to look in this way, certain symmetries emerge.

Trump, for instance, does not optimize for strategy in the classical sense. Strategy implies subordination to an external objective. He optimizes for dominance within the immediate frame. The conversation itself is the territory. To concede dependence is to lose ground. To acknowledge assistance is to redraw the map in someone else’s favor.

And so assistance becomes unnecessary. Reporting becomes hostile. Ownership becomes a solution.

The system is internally coherent.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

Now consider the other system.

While Z was explaining competence to Europe, Ukraine’s anti-corruption bureau was investigating a scheme inside Energoatom. Percentages were discussed. Contracts were arranged. Money moved with the quiet efficiency that money often displays when unobserved.

At the center of the network was a familiar figure: a longtime associate, a business partner, a man without formal office and therefore without formal constraints.

And, incidentally, a man connected to the structure that produces the national broadcast through which a significant portion of the country understands the war.

This is not, strictly speaking, unusual. Power tends to concentrate. Information tends to follow it.

Z responded correctly: support for investigators, dismissals, sanctions.

Correct responses, however, do not dismantle systems. They adjust them.

The broadcast continues. The access remains limited. The message retains its shape.

One might say: the house still creaks, but the facade has been repainted.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

It is important, here, to resist the temptation of symmetry carried too far.

These situations are not equivalent. One country is fighting for survival. Another is managing its image of strength. The moral weight is not distributed evenly.

But methods have a way of resembling one another, even when purposes diverge.

And resemblance, once noticed, is difficult to unsee.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

A recent study in Ukraine found something quietly interesting[1](#footnote-1).

People have become better at recognizing falsehood. They check sources more often. They identify manipulation with increasing skill.

At the same time, they trust less.

Fewer believe that the media reports what matters. Fewer believe it serves the public. Many assume, with a certain resignation, that it serves those who control it.

This is not confusion.

It is adaptation.

When the environment produces conflicting signals, the rational response is not certainty. It is caution.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

There is a tendency to attribute this condition entirely to external influence. And external influence exists. It is persistent, well-funded, and often effective.

But one should not overlook the contribution of internal design.

If a system rewards alignment with power, it will produce alignment. If it constrains certain questions, those questions will diminish. Over time, the absence becomes normal.

The fog, in other words, does not require a single author.

It can be the collective output of many small decisions, each of which appears reasonable in isolation.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

Democracy, like certain delicate mechanical devices, depends on tolerances.

Not agreement. Not harmony. Simply the shared assumption that reality exists independently of the mechanisms used to describe it.

When that assumption weakens, the device continues to function — for a time. The dials move. The readings change. But the connection to the underlying system becomes, gradually, unreliable.

At that point, performance becomes not merely an addition to governance, but a substitute for it.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

And so we return, as these things often do, to the question of the objective function.

Observe long enough, and patterns emerge.

Not alliances. Not institutions. Not even, reliably, outcomes.

Presence.

Visibility.

Control of the frame.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

Once this becomes apparent, a small shift occurs.

One stops asking whether the performance is persuasive.

One begins to ask what it is designed to obscure.

This is a less entertaining question.

It is also a more useful one.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

There is, however, a modest consolation.

Under pressure, people learn. The proportion of those who accept information without question has declined. Verification, once a habit of specialists, becomes a general practice.

This does not solve the problem.

It changes the conditions under which the problem is experienced.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

In the end, the prescription is neither technical nor novel.

Do not outsource your judgment. Not to systems. Not to narratives. Not even to those with whom you agree — especially not to those with whom you agree.

Thinking is uncomfortable. It always has been. But we now live in an age engineered to fill every pause, every idle moment, with something frictionless and immediate. The scroll doesn’t ask you to think. It thinks for you — confidently, at scale, in fifteen seconds.

So the competition isn’t really between good ideas and bad ones. It’s between ideas and the path of least resistance.

Ask a simple question: what are they actually optimizing for?

Then watch what they do. Not between notifications — just watch, for long enough that the answer becomes obvious.
