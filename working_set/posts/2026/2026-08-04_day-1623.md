The same week Ukraine’s ambassadors were told to start tracking Patriot stock levels in their host countries and moving interceptors off warehouse shelves, Denys Shtilerman published [an op-ed in The Washington Post](https://www.washingtonpost.com/opinions/2026/08/03/ukraine-shows-war-how-build-modern-defense-industry/) arguing that modern war punishes single points of failure — a single supplier, a single factory, a single political decision — and rewards redundant, networked, auditable ecosystems. He is right about the argument.

He is, at the moment, the least credible messenger available to make it.

Shtilerman is the founder of Fire Point, the company behind Ukraine’s FP-1 strike drones and the Flamingo cruise missile, and by his own account soon to be a manufacturer of ballistic-missile interceptors and a ballistic missile of its own. Fire Point is also the company that received roughly **billions (\$)** in state contracts while operating, essentially, as Ukraine’s only domestic producer of long-range strike systems — the closest thing the war effort has to the single-manufacturer dependency Shtilerman spent his op-ed warning against. Ukraine’s Antimonopoly Committee has been asked, more than once, why that concentration was allowed to happen. Fire Point’s own chief technology officer has given contradictory answers about whether a proposed foreign partnership even passed antitrust review.

Then there is the matter of who almost bought half the company. Shtilerman told a parliamentary investigative commission in May that Z’s former business partner, Tymur Mindich — the sanctioned businessman at the center of the corruption case implicating the president and his top managers — negotiated for a fifty-percent stake in Fire Point from 2024 into 2025, with an offer eventually reaching a billion dollars. The deal collapsed, Shtilerman says, over his own insistence on “clean” financing and a legal firewall against toxic shareholders. Perhaps. The recordings that triggered the wider Mindich scandal include Mindich speaking to the then-defense minister, **Rustem Umerov**, in terms that anti-corruption investigators read as those of an owner discussing his asset, not a rejected suitor. Fire Point calls this a smear campaign by jealous competitors. It may also simply be the going register of Ukrainian defense procurement as we approach year 13 of the war.

There is a third thread, pettier but not irrelevant: an aviation engineer testifying before the same commission called Fire Point’s claimed capacity — thousands of ballistic missiles a year — a technical absurdity, the kind of number that exists to secure the next contract rather than to describe an assembly line. And the Anti-Corruption Center (anTAC) has spent the spring pressing Shtilerman on an old Russian court filing under a different surname, seeking to restore a citizenship he says he no longer holds. None of this makes the Freyja consortium a bad idea. Distributed production, IP escrow, independent audit — the architecture Shtilerman describes in the Post is the correct answer to the Patriot shortage, and Europe is right to build it.

But the op-ed asks readers to trust the diagnosis of monopoly dependency from a man whose own company is the case study, and to admire the audit provisions of Freyja from a founder whose own books have become the subject of a parliamentary commission. Ukraine did not build a fast, adaptive defense industry by accident, as he says. It built one fast enough that nobody had time to ask who owned it until the money was already spent.

### Addendum: the domino, not the design

Rustem Umerov appears in this story twice — as the defense minister Mindich allegedly courted on those recordings, and, as of this week, as the man Z has just installed atop foreign intelligence, where his stated brief includes closing drone deals. It’s worth asking how he got there, Spoiler: not by selection, but by elimination.

Trace it forward. Umerov left Defense, so Denys Shmyhal — the former prime minister, whose own removal from that post was never explained — was installed in his place. Shmyhal then had to be pulled out again to cover a hole at the Energy Ministry, so Mykhailo Fedorov, until then the digitalization minister and a Z loyalist of long standing, moved into Defense by default. When Fedorov’s arrangement with the Presidential Office or the military command stopped working, Interior Minister Ihor Klymenko was floated to replace him — until public reaction made that untenable, and Klymenko was rerouted instead to the now-vacant secretaryship of the security council. That left Umerov holding no portfolio at all, which is how a man just removed from one security post arrives at the top of foreign intelligence. Every step in the chain was forced by the step before it. None of it was chosen for the job it produced.

This is the same failure mode Shtilerman’s op-ed diagnoses in defense procurement, one level up. **A system with no bench, run by one point of trust, will keep producing exactly the kind of concentrated, unaccountable dependency he says Ukraine’s drone industry has learned to avoid.** It hasn’t avoided it. It has simply relocated it — from a single company to a single man, currently sitting in the chair from which Fire Point’s own patron once tried to buy his way into ownership.
