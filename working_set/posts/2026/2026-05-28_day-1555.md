The “ia” diphthong rendering is the tell. [ISW](https://understandingwar.org/research/russia-ukraine/russian-offensive-campaign-assessment-may-27-2026/) is using “Kosty-” which slides toward a softer, Russified-adjacent transliteration, when the Ukrainian standard calls for “Kostia” with that distinct “ia” break. Same goes for Zapsillya (sic) and Myropillya (sic), small flattened mistransliterated Ukrainian towns in northern Sumy region.

No one notices this. It is the kind of detail that only matters if you are looking directly at the mechanism. But it is the mechanism: the habit of rendering Ukrainian through a Russified filter, the unconscious drift toward Moscow’s phonetic gravity, the way language itself gets colonized before analysis even begins.

Late choice starts here. In the small decisions that accumulate. In the transliterations that slide. In the descriptors applied without examination. By the time you notice the pattern, the machinery has already been running for years.

[The Economist](https://www.economist.com/europe/2026/05/26/ukraines-latest-challenge-is-how-to-deal-with-hope) publishes on May 26 with the same information we have been circulating for years. Ukraine is winning militarily. Ukraine is losing institutionally. The paradox is real. The outcome is unknowable. All accurate. All incomplete.

Here is what separates their analysis from understanding: they see the problem as corruption within a democratic framework, when the framework itself is not democratic. They prescribe reform to institutions that were never institutions in the first place.

At one point, they describe the system around Z as “Byzantine.” This is revealing in exactly the same way as the transliteration errors are revealing.

Byzantine and feudal are not interchangeable words. Byzantine describes a style of governance: opaque court politics, intrigue, informal influence networks, decisions made through whispers and rivalries rather than transparent procedure. Feudal describes a structure of governance: vertical patronage relationships, extraction flowing upward, protection and resources flowing downward, loyalty maintained through dependency.

One is atmospheric. The other is architectural.

Calling the system Byzantine keeps the analysis at the level of personality and leadership style. It implies complexity, intrigue, perhaps excessive centralization, but ultimately something reformable. Calling it feudal names the underlying structure itself. And once you name the structure, a different set of conclusions follows.

The transliteration error performs the same operation linguistically. “Kosty-” instead of “Kostia.” Byzantine instead of feudal. In both cases, the descriptor quietly shifts the center of gravity eastward toward inherited imperial frameworks while preserving the illusion that nothing important has changed.

They write: “Ukraine will probably emerge as a damaged but functioning democracy and a new middle power: poorer, traumatised, but confident in its identity.”

This assumes that institutions can be reformed under wartime stress. It assumes that Yermak’s arrest signals accountability rather than reorganization. It assumes that the system contains within itself the seeds of its own democratization.

None of this is true.

The real problem is not with people trying to understand from inside the situation. It is conveying what is actually happening, or are compartmentalizing the information for future use, being oblivious to the feudal logic staring them in the face.

This is a familiar dynamic. A month ago we wrote about conversations that don’t happen—about analysts and historians who understand patterns clearly elsewhere but won’t apply them to Ukraine because naming those patterns late means admitting they chose not to see them, or chose not to say them. The same logic applies here. The pattern is visible. The cost of naming it is admitting late choice. So the naming doesn’t happen.

There’s nothing quite like having a polemic with oneself across a four year’s worth of dispatches. But the consistency is instructive.

This is the occupational hazard of liberal institutional analysis: it cannot conceive of a system that is fundamentally not liberal, so it treats every feudal mechanism as a temporary deviation that will eventually self-correct.

It will not.

There is, however, something new in the landscape that the Economist touches on without quite grasping its implications. Ukraine has developed asymmetric warfare innovations that the Pentagon now desperately wants. Drone technology. Dirt-cheap homemade interceptors. Tactics that make expensive military platforms obsolete. Z, having been told by Trump he had no cards, now holds a deck that matters strategically.

This does not reform the feudal system. But it changes the West’s tolerance for it.

Europe will back Ukraine qua state because Ukrainian sovereignty matters strategically. The Pentagon will back Ukraine qua innovation because drone warfare has become essential to American military doctrine. Neither will back Z qua democrat, because he is not one and never will be.

But that distinction—between backing the state and backing the person—means the feudal system can persist longer than its internal contradictions would normally allow. The street may remain quiescent because the West keeps funding the machinery. The oligarchs may maintain stability because external support buys time. The apparatus may reorganize smoothly enough that fracture can be deferred.

Not forever. But long enough.

There is, however, a pressure building on the other side that complicates this stability. Russia is losing nearly 35,000 soldiers monthly, more than the Kremlin can recruit. Continuing the war at its current pace will soon become untenable without forced mobilization—something Putin fears because it signals weakness and damaged his popularity the last time. So he faces a trap: admit defeat, mobilize and risk internal collapse, or escalate horizontally. Test NATO cohesion in the Baltics while Trump undermines European deterrence and far-right parties demand an end to Ukraine aid.

As one EU official noted, this is “a very dangerous point.” Putin will have to escalate in order to justify mobilization.

The signals are already visible. Russia has launched massive strikes on Kyiv, threatened “sustained” bombardment, and warned diplomats to leave. [But as the NYT reports](https://www.nytimes.com/2026/05/27/world/europe/russia-ukraine-kyiv-attacks.html), analysts believe the escalation rhetoric is partly directed at a domestic audience—Russia must show that its response is “not symbolic, but tough and systematic.” More importantly, Moscow is signaling to Washington: you cannot cool toward Ukraine without consequence. The Kremlin is effectively demanding that Trump re-engage in mediating a settlement, or face the possibility of further escalation, potentially including tactical nuclear weapons.

The stalemate may not hold. The feudal system may persist, but the arithmetic may force Putin’s hand toward expansion anyway.

Here is the cost of late choice: by the time the pattern becomes undeniable—by the analysis catches up to reality—the machinery has already calcified. The West has already decided to prop up the feudal system because it produces useful weapons. The oligarchs have already reorganized. The decisions that should have been made when the pattern was still deniable can no longer be made at all.

You can document everything. You can know the system inside out. But if you name it late, you name it when naming no longer changes anything.

That is the trap. And we are already in it.
