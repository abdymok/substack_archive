[Day 1570](https://abdymok.substack.com/p/day-1570?utm_source=publication-search) ran a paragraph twice this summer, which is either a Freudian slip about the repetitiveness of the war or a Ctrl-V accident. I’ve now deleted the extra one. The passage worth keeping was this:

> Fifteen minutes ago, three heavily tattooed men in khaki sat outside a café opposite the Radisson Blu on Yaroslaviv Val beneath an awning that read, with touching optimism, “Любов.” They were lounging with the particular stillness of people who have learned, professionally, not to fidget. They may have been discussing drone procurement. They may have been discussing the Knicks comeback in Game 4. The point is that one can no longer tell. The clientele of [Tootsie Club](https://en.tootsie.kiev.ua/) has matured.

I bring it back up because the men have not gone anywhere. There are more of them now, and some of them are not from here.

No one enjoys being surrounded by large tattooed men with tactical earpieces wandering the center in twos and threes, radiating the specific patience of people paid to wait. What sharpens the discomfort is the internationalism of it — the accents, the different flags sewn on differently, the same posture underneath all of them, the same habit of standing with their backs to the wall.

One does not ask what army. One asks nothing.

It has the texture, if you were unlucky enough to be in Belgrade in the autumn of 1998, of Belgrade in the autumn of 1998.

I had, for reasons that will become clear, Tool’s *Invincible* in one ear while I watched them — a song about an old warrior calling out to whatever gods remain, insisting he is still consequential, still dangerous, while the armor thins and the body keeps score.

It is not subtle. It did not need to be, watching men who have aged out of one war try to look useful loitering near the entrance to another. The song and the corner agreed with each other in a way that felt less like coincidence than like the city supplying its own soundtrack, which Kyiv has always been generous about doing, usually at the worst possible moments.

Middle-aged men in half-tac gear, carrying black leather shoulder pouches — retransmitters, radios, something with an antenna, I didn’t ask — stand on corners in twos, smoking with the economy of people who have been told to conserve batteries or nerves. They speak quietly. They do not look at each other while they speak, which is either professional discipline or the thing that happens to people who have run out of things to say to each other and said them anyway.

This is not the pleasant kind of cold — not the pocket of lake water you swim into on a late-August afternoon after a thunderstorm has broken the heat, the kind that makes you gasp and then grateful.

This is the other kind. The kind with no bottom you can feel for.

I don’t know what they’re waiting for. I suspect they don’t entirely know either, which is worse, because a man who knows what he’s waiting for eventually stops smoking on corners and goes and does it.

These men have the look of men still waiting to be told.
