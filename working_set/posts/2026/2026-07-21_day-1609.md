When Russia invaded Ukraine in 2022, almost everyone thought they already knew how the story would end. Ukraine was expected to lose before anyone had time to become tired of reading about it. That did not happen.

Instead, something stranger happened. The front moved less than people expected. The missiles moved more.

Russia began the war convinced that only Ukrainian cities would live under this particular weather. Moscow was somewhere missiles departed from, not somewhere they arrived. In those first months Ukraine possessed almost nothing capable of reaching deep into Russia. There were the Neptune missiles, intended for ships rather than cities. One of them found the *Moskva*. Otherwise, Ukraine watched while Russian missiles crossed the sky in the opposite direction.

The arrangement seemed permanent. Nothing ages faster than a permanent arrangement.

For months now Ukrainian drones have been appearing over Moscow and St. Petersburg. People gradually became accustomed to them, which is one of humanity’s more unsettling talents. Ballistic missiles are harder to become accustomed to.

Last week Ukraine’s [outgoing defense minister said the country had successfully tested one](https://euromaidanpress.com/2026/07/16/symbolically-says-ukraines-departing-defense-minister-new-ballistic-missile-was-tested-on-day-government-resigned/). Two weeks earlier, [Russian sources quietly reported intercepting](https://defence-blog.com/russia-confirms-intercept-of-new-ukrainian-ballistic-missile/) what they described as a Ukrainian tactical ballistic missile over Moscow Oblast.

If the reports describe the same event, Moscow has entered a different chapter of the war.

## An old comparison

People have begun comparing it with the Iran-Iraq War. The comparison is not exact. History rarely bothers with exact copies. It prefers family resemblance. During the 1980s neither Iran nor Iraq could defeat the other, so they began shooting missiles at each other’s cities instead. The newspapers eventually gave it a simple name: the War of the Cities. Simple names usually belong to complicated tragedies.

Readers with improbably good memories may recall that we wandered into the same neighborhood three years ago, in [“2023 Military Campaign Prospects.”](https://abdymok.substack.com/p/2023-military-campaign-propects) The argument then was less about missiles than about geometry. The authors identify Russia’s center of gravity: its monopoly on long-range strike. Not territory. Not manpower. Not Crimea. Long-range strike.

Three years later, the front has moved less than many expected. The mental map has moved rather farther.

## Three roads

Ukraine now appears to be approaching the same destination by three different roads.

**The first road belongs to the state.** The old Sapsan program, abandoned and revived often enough to resemble an orchard rather than a weapons project, appears to be alive again. Much of what is known comes from Defense_SCM’s *[The War Russia Fears](https://defensescm.substack.com/p/the-war-russia-fears)*. It is an unusual essay — rich in detail, poor in footnotes. Some of its claims can be checked independently, others cannot.

**The second road belongs to private industry, propped up by the state.** Fire Point first became known for drones. Now it speaks increasingly about ballistic and antiballistic missiles. [Joshua Yaffa, writing in The New Yorker](https://nationalsecurityjournal.org/ukraines-most-revered-general-has-a-warning-about-the-deep-strike-campaign-russia-can-strike-back-with-equal-or-greater-force/), watches the consequences. Oil depots burn. Airfields fall silent. Fear migrates eastward. *(Links to coverage of the July 15, 2026 New Yorker piece; the New Yorker article itself couldn’t be fetched directly.)*

[Simon Shuster, in The Atlantic](https://www.theatlantic.com/national-security/2026/07/ukraine-denys-shtilerman-drone-missile/687931/), walks through the workshop. He returns smelling faintly of epoxy and carbon fiber while his host points from drones to cruise missiles to whatever comes next.

Neither man is wrong. They are simply standing in different places.

[Z promised three thousand missiles and missile-drones in a year](https://kyivindependent.com/zelensky-missiles-drones/). A factory owner speaks of hundreds of drones every ten days and a handful of missiles each afternoon. The numbers refuse to agree. Numbers often behave like relatives. They share a family name while declining to share an inheritance.

Perhaps there are factories nobody has mentioned. Perhaps “missile” has acquired cousins. Perhaps wartime arithmetic follows rules unknown to accountants. Factories, however, are stubborn creatures. They eventually settle every argument.

**The third road** runs through Britain, which is working with Ukraine on a longer-range missile expected to enter production over the next several years.

## The direction of travel

Three roads. Different travelers. The same destination.

The direction of travel has changed. Russia invaded believing that distance was one of its allies. Distance has an awkward habit. Once technology notices it, it begins to disappear.

Fire Point is plainly building things. Russian oil depots testify to that often enough. Whether it will someday build ballistic missiles in the quantities people now imagine remains an open question. Reality has the irritating habit of publishing its own corrections. Factories answer such questions more honestly than interviews.

Russia entered this war believing missiles traveled in one direction. Technology, unfortunately for Russia, has never been especially sentimental about permanent arrangements.

History rarely invents new jokes. It prefers to tell the old ones to someone else.
