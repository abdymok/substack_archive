Sunday night into Monday morning. A Russian drone hit a private house in Zaporizhia. A woman died. Three people were injured, including an 11-year-old boy who is under medical supervision. Ivan Fedorov, head of the regional military administration, posted a photograph of the house. The photograph exists. The house is on fire in it.

[Image]

In the Black Sea, a Russian UAV struck the cargo vessel *Victress*, flying the flag of Panama, and set it on fire. Nine crew members — citizens of Egypt, Turkey, and India — were aboard. Ukrainian Navy boats conducted a rescue operation and evacuated the crew. One did not make it: a 58-year-old Egyptian cook. Eight sailors went into the water on a life raft. The vessel lost its seaworthiness. Deputy Prime Minister Kuleba called it a war crime and said it required a decisive response from the international community, which is the kind of sentence that has been said, in various forms, for 1,580 days.

Two other vessels were also struck overnight — one flying the flag of Palau, one flying the flag of Belize. Preliminary data showed no casualties. Both continued moving with damage. This detail, the vessels continuing to move with damage, is doing more work than it appears to.

Also Sunday evening: an Iskander ballistic missile hit an agricultural enterprise in Odesa district. One person killed, three injured. Vehicles, fuel tanks, a warehouse. A fire.

On Sunday, Z told 1+1 TV that Ukrainian drones had struck oil refineries in Tyumen — 2,070 kilometers in a straight line, 2,500 by route. The next generation of drones will reach 3,000 kilometers and beyond. “We understand where all their military factories are, where their oil bases, gas storage facilities are,” he said. “We need tools for a longer distance for a longer time.”

Tyumen is in western Siberia. It is not a place that, on February 24, 2022, most people would have included in a map of this war.

### The map keeps getting larger

The archive contains 1,702 posts. Of these, 1,423 were published and 279 abandoned — drafted, reconsidered, and left in the condition of thoughts a man decides not to finish in front of witnesses. The published pieces total roughly 706,000 words across twelve years: about the combined length of *Journey to the End of the Night*, *The Unbearable Lightness of Being*, *Humboldt’s Gift*, and *Sometimes a Great Notion* — a novel about war, one about occupation, one about a poet destroyed by America, and one about people who refuse to quit when quitting is the only rational choice. As comparisons go, it is more honest than *War and Peace*.

Of those 706,000 words, 492,000 were written after February 24, 2022. Such is the arithmetic of full-scale invasion: eight years of intermittent observation compressed into a daily obligation. Between 2014 and 2021 there were 260 posts; from 2022 to mid-2026, 1,044. Nineteen exceed 2,000 words. Seventy-four contain fewer than 100. The longest runs to 5,787 words and concerns Vladimir Putin, Karl Popper, and the Russian Orthodox Church, which explains, without further comment, why 74 others are so short.

The subject is a country that, over twelve years, has been called a post-Soviet democracy, a failed state, a frontline civilization, a laboratory for Western defense technology, a feudal autocracy sustained by popular uprisings, and a living argument against the idea that history moves in one direction. Only one of these descriptions appears in the archive. The others belong to people writing at a comfortable distance, which is itself a useful fact. The archive disagrees with all of them on most days, without raising its voice.

This is not only a book about Ukraine. It is also about the problem of writing about Ukraine, which turns out to be, in large part, a problem of tools.

Long before the archive began, English-language commentary on this corner of the world suffered from two persistent ailments. The first was the conviction that every catastrophe somehow confirmed the perfection of the market, producing a steady supply of books praising economic self-surgery while cities were busy being shelled. The second was the habit of dressing up the latest internet gold rush in manifestos, slogans, and revolutionary iconography and calling it analysis. Neither inspired much editorial skepticism.

Writing around both required precision, which required decent instruments. A decade ago the author spent an unreasonable amount of time complaining that software for producing clean prose behaved like farm machinery repaired with string. Switching between formatted text and markup was harder than it ought to have been; formatting conversations resembled untangling barbed wire; every paragraph had to be inspected in a browser to make sure it had not quietly become ridiculous. As content management systems multiplied, writers did more podcasting and less page construction, which solved nothing.

Artificial intelligence has since relieved some of the drudgery. Flagging text is easier. The ideal application — one that moves effortlessly between elegant prose and structured markup without making the writer feel like a plumber — remains somewhere over the horizon. Still, the tools are better than they were.

The archive is partly evidence of that: 706,000 words produced from an apartment in Kyiv about a country most of the tools’ designers had scarcely considered. Whether the words justified the tools is another matter. That, after all, is what archives are for.

Graphic design is, in part, the construction of navigation systems: imposing visual structure on meaning, establishing hierarchies, placing elements in context — saying, look at this for a while, then look at that. Yet a great many professionals approach design as a switch with which to turn on experience, or complete the accessories of lifestyle. The distinction matters more than it sounds.

A long time ago, it occurred to me that the first year of a communication design education should not replicate kindergarten — now you are a tree, blowing in the wind — but should instead focus on nothing but maps. For a year, students would be dropped into utterly alien environments: Luhansk, say, or Millwood, New York, armed with whatever maps were available. The exercise would be to gauge how long it takes to get oriented using these baffling, arbitrary wayfinding systems, each one more chaotic than the last — and then, having survived that, go be a designer.

This project is, among other things, a map. Ukraine is among the more alien environments available to the Western reader: a country that has been mislocated, mislabeled, and misread for thirty years by people who were holding the wrong chart. What follows is one person’s attempt at orientation, conducted in real time, from inside the territory, over twelve years. The maps are imperfect. The environment kept changing. Neither of these things is unusual.

There is also a case to be made — not here, but somewhere — for reviving the pulp fiction tradition of putting maps on the back cover, so that a reader lost in a labyrinthine plot could simply turn the book over and find out where they were. The argument for it applies, with some force, to nonfiction about Eastern Europe.
