The problem:

[Video: https://youtu.be/OugO7FvABpk]

The last piece I published this week began with a surgeon and ended with a general in Russia. In between, I tried to trace what happens when the instrument of knowledge loses contact with the thing it claims to know — and what it costs when no one in the room notices, or cares.

Borges and Kundera arrived in that piece not as decoration but as diagnosis. They are part of the same tradition — Central European and Latin American literary thought — that took epistemological humility as its central moral commitment. The idea that the gap between what we know and what is actually there is not a technical problem to be solved but a condition to be honored. Both wrote from positions of political precarity. Both understood, from direct experience, what happens when power decides it has closed that gap.

The filiation between them is not often traced explicitly, but it runs deep. Borges came first. His fable of the map that becomes the territory appeared in 1946, in a story barely longer than a paragraph. The empire that builds the perfect map is not made wiser by it. It is made helpless. The map rots in the desert alongside the territory it claimed to describe.

[Image]

Kundera, writing three decades later from the ruins of the Prague Spring, was working a related seam. His great theme — lightness versus weight, the irreversibility of the real against the consoling fiction that things might have been otherwise — is in some sense an extension of the Borgesian problem into lived political history. For Borges, the danger was the map that replaced the territory. For Kundera, the danger was the ideological certainty that replaced the ambiguous, irreducible particular — the individual life, the specific body, the child in the dialysis ward.

Kundera had another word for it: kitsch. **The aesthetic of certainty.** The refusal of ambiguity. The brass band that plays over the graves. Kitsch, he wrote, is the absolute denial of shit — by which he meant the irreducible remainder, the thing that does not fit the picture, the dialysis machine in the rubble, the sports field that appeared on the satellite image too late. The briefing room is a kitsch environment. It is designed to be. The maps are clean, the arrows are decisive, the adjectives are superlative. Most-lethal. Most-precise. Picture perfect.

I should have included Vasyl Bykov in the piece, and I didn’t. I knew him in Belarus — the last of the great Soviet-era writers who refused the Soviet consolation, who kept returning to the particular weight of the particular man in the particular frozen field. Bykov’s soldiers do not die for abstractions. They die in the mud, in the dark, having made a wrong turn or trusted the wrong person or simply run out of time. *Sign of Misfortune* (1982) and *To Live Till Sunrise* (1973) are not war novels in the heroic sense. They are novels about the catastrophic distance between the map — the order, the objective, the ideological justification — and the territory, which is cold and does not care about justifications.

Bykov belongs in the same line of filiation as Borges and Kundera, though the three never occupied the same literary conversation and would have made awkward dinner companions. What they share is the insistence that the irreducible particular — the body, the face, the specific moment of dying — cannot be abstracted away without moral catastrophe. For Borges the catastrophe is epistemological. For Kundera it is political. For Bykov it is military, and the mud is real, and he knew because he was in it, along with Valentyn Taras, [whose recollections of the underground](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=sgfy9lans1s), documented by Alexander rounded out my understanding of the mess. Alexander, as I recall, [got the boot right](https://www.hrw.org/legacy/russian/reports/belarus/1997/august/topic2a.html) after me.

Had my favorite philosopher Daniel Dennett still been alive, I would have brought him in as well. His framework of physical, design, and intentional stances maps cleanly onto the problem. A targeting system takes the intentional stance toward a building: it treats the structure as having a function, a purpose, a place in a military design. That stance is what makes it targetable. What it cannot do, by definition, is take the physical stance toward the children inside — to see them as particular bodies, with particular weights, in particular seats, on a particular Saturday morning. The targeting system is not evil. It is something more dangerous: correct within its own framework and blind outside it.

I thought about all of this talking to an old friend from Minsk this week — Sergei Grits, the AP photographer, who materialized in Kyiv recently. We talked about the briefings, about the satellite imagery, about picture-perfect strikes. He told me about Basrah in 2003, shooting on streets so hot the air bent. His local fixer kept fifty meters of distance from him at all times. Not because he didn’t trust Sergei. Because he understood, with a precision that no targeting system has yet replicated, that the man with the AP camera was a different kind of coordinate — one that attracted a different kind of attention — and that fifty meters might be the difference between the map and the territory, between the picture and the thing.

That fixer was doing applied epistemology in real time. He understood, with the clarity that comes only from skin in the game, the difference between the picture and the thing.

Between the picture and the thing: a bullet between his eyes.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

Which brings me to Russia’s strikes on Ukrainian cities, and the difference — important, clarifying, and finally irrelevant to the dead — between the American precision I wrote about this week and what I have watched from Kyiv’s streets.

The American strikes on Minab were, in the technical sense, precise. The school was hit because the instrument said it was a target. The instrument was wrong, but the aim was accurate. That is the Borgesian pathology: the map was perfect; the territory had moved on.

Russia’s strikes on Ukrainian cities are not precise. They are deliberately, programmatically, almost aesthetically imprecise. I know this because I have watched them from what locals call the mystic triangle — a stretch of Kyiv that has absorbed more than its share of incoming, for reasons that remain, to put it generously, unclear. The Kh-101 that hit Okhmatdyt did not go astray. The Shahed drones that circle in the dark over residential blocks are not malfunctioning. The pattern, observed over months and years, is not the pattern of a military force trying to hit military targets and occasionally missing. It is the pattern of a force that has decided the civilian is the target, and is working outward from there.

This is the inversion of the American problem. Hegseth’s instrument is too confident in an outdated picture. Kuvaldin’s instrument knows exactly what it is looking at and strikes anyway.

Bykov understood both failure modes. His officers are sometimes incompetent and sometimes malicious, and the men under them die either way. What he never allowed his readers was the comfort of clean categories — this death was a mistake, that one was a crime. The mud does not organize itself into legal distinctions. The mothers of Minab and the mothers of Kyiv are not waiting for the relevant jurisdiction to reach a finding.

Vasyl Bykov died in June 2003 — the same year Sergei Grits’s fixer was keeping his careful fifty meters on the streets of Basrah.

The mud, as always, did not care about the timing.

I subjected myself to considerable C-SPAN, Fox News, and Trey Yingst this week in the service of understanding the current grammar of American military jingoism. It is a specific dialect — confident, present-tense, passive when convenient. Mistakes are investigated. Responsibility is pending. The throttle is always going up.

Next week I will turn back to Ukraine’s ruling elites, who have their own complicated relationship with the gap between the map and the territory — less bellicose than Tampa, more Byzantine, and in some ways more dispiriting for being so close to home.
