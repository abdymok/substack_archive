We’re on day four of an AI-assisted experiment to rewire how I run — specifically, to bring the glutes back online, reduce the over-reliance on quads, and find what has been sitting there unused. Not more training, not more force. Just a small shift: push behind, stop reaching in front. The glutes wake up, the stride opens, more space, more flow. Same body, working differently.

And it is not comfortable. It is, in fact, surprisingly painful. Not because it is harder in the muscular sense, but because it requires attention. You have to unlearn what your body has been quietly doing for years and ask it to do something more honest.

I think ageing can feel like this. Not only losing things, but discovering that many of them were never gone — just misfiled. Strength that was still there, but no longer accessed. Movement that still exists, but needs to be reintroduced, carefully, patiently. The work is not to go back. It is to reconnect. To adjust small details. And in doing so, to find that “same body, working differently” is not a loss of identity, but a form of progress.

Not everyone, of course, is interested in reconnecting. Some prefer the soreness to simply go unnoticed — the kind that makes contradiction feel normal because it persists long enough. The kind that stops noticing when the lines connect.

Let us notice, then. Briefly. With whatever alertness remains.

Pete Hegseth stood before cameras on Easter Sunday and noted, with evident satisfaction, that the rescued airman’s plane had been shot down on a Friday, that he had hidden through Saturday in a cave, and had been flown home as the sun rose on Sunday. A pilot reborn. God is good. Tattooed on Mr. Hegseth’s bicep is *Deus vult* — “God wills it” — the battle cry of the Crusades[1](#footnote-1). He is now directing a bombing campaign against a majority-Shiite nation. The Pope has called this use of Christianity to justify warfare “entirely foreign to the way of Jesus Christ.” Mr. Hegseth has not appeared troubled by the disagreement.

The war itself offers no good exits. Destroy Iran’s energy infrastructure, and Iran destroys the Gulf’s — global recession as party favor. Walk away without a deal, and Iran becomes king of the strait, extracting tribute from passing vessels. Launch a ground operation, and as empires periodically rediscover, occupying territory is not the same as solving a problem. Each path leads, with differing elegance, to the same destination: economic damage, political ruin, and the particular humiliation of a man who believed he was winning.

Meanwhile, J.D. Vance has flown to Budapest to campaign for Viktor Orbán six days before the Hungarian election. The same Russia that arms Iran and shields it diplomatically is the country whose preferred candidate Mr. Vance has traveled to endorse in person. Hungary’s foreign minister, recorded telling Sergey Lavrov “I am always at your disposal,” has the manner of a man comfortable with his geometry. The billboards in Budapest show the opposition candidate beside a shadowy Z: *They are dangerous. Let’s stop them.* In one circulating image, Hungarian police wear Cyrillic insignia. The correction will arrive eventually.

I remember Budapest in the 1990s. Dilapidated restaurants and very good steak tartare. Lounge bars and hours-long conversations about open society and what it might become. Serbian refugees. City squares later reconstructed with Gazprom funds. Running laps around Margaret Island, then the Palatinus Baths, hot summers — and once, in a thunderstorm, a potted plant falling from a balcony high overhead, missing my head by not very much.

The people had a direction. Imperfect, uneven, occasionally dangerous in the literal sense. You could feel it in the conversations, in the scaffolding.

That feeling is gone. Not because history reversed, but because the people entrusted with its direction found, to their evident relief, that cynicism required so much less of them.

The lines connect. They have been connecting for some time. The soreness that stops you seeing it is not wisdom, not experience, not the hard-won equanimity of someone who has watched the world long enough to know its habits.

It is simply surrender, dressed for dinner.
