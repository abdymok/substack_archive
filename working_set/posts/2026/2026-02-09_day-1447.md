So Kyiv celebrated yesterday. Prime Minister Shmyhal convened the Emergency Situation Headquarters for Energy and [announced](https://t.me/Denys_Smyhal/12279) the capital would receive an *extra* nine megawatts of power.

Nine.

At current wholesale rates, we’re talking about €765 per hour. For a capital city of three million people.

But wait, there’s more: Ukraine has also received 27,000 tons of humanitarian aid since the invasion began. Of which 25,100 tons has already been distributed to the regions. In just the last two weeks, 17 humanitarian shipments arrived from 11 partners. Including 774 generators. And 40 units of modular boiler rooms, cogeneration plants, boilers, and other equipment.

Victory these days looks like press releases itemizing donated generators while announcing additional electrical capacity worth less than a Tesla Model 3 per hour.

**On Social Cohesion**

What happens when the people in charge of defending the motherland are too busy dividing kickbacks “by three” to actually defend anything? Frostbite. You get a society that needs both external enemies *and* internal enforcers to remain intact.

**On Corruption as Strategy**

Those nine megawatts arrive courtesy of an energy infrastructure systematically pillaged by the heroic leaders in charge of defending it. The resignations last November of top bureaucrats—caught on tape dividing kickbacks in the Energoatom scandal—came just in time for winter.

The alleged ringleader? Z and his top lieutenants.

Two years ago, Z replaced the commander-in-chief and sacked a bunch of generals. It’s been downhill ever since—though the trajectory was already set by defense procurement and fall-back fortification schemes that treated Russia’s invasion as primarily a business opportunity.

When your energy-turned-justice minister is codenamed “Professor” on wiretaps discussing how to split the take, you’re running a very particular kind of wartime government.

Trump opened the door, Putin walked through it, and Ukraine’s leadership spent the intervening years ensuring the house was already half-looted before the demolition began.

**On Narrative as Strategy**

*“Narrative is strategy in story form.”* —Peter Pomerantsev, at 2,600 words. [TLDR](https://archive.ph/5GN8i).

Or so we’re told, at exhaustive length, by someone who has confused word count with insight.

For Ukraine’s most prominent expat champions who have built careers on narrative warfare, acknowledging Ukrainian malfeasance would complicate the story.

Better to write thousands of words about “medium-sized democracies” and “predator states” than to mention Z and his closest associates allegedly running wartime corruption schemes.

Better to theorize about “narrative escalation dominance” than to note that replacing your most competent military commander mid-war for reasons of ego might have strategic consequences.

The problem with treating narrative as strategy: eventually someone has to keep the lights on.

When your actual strategy has been to loot the energy infrastructure while opining about about resilience, you end up celebrating €765 worth of electricity as if it were the Marshall Plan.

**On Historical Memory**

There will be a reckoning. The residue left in Europe’s historical memory by the systematic destruction of civilian infrastructure will not wash away.

Sooner or later, a price will have to be paid—either by those responsible or by their children. Perhaps the children will ask their fathers: what was that about back then?

But Ukraine, freezing in the dark, counting its nine megawatts and 774 generators, cannot wait for history’s judgment.

It can only endure the present—a present where Trump’s strength delivered the conditions for civilizational collapse, one demolished power plant at a time, while Z’s inner circle divided the proceeds by three.

**On Presidential Messaging**

Z’s Sunday evening address: a masterclass in saying nothing while appearing to say something.

*“Air defense missiles are needed every day.”* That’s what air defense means.

*“The coming weeks will be active in working with our partners. As always, results are needed. There must be results.”* Three sentences to say: we will do things and hope they work.

*“Sunday was almost entirely devoted to restoration in the regions.”* Almost entirely. One wonders what the other portion was devoted to.

Best of all: the statistics. More than 200 heating points in Kyiv! Almost 9,000 people applied! Almost 40,000 heat packages issued!
