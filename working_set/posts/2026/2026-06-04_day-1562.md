Last week Edward Luce, writing in the [Financial Times](https://archive.ph/7tMnY), offered a diagnosis: both Putin and Trump gambled on weak adversaries, assumed quick victories, and have instead produced case studies in geopolitical self-harm. This week Jack Watling, in *[Foreign Affairs](https://www.foreignaffairs.com/ukraine/ukraine-turns-tide-cease-fire-jack-watling)*, adds the granular military argument — Russian combat performance is waning, Ukrainian tactical proficiency is quietly improving, and a cease-fire has moved from fantasy to realistic possibility.

The arguments are reasonable. They also are incomplete.

Its hidden assumption is that people eventually submit to reality. Reality arrives, presents its credentials, and is admitted indoors. The mistake lies in believing this always happens.

Consider Iran.

Ayatollah Mojtaba Khamenei is fifty-six years old. He did not inherit an ordinary state. He inherited an idea disguised as a state. The Islamic Republic is often described as a government with a foreign policy. In truth it resembles a foreign policy that acquired a government for administrative convenience.

Its central proposition is simple: America is the Great Satan, and accommodation is apostasy.

A man can retreat from a battlefield. He can retreat from a negotiating position. He can even retreat from a promise. But how does he retreat from the premise that gave meaning to his life?

If Trump’s terms are accepted — whatever those terms may be — something larger than a policy has failed. The foundation itself begins to crack.

Putin’s predicament differs in language but not in structure.

For more than twenty years he has told Russians — and, one suspects, himself — that Ukraine is not truly a nation, that its sovereignty is a historical misunderstanding, and that history itself was waiting patiently for correction. When a man spends enough years repeating a story, the story ceases to be propaganda. It becomes furniture. He begins bumping into it in the dark.

This is why it is not quite right to say Putin miscalculated the war. He miscalculated history. A military error can be corrected. A mistaken view of history is harder to amend because it has usually become part of the person holding it.

Z and Trump, both wreckers, are trapped as well, but theirs are more ordinary traps.

Their constraints depend on audiences, elections, approval ratings, headlines, donors, allies, and opponents. These are powerful forces, but they belong to circumstances. Circumstances change. A politician can discover a new argument, a new coalition, a new justification. Ego is remarkably flexible when survival requires flexibility.

The traps of Putin and Khamenei are different. Not external. Internal.

Ideology is not a prison wall surrounding the self. It is part of the self.

To admit defeat is not merely to lose. It is to become someone else.

And here the two kinds of traps intersect.

Z cannot end the war by satisfying Putin because what Putin requires is not a concession but a confirmation: Ukraine must cease to be fully Ukraine. Trump cannot settle matters with Iran by satisfying Khamenei because what Khamenei requires is not an agreement but a world from which American power has withdrawn.

The result is a curious mechanism. The rigid trap on one side makes the flexible trap on the other permanent. The more room Z or Trump have to maneuver, the less useful maneuver becomes.

One side cannot concede enough. The other cannot compromise enough.

The locks face in opposite directions. No one has the key.

This is why the assumption of eventual rationality deserves scrutiny.

Observers ask how the Kremlin will react once it recognizes that its prospects are worsening. But recognition is not a mechanical process. Human beings do not absorb unwelcome truths the way thermometers absorb temperature.

A man who believes he is correcting a historical injustice does not easily conclude that the injustice should remain uncorrected. More often he concludes that one final effort is required. One more offensive. One more winter. One more missile salvo.

Reality may indeed be winning its argument.

The question is whether the men losing that argument can afford to hear it.
