Emergency workers are still pulling dead bodies out from the rubble of the apartment building in Kyiv’s Darnytsky District. As of this writing, 24 dead and over 50 in the hospital. Mayor Klitschko has declared today a day of mourning.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

    Alex Karp was back in Kyiv this week, moving through the city with the curious ease of a man who knows he is both welcomed and distrusted. He met with Z, praised Ukrainian engineers, admired the improvisational genius of wartime coders, and offered Palantir’s software as a weapon in Ukraine’s survival kit. In his interview with UNITED24 Media, Karp sounded almost tender. Ukrainians smile at him on the street, he said. They understand what his software does.

    Of course they do.

    When Russian drones circle overhead at four in the morning, philosophical objections to predictive analytics lose their force. If software helps identify a Shahed drone faster, route an interceptor faster, clear a minefield faster, or spare a city block, people are not inclined to ask whether the algorithm has read Hannah Arendt.

    Ukraine, in this sense, has become Palantir’s strongest moral argument.

    And yet.

    Meanwhile, The New York Times published a long profile of Mykhailo Fedorov, Ukraine’s 35-year-old defense minister, presenting him as the visionary architect of a new kind of warfare—autonomous weapons, algorithmic killing, the “mathematics of war.” The article notes, almost in passing, that Fedorov has met with Karp and is “working with Palantir to further integrate A.I. into warfare.” This is the distribution mechanism. The ideology arrives not as manifesto but as profile. As innovation. As inevitability.

[Image]

*Karp and Fedorov (c/o United24, May 12, 2026)*

    Reading Karp’s 22-point manifesto is like overhearing a brilliant man mistake synthesis for wisdom. The document announces that “some cultures have produced vital advances; others remain dysfunctional and regressive.” It lunges from civilizational decline to technological destiny, from national service to cultural hierarchy. It reads as though Oswald Spengler, Ayn Rand, and a defense procurement officer were locked overnight in an airport lounge.

    The manifesto has been called “an irredeemable mess”—not as insult but as diagnosis. Palantir is no longer content being a contractor. It wants to be a philosopher-king in fleece. Its stock trades at a P/E ratio over 230. Trump praised it on Truth Social. The company has made a simple calculation: there is money in American authoritarianism, and alignment with it.

    That is where Ukraine complicates everyone’s moral geometry. It too is an irredeemable mess.

    Palantir’s critics in comfortable democracies have long described the company as a digital mercenary—embedded too deeply in ICE deportations, police surveillance, and the machinery of authoritarian governance. They are not wrong. Palantir’s software sits uncomfortably close to the mechanisms of state power. It routinely arrives wrapped in the language of efficiency and departs leaving behind questions about accountability the company has shown little interest in answering.

    But then there is Ukraine—that impolite fact.

    Ukraine did not summon Palantir because it was seduced by techno-messianism. Ukraine summoned everyone. Drone startups. Satellite companies. Hackers. Volunteers. Retired generals. Teenagers soldering components in garages. When a larger empire arrives with missiles, glide bombs, and mass graves, ideological purity becomes a luxury item.

    The Ukrainians, as Karp himself admitted, may be the real story. They took foreign systems, broke them apart, rebuilt them under fire, and created a battlefield feedback loop that traditional militaries envy. Palantir supplied software. Ukrainians supplied the thing no Western consultant can manufacture: existential urgency.

    This is the contradiction Karp cannot solve.

    He may be right that democracies need sharper tools. He may be disastrously wrong in believing the men who build those tools should also be trusted to write manifestos about civilization—or worse, to believe their own.

    Ukraine genuinely needs software, but this software comes packaged with an ideology about cultural hierarchy, and that's not accidental or separate from the technology itself.

    It's the point. And that's the problem.

    Just ask the techno messianists in baseball caps.
