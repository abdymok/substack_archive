**Which Stance Are They Taking Now?**

My favorite professional philosopher Daniel Dennett argued we adopt different “stances” to understand systems. The physical stance uses laws of nature. The design stance assumes functional purpose. The intentional stance treats systems as rational agents with beliefs and desires.

The intentional stance asks: What does this agent want? What does it believe? What will it rationally do next?

In evolutionary biology, there’s a parallel debate. Does life converge on similar solutions—eyes in arthropods, vertebrates, cephalopods—suggesting law-like patterns? Or is history radically contingent, dependent on accidents that could easily have gone differently? Stephen Jay Gould, Dennett’s co-panelist in [Wim Kayser](https://www.librarything.com/author/kayzerwim)’s 1993 documentary “[A Glorious Accident](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=RVrnn7QW6Jg),” argued for contingency. Rewind the tape, let it play again, you get something completely different.

On Day 1436 of Russia’s war against Ukraine, observers are taking their stances.

Western diplomats adopt the design stance. They see a negotiation designed to produce equilibrium. Rubio calls the situation “very fluid.” Trump asks Putin not to bomb us for one week. The design assumes: enough pressure → enough incentives → deal.

Some Russian emigré aspiring philosophers adopt a version of the physical stance. They observe historical patterns—Europe’s weakness, Russia’s survival wars, the spirit of Anchorage—and predict outcomes as if they follow physical laws. Europe has no agency. Ukraine will fold. The final episode was written before it began.

Both are treating us as systems to be predicted, not agents with beliefs, desires, and the capacity to intervene.

But here’s what Judea Pearl understood about causality that these observers don’t: Observation is not intervention[3](#footnote-3).

You can observe that negotiations correlate with reduced bombing. You can observe European statements correlating with inaction. You can observe historical patterns. But correlation isn’t causation. The intentional stance matters precisely when agents decide to do something different.

Almost thirteen years ago this February, hours before the massacre, politicians and diplomats agreed to a truce. The design stance predicted: negotiation → agreement → stability. The physical stance might have predicted: people disperse, regime consolidates.

But the people on Independence Square took the intentional stance toward themselves. They had beliefs about dignity. Desires for sovereignty. And they decided—rationally, given those beliefs and desires—to refuse the deal opposition leaders had just accepted.

*Ibidem*

There are many photos I took on the square at the exact moment this was happening[4](#footnote-4). I tried using AI to enhance some of them—to clarify what was actually visible through the fog of borrowed lenses and burning tires. ChatGPT refused: “We’re so sorry, but the image we created may violate our guardrails around violence.”

The AI won’t enhance the images because they show violence. But these *are* the images. This is what intervention looks like when you stop being observed and start acting. The guardrails aren’t there to protect us—they’re there to protect observers from having to see clearly what they’re watching.

The tape rewound itself that morning. Not because of physical laws or functional design, but because agents intervened—kids in helmets carrying hatchets, hunting rifles manning homemade mortars. And people taking their pictures.

[Image]

*Ibidem*

The algorithm hates gushing blood. It won’t clarify violence even when clarification is precisely what’s needed. It’s the perfect metaphor for how David, emigré fatalists and Trump’s negotiators approach Ukraine: observe from a safe distance, refuse to see clearly, impose guardrails that prevent understanding.

This is what they miss from their comfortable observation posts. They weren’t there then. They’ve adopted a stance that denies agency—theirs, ours, everyone’s. Europe has no “subjectivity” (their word). Ukraine lacks “internal guarantees” beyond the will to fight. Russia always wins survival wars. It’s all so predictable when you refuse to take the intentional stance toward the people you’re observing.

The irony: they’re right that Ukraine’s security depends on Ukrainians’ willingness to fight. They’re just wrong about what follows. It doesn’t mean inevitable defeat observed from afar. It means we’re the agents. We hold the beliefs. We have the desires. We make the decisions.

In “A Glorious Accident,” the final episode asked: “We wonder, ever wonder why we found us here?” Gould’s answer was contingency—a glorious accident of history. Dennett’s was the intentional stance—we’re here because we’re rational agents pursuing goals.

On Day 1436, we know why we’re here. Not as variables in someone else’s equation. Not as systems following physical laws or functional designs. But as agents who refuse the stances others are taking toward us.

Trump’s one-week “pause” isn’t convergence. Europe’s weakness isn’t physical law. Russia’s patterns aren’t destiny. These are observations from stances that deny our agency.

David still thinks there was an insurgency in eastern Ukraine. The AI won’t enhance images of violence. Russian emigré intellectuals won’t acknowledge Ukrainian agency. The diplomats won’t see intervention as different from observation.

The protesters on Independence Square understood this in 2014. They rejected being observed and chose to intervene.

We’re not taking your stance. We’re taking ours.

Ladder of Causality

- Association (observation) - Seeing/observing correlations: P(Y\|X) - “What if I *see* X?”

- Intervention - Actively doing something: P(Y\|do(X)) - “What if I *do* X?”

- Counterfactuals - What would have happened: “What if I *had done* X instead?”
