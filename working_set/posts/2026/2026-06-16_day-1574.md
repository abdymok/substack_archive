One of the first things I saw on social media yesterday was Z and the prime minister stumbling around on the roof of the Assumption Cathedral with a priest, surveying the damage.

[Image]

*Ukraine's president Z and Prime Minister Yulia Svyrydenko visit the Dormition Cathedral of the Kyiv Pechersk Lavra, which was hit during Russian missile and drone strikes © Reuters (June 15, 2026)*

The algorithm, with its usual sense of timing, served up Caolan Robertson’s chat with Tim Miller on [The Bulwark](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=iyQA71bLKZU&t=1007s) before I was fully conscious.

The interesting part comes around minute thirteen. Miller asks what one is supposed to do when the air raid sirens go off. Robertson explains that he goes out and films, “but that’s not advised, really.” He then produces a chunk of metal — a missile or drone fragment, “probably made in Ireland actually, with the aluminum,” he says, that landed on his roof the night before.

[Image]

“So you went up to the roof to grab that?” Miller asks.

“Yeah. I heard a massive bang and then saw this land.”

Miller, doing his best impression of someone’s mother: “Shouldn’t you be going into a bunker rather than onto the roof when missiles are firing in?”

“I guess so,” Robertson says, “but I also want to show people what’s actually happening here.”

He lives, by his own account, on the top floor of the tallest residential building in Kyiv. Two of his windows blew out that night. He went up anyway, retrieved the debris, brought it down, and showed it to camera. This is an extraordinary way to live, and also a very particular kind of way to live — one in which the danger and the documentation of the danger have become slightly difficult to distinguish from each other.

Caolan really does have nice digs.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

The last time I encountered him in person was in March, at Golden Gate, practicing stand-up[1](#footnote-1). He looked lonely. I remember thinking, with the mild dread of someone recognizing a pattern, that he would eventually go the way of Sarah Ashton-Cirillo.

For those who weren’t following Ukraine media in 2022 and 2023: Ashton-Cirillo was an American who arrived in Kharkiv that March, became a war correspondent for LGBTQ Nation, then a combat medic, then a Territorial Defense Forces spokesperson with a Twitter following Kyiv was happy to put to use. El País called her one of the most significant voices for Ukrainian propaganda. She was wounded by shelling in Donbas in February 2023, came back, kept going, and then — suspended in September 2023 over unspecified statements, discharged in February 2025. She has, I’m told, since started a Substack called *Political Tips* and joined a foreign UAV unit. She is, by any honest accounting, now a [former journalist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sarah_Ashton-Cirillo).

I bring this up because at the time, watching Caolan, I genuinely imagined him as her heir — not in the specifics, which differ wildly, but in the genre: the foreigner who arrives, fuses with the cause so completely that the line between reporting it and performing it disappears, and is eventually consumed by the fusion. It is a genre with a fairly reliable shelf life.

But this is only the segue. The actual point of today’s post is the move Caolan will never make, and the reason he will never make it.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

He has been, by my reading, anointed. The Honorary Ambassador of Ukraine Award for Digital Diplomacy, issued by the Ministry of Foreign Affairs, supported by the president’s wife — this is not a journalist being covered by a government. This is a content creator being curated by one. And the curation comes with an unstated but perfectly clear condition: you may say anything about Russia. You may not say anything that complicates the picture of Ukraine itself.

Which means Caolan — sincere, brave in the physical sense, willing to climb onto a roof during a massive combined Russia air missile strike to retrieve evidence — will never make the observation that Operation Midas has made unavoidable for anyone actually paying attention: that the war has not displaced the patronage system that ran Ukraine before February 2022, it has simply been laundered through it. That Yermak’s circle was extracting value from defense procurement while soldiers were short of ammunition. That Z, the man whose roof tour he will dutifully amplify today, presides over a state that is simultaneously heroic and, in its administrative core, rotten.

This is not a contradiction Caolan is equipped to hold. It’s not that he’s stupid or cynical — by every account he isn’t. It’s that his entire genre depends on a single, uncomplicated moral current running in one direction. Introduce corruption into the frame and the frame itself stops working. The sirens, the roof, the fragment of Irish aluminum — all of it depends on Z remaining legible as simply the good man under bombardment, not also the man whose government has been quietly stealing from the war effort while asking the world to die for it, metaphorically and otherwise.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

Caolan should meet Trey. I am confident they would hit it off.

Trey Yingst is Fox News’s chief correspondent in Ukraine. He [speaks zero Ukrainian, knows zero about Ukraine’s history, has never served in the military, and usually gets the story wrong](https://abdymok.substack.com/p/day-1005?utm_source=publication-search) — this according to me, eighteen months ago, and I see no reason to revise the assessment. He suffers, by his own account, from PTSD triggered by the smell of barbecue. He has given two pieces of advice to the public on how he survives his job. The first, to [Bloomberg](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2023-04-11/8-travel-tips-from-ukraine-war-journalist-trey-yingst-any-globetrotter-can-use): balance a camera battery on a water bottle against the hotel room door, so an intruder knocks it off and wakes him. The second, to [Men’s Health](https://www.menshealth.com/health/a45781351/trey-yingst-mental-health-war-israel-friday-sessions/): practice cold plunges in peacetime so you learn to control your breathing when your body wants to panic.

Neither tip is about Ukraine. Both are about Trey. This is, I think, the actual genre, stated as plainly as it is ever likely to be stated: a man teaching the audience how he, personally, manages the experience of being near a war, in lieu of teaching them anything about the war itself.

Caolan, climbing to his roof for the aluminum, narrating his own nervous system in real time, is doing the same exercise with a different aesthetic. Trey performs control. Caolan performs exposure. Both performances require the war to remain legible as backdrop — a place where brave, interesting things happen to the narrator — rather than as a country with its own administration, its own patronage networks, its own Yermaks and Mindiches, capable of disappointing the people currently building careers on its suffering.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

The goat thief understood goat theft better than anyone alive and understood it less than almost anyone, because his understanding had become inseparable from his need to be against it. Caolan’s understanding of Ukraine has become inseparable from his need to be for it. Both states are, in their way, sincere. Neither one is curious.

What Z’s government is counting on — what every government counts on, when it can get away with it — is that the people best positioned to amplify the cause will never look too closely at the cause’s custodians. Caolan won’t, because looking closely would cost him the access, the award, the entire architecture of meaning he has built. Sarah didn’t either, until the system that built her up quietly set her down. The moral contrast holds only as long as nobody mentions the contrast running through Kyiv’s own government — and someone curating “digital diplomacy” awards understands that better than anyone.
