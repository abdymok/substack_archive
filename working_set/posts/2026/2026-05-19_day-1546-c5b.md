We have long observed that there are two wars in Ukraine—or perhaps more accurately, two simultaneous interpretations of the same war. A paracosm, they call it in English: a detailed, complex imaginary world created in the mind, so internally consistent that it becomes real to those who inhabit it. The West has constructed such a world.

It is told by people who have visited Kyiv for a week, eaten at the InterContinental (which has a parking garage converted to a bomb shelter, a detail that somehow proves institutional competence), and spoken with officials who have been carefully selected by other officials to speak with them. In this world, Ukraine’s state capacity is crystallizing. Drones favor the defense. Palantir’s dashboards can see the entire conflict with the clarity of gods. Resilience will prevail. The narrative is clean. It has the shape of a story.

But there is another world—one that exists in parallel, often in the same rooms, sometimes in the same moments, in different heads.

In March of this year, the Defense Minister Umerov, a man of some importance, invited a procurement official named Zhumadilov to dinner. Not to his office—to his apartment. On Hrushevskogo Street. With another fellow, a businessman named Mindich, who had certain interests in defense contracts. They discussed body armor. Over veal, presumably. They did not announce this dinner. They did not hold a competitive bidding process. They simply had dinner, as men do, and discussed how the business of defending the nation might be arranged[1](#footnote-1).

By August, this same Mindich had offered to purchase half of Fire Point—a company already receiving major defense contracts—for approximately one billion dollars. The progression is rather transparent, you understand. Get to know the Minister. Gain access to contracts. Buy into the companies receiving those contracts. Become richer. It is, as we say, a formula.

Now, I watched a 8-minute video by a man named Nick Paton Walsh, a correspondent for CNN. He skipped along a road in eastern Ukraine called the Road of Life[2](#footnote-2). It took him five hours to cover a distance that should take one hour, because of the drones. Soldiers were sprinting between trees. There were burned-out vehicles. A young woman named Afina, twenty-five years old, said simply: “I didn’t expect anything like this.”

This seemed to me the truest thing I had heard about the war.

And the American analysts—they see the drones, the adaptation, the soldiers staying low and moving alone rather than in groups, and they conclude: institutional coherence is emerging[3](#footnote-3). Capacity is being built. But I ask myself: what is institutional coherence when the institutions are simultaneously being hollowed from within by their own administrators? What is capacity when it is being redirected through private apartments and billion-dollar stock purchases?

The soldiers on the Road of Life—exhausted, frail, their supplies carried by small robotic trucks—they are doing something extraordinary. They are surviving. They are adapting. This is true.

But their survival is being managed by people who are, at the very same moment, enriching themselves through the management of that survival.

This is not unique to Ukraine. This has been the eternal pattern. But it is peculiarly dangerous in a country that is dying to build something worth being.

I do not say Ukraine will lose. The evidence suggests otherwise. But I wonder—and I say this with the affection one feels for a cousin who is both brave and unfortunate—I wonder whether Ukraine will win *and* remain a place worth living in afterward.

This is the question that no Western op-ed addresses, because it is uncomfortable, and Americans prefer comfort in their narratives. They see soldiers adapting brilliantly and conclude the state is being competently rebuilt. They do not ask what happens when courage and corruption advance at the same pace. When survival and enrichment happen in the same apartment, sometimes in the same conversation.

The veal dinner at Hrushevskogo Street 9a: that is the future. That is what we are actually building, while the paracosm shows us something else entirely.
