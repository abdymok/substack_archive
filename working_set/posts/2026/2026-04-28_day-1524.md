There are two narratives about Ukraine, and they cannot both be true.

The first is the one now routinely told in Western capitals and respectable opinion pages: [that Volodymyr Z has emerged as the standard-bearer of liberal democracy](https://www.nytimes.com/2026/04/26/opinion/zelensky-ukraine-trump-nato-leader.html), a Churchillian figure rallying not only his own country but the moral imagination of the West. The comparison to Cold War Berlin is popular. But let’s be honest about what that comparison actually is.

A Guardian podcast quoted someone saying Kyiv “feels like Berlin felt during the Cold War.” The problem: the person saying it was 13 years old when the Wall fell. He didn’t live through Cold War Berlin. His Berlin correspondent job started in 2003—a decade after the Wall came down. He’s invoking historical gravitas he never actually experienced, wandering around cardboard protests with a microphone, interviewing scene kids as atmospheric color. [This is a pattern.](https://abdymok.substack.com/p/day-1252) Western journalists and analysts donning the costume of historical insight they didn’t earn.

The second story is less cinematic but rather more consistent with observable reality. Ukraine has indeed performed something extraordinary: it has resisted a full-scale Russian invasion. But this achievement belongs, in the main, to its soldiers, its junior officers, and a society capable of improvisation under extreme stress. It does not follow that the country’s political leadership has therefore exemplified the highest standards of democratic governance.

One must insist on a simple distinction that is being willfully blurred: military resilience is not the same thing as liberal democracy.

A liberal democracy is not defined by the eloquence of its wartime speeches or the sympathy it elicits abroad. It is defined by constraint: independent courts, a legislature that can obstruct as well as applaud, and a press permitted to ask questions leaders would prefer not to answer.

On these measures, the Ukrainian record under Z is, at best, ambiguous and, at worst, troubling.

Power has become concentrated. Media has been consolidated. Parliamentary dissent has narrowed to near-irrelevance. Access between the presidency and independent Ukrainian journalists has become selective in ways that would raise eyebrows in any democratic state.

Consider the rotation crisis. [Ukraine’s military ombudswoman has documented something stark](https://www.pravda.com.ua/articles/2026/04/27/8031931/): soldiers are spending 200, 300 days on position without rotation. The order mandates a 15-day maximum. No one follows it. Why? Because there’s no accountability. As she put it: “This is a dead norm that no one observes, which means there are no limits at all. Some commanders don’t feel responsible for the fact that their soldiers have been sitting in bunkers for a year or more.”

The ombudswoman’s research shows that after 40 days, soldiers develop complete apathy—they stop caring whether they survive. Beyond that is not effective. It’s torture by another name.

Mobilization is simultaneously failing. Ukraine has 1.6 million eligible citizens but cannot reach them. Why? Inadequate state communication about defense needs. Citizens evade service. Instead of building public consensus through transparent policy, the government relies on police enforcement and recruitment centers operating under opaque authority. This is Z leadership. This is administrative failure.

When the ombudswoman tried to implement systemic change—to establish clear term limits for service—she encountered resistance at every level. Commanders ignore written orders. Vague authority means zero accountability. She has written 10 formal requirements for compliance. There are no teeth. As she noted: “We lack instruments to punish commanders who don’t follow the ombudswoman’s lawful requirements.”

Consider, too, endemic corruption. [Operation Midas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Operation_Midas) documented substantial schemes in the energy sector, implicating networks close to the center of political power. Senior figures associated with the presidential administration found themselves dismissed, investigated, or removed. One may call this a purge, a reform, or coincidence. What one cannot call it is irrelevant.

More revealing still was what followed. As anti-corruption bodies pressed investigations, legislative efforts emerged to curtail their independence. The response was public: the 2025 anti-corruption protests in Ukraine, the first significant anti-government demonstrations since the full-scale invasion. Faced with public pressure, the government retreated.

This sequence deserves attention. It suggests not a system comfortably committed to accountability, but one in which accountability must be extracted, often against resistance.

Ukraine’s most impressive achievements in this war—its adaptation to drone warfare, its decentralized tactical innovations, its capacity for rapid learning—have not been products of centralized political control. They have emerged from below, from units and commanders operating where initiative is rewarded because it must be.

This creates an awkward but unavoidable conclusion: the success of Ukraine’s resistance does not, in itself, validate the structure of its governance.

To say this is not to diminish Ukraine’s struggle, nor to question the necessity of supporting it. It is to insist on a distinction that allies, of all people, are obliged to maintain: the difference between solidarity and myth-making.

If Ukraine prevails—and one hopes it does—it will face a second challenge. It will need to reconstruct the institutional safeguards that have been bent, suspended, or quietly eroded since Z was elected. Courts will need to reassert independence. Legislatures will need to recover the habit of resistance. Journalists will need to ask questions that no longer have the convenient excuse of national emergency.

At that point, the flattering fictions of wartime will become less useful. And the question of what, exactly, has been built in the meantime will no longer be avoidable.
