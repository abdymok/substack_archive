Tomas, the surgeon in *The Unbearable Lightness of Being*, knew from the resistance of tissue under the knife exactly where he was. This knowledge was not innate. It was the residue of ten thousand operations — ten thousand moments in which the hand learned what the eye could not see.

When Tomas was certain, it was because he had spent years being unsure.

This is one kind of confidence. *Einmal ist keinmal*.

There is another kind — the confidence of the man who has read about surgery and found it straightforward. This kind is not diminished by failure. If anything, failure improves it, because failure is always someone else’s instrument.

Pete Hegseth stood before the cameras at U.S. Central Command headquarters in Tampa — six days into Operation Epic Fury — and [explained that America was winning decisively](https://www.timesofisrael.com/hegseth-iran-is-toast-and-the-us-and-israel-will-rain-down-death-and-destruction/).

Iran is toast.

Its navy rested at the bottom of the Persian Gulf. Its air force, built for 1996, had been destroyed in 2026. Its ballistic missiles — the ones not yet buried deep enough — were being hunted by B-2 bombers dropping 2,000-pound penetrator bombs. The next phase, he said, would raze the missile industrial base entirely.

The throttle was going up.

Pick your adjective: violent, decimated, most-lethal, savage.

We are punching them while they’re down, which is exactly how it should be.

Our timeline, he said, is ours and ours alone to control.

It was, by any measure, a confident briefing.

Werner Heisenberg, who was a better pianist than a prose stylist, understood something about instruments that Hegseth has not yet had occasion to consider.

If you wish to observe an electron, you might bounce a photon off it and detect what returns. But the photon, in striking, imparts momentum to the electron and changes the path of the thing you were trying to measure. Or the electron, moving as quantum particles do, is no longer where it was when the photon first bounced.

Either way, the act of observation alters what was being observed.

You cannot know both where the particle is and where it is going. This is not a failure of technology. It is not a problem better instruments will eventually solve.

It is a condition of reality.

Kant saw this before quantum mechanics gave it a name. The thing-in-itself — the *Ding an sich* — is not available to us directly. We know only what our instruments can reach. Heisenberg formalized it: measurement disturbs the thing measured. Borges turned it into a fable — a map so detailed it becomes the same size as the territory, and therefore useless.

Three centuries, three disciplines, one problem.

The distance between the knower and the known is not an inconvenience to be engineered away. It is the condition that makes knowledge possible in the first place.

Erase it entirely — let the map become the territory — and you do not achieve perfect knowledge.

You achieve the blindness of the man who has confused his picture for the world.

Hegseth’s briefing contained thousands of words. Humility occupied none of them.

*The most-lethal, most-precise aerial operation in history. Our timeline is ours and ours alone to control.*

These are not the words of a man who has considered the gap between the picture and the thing. They are the words of a man who has not been asked to.

**The map is never the territory.**

Coordinates programmed in 2013 do not know that a sports field was added in 2014, that recreational areas appeared over time, that children were sitting at desks on a Saturday morning — which in Iran is the first day of the working week.

[On February 28, a precision strike hit the Shajarah Tayyebeh elementary school in Minab](https://www.nytimes.com/2026/03/05/world/middleeast/iran-school-us-strikes-naval-base.html), near the Strait of Hormuz. At least 175 people were killed, many of them children.

The school had once been part of the Revolutionary Guards’ naval base. By September 2016 it had been partitioned off, no longer connected by road, bearing by then all the hallmarks of a school — a sports field, recreational areas added over time.

A former Pentagon adviser, reviewing the satellite imagery, called the strike picture perfect.

Asked about the school, Hegseth said: we’re investigating that. We of course never target civilian targets.

The White House press secretary, Karoline Leavitt, was more precise: not that we know of.

U.S. military investigators now believe it is likely that American forces were responsible, though they have not reached a final conclusion. The investigation has largely answered itself while Hegseth is still calling it an investigation.

This is not exactly a lie.

It is the genuine belief of a man whose instrument does not include, in its field of measurement, the possibility that the picture might be wrong.

Tomas knew the picture could be wrong. That knowledge is what made him a surgeon rather than simply a man holding a knife.

I am writing from Kyiv, where we have some experience with picture-perfect strikes and the investigations that follow them.

On July 8, 2024, a Kh-101 cruise missile struck the Okhmatdyt National Children’s Hospital. The missile was programmed using predetermined coordinates, which the Ukrainian Prosecutor General says indicates the deliberate nature of the strike.

A thirty-year-old doctor named Svitlana Lukianchyk was killed. The dialysis ward, where children received treatment, was obliterated.

It cannot be rebuilt.

This week Ukrainian law enforcement served Russian Major General Sergei Kuvaldin with [a notice of suspicion in absentia](https://www.pravda.com.ua/eng/news/2026/03/04/8023813/). He issued the combat order. He is currently in Russia.

Measures are being taken.

Measures are always being taken.

Milan Kundera’s great theme was the weight of the irreversible. *Once is never*, because what happens once might as well not have happened at all.

But the heavy things cannot be taken back.

The coordinates cannot be unprogrammed. The school cannot be unstruck. The dialysis ward cannot be unbuilt from rubble.

The Minab instrument was working from old imagery. Kuvaldin’s instrument was working from malice.

The origins were different.

The children were the same.

Outside Minab, excavators dug nearly a hundred small graves before the funerals.

There is a notice of suspicion. There is an investigation. There is a press secretary who said *not that we know of*.

These are the instruments that arrive after the picture-perfect strike — when the picture is examined and found, with some embarrassment, to have been taken ten years ago.

Heisenberg would have recognized the situation.

The electron was no longer where the photon expected it to be. The school was no longer what the coordinates remembered.

The observation changed nothing about the thing observed, because the thing observed was already gone — replaced, over years, by children and a sports field and a Saturday morning the instrument did not know about and did not ask.

The uncertainty principle does not excuse the measurement.

It demands more care in the taking of it.

The strike was picture perfect.

The picture was ten years old.

Somewhere, a general is in Russia.

Somewhere, a secretary of war is returning to the cameras.

Our timeline, he will tell us, is ours and ours alone to control.
