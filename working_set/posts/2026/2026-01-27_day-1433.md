Depending on the robustness of your cardiovascular system, Kyiv is practically balmy this week—despite foreign media solemnly informing the world that we are all freezing to death[1](#footnote-1)[2](#footnote-2)[3](#footnote-3). The city sits on the ragged edge of the great purplish low-pressure blob smothering European Russia, which means temperatures may flirt with 0°C today and tomorrow.

The ground hasn’t gotten the memo. Pavements and streets remain brutally cold, so every bout of half-rain, half-snow mist instantly flash-freezes into a delicate, treacherous glaze. Uncleared sidewalks are coated in a good half-inch of it, growing slicker by the minute as everything turns to slush. Lots of people walking around with broken arms after slipping.

Janine’s article for [The Guardian](http://theguardian.com) uses the same pic as Viktor Yuschcenko’s [Instagram post](https://www.instagram.com/p/DT9u_FLDB7h/). The comparison between Troieshchyna and Sarajevo is inapt. The siege began in April 1992 and caught the city unprepared. Ukraine had the entire 2022-2023 winter as a preview, then 2023-2024, then all of 2024 and 2025 watching Russia methodically target energy infrastructure. The pattern was obvious. The response should have been systematic hardening of the grid, distributed generation capacity, underground facilities, redundancy built into heating systems.

Instead, we get tents pitched in living rooms and bricks heated on gas stoves—the same improvised desperation that characterized Sarajevo’s siege. Except Sarajevo had an excuse: they were caught off-guard. What’s Z’s excuse after three years of this playbook? [Operation Midas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Operation_Midas).

Di Giovanni writes that “no statistic can capture what it means to live in a city where winter has been deliberately turned into a tool of terror,” but there’s a statistic that matters enormously: how much money and effort went into preparing for exactly this scenario between 2023 and 2025? Because if the answer is “not nearly enough,” then the Yushchenko-style rhetoric about “altars of freedom” and “light that cannot be extinguished” starts to sound less like defiance and more like unnecessary pathos—a substitute for competent governance.

The heroism is real—the divers in the Dnipro River, Brekht dying at the substation. But heroism shouldn’t be necessary on this scale when the threat was telegraphed years in advance. We want less heroism, more competence.

We’re all about maintenance these days, having realized that the only way to make our Krebs Cycle go forward, is to eat less and run more. In the end, it’s all thermodynamics, as [Nick Lane](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=vBiIDwBOqQA) would say. We definitely don't want our Krebs Cycle running in reverse because instead of producing the energy our cells need to function, it begins generating harmful byproducts and consuming resources—essentially working against us rather than for us.
