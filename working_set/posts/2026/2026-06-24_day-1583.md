*Closer to home*, **The Conference That Will Definitely Take Place**

There is a conference scheduled for Gdansk on June 25th and 26th concerning the reconstruction of Ukraine, and it will definitely take place. This has been confirmed by Adam Szlapka, spokesperson for the Polish government, who wished also to confirm that he has no signs that Z will not be present, which is a subtly different thing from confirming that Z will be present, though in the language of diplomatic communications these distinctions are sometimes considered impolite to dwell upon.

Z himself has not yet provided an answer. His communications advisor Dmytro Lytvyn explained on Monday that they are still analyzing. As soon as a decision is made, he said, they will report immediately. This is reassuring. The conference starts tomorrow.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

The immediate cause of the current unprecedented tension — the greatest in many years, as Szlapka described it, with the careful tone of a man who has lived through lesser tensions and is now, for the first time, genuinely impressed — requires a brief history.

On May 26th, Z signed [a decree](https://archive.ph/w9hEP) honoring the Ukrainian Insurgent Army, known by its acronym UPA, a formation that fought against the Soviet Union during and after World War II, and that also, in 1943, conducted a campaign of ethnic cleansing against Polish civilians in what Poland remembers as the Volyn Genocide. Historians estimate the campaign killed approximately 80,000 Polish civilians. To Ukrainians, the UPA is remembered for its armed struggle against Moscow. To Poles, it is remembered for something else entirely. These two memories have coexisted uneasily for decades. Z’s decree made them less easy.

Poland’s President Nawrocki responded on June 19th by revoking the Order of the White Eagle — Poland’s highest state decoration, named after the bird on the Polish coat of arms, which has been white since the thirteenth century and shows no signs of reconsidering — that had been awarded to Z in 2023 by Nawrocki’s predecessor, Andrzej Duda. To strip someone of the Order of the White Eagle is to say, with the full ceremonial weight of the Polish state behind you, that you have changed your mind about this person. The Ukrainian government, having received this message, photographed the Order being packed into its presentation box in Kyiv and released the photograph to the press. The photograph has the quality of a statement.

Z said he would return it first. His chief of staff Budanov and Foreign Minister Sybiha returned their Polish state awards the same day. The following Saturday, former presidents Kuchma, Yushchenko, and Poroshenko announced they were returning theirs as well. The eagles were accumulating.

Former Foreign Minister Czaputowicz delivered perhaps the most honest Polish assessment of the situation: Poland did not win this exchange. Ukraine has not changed its stance. Poland’s international position has decreased, and will decrease further. He said this on a television program called *View of Szubartowicz*, which is either a very serious program or the name of a man who bought a television channel and named it after himself, and either way seemed an appropriate venue for this particular reckoning.

Tusk, meanwhile, said what everyone sensible was saying and nobody sufficiently powerful was hearing: “The conflict between Poland and Ukraine pleases Putin and shocks our allies. The front line is elsewhere.”

The far right disagreed. Sławomir Mentzen, whose Konfederacja party received fourteen percent of the vote in last year’s presidential election, wrote on Facebook that it was good the Ukrainians were giving back their awards, but that “it’s just a pity that we handed them out like candy in the first place.” He added that once they had returned all the awards, they could also return the money, weapons, ammunition, and everything else Poland had previously given them. This position has the virtue of consistency and the defect of arithmetic, since the removal of Polish transit routes for Western military aid to Ukraine would primarily affect the front line that Mentzen does not appear to have visited.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

Nawrocki was not invited to the conference in Gdansk. This is because the conference was organized by Tusk, jointly with Z, and Tusk did not invite the President of Poland, because it is a government event, and the President is not the government. Szlapka pointed out, helpfully, that neither the German president nor the Italian president attended similar conferences in Berlin and Rome. “There is absolutely no malice here,” he added. When a spokesperson says there is absolutely no malice, the correct interpretive procedure is to note the word *absolutely* and continue.

Nawrocki’s office noted that the president had not been invited by Tusk or by Z, and therefore would not attend an event to which he had not been invited. This is the kind of statement that presents itself as obvious but rewards a second reading. It is, in the most precise sense, true.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

There is also the matter of the canceled meetings. Marcin Przydacz, head of the International Policy Bureau at the Presidential Chancellery, explained that a phone call had been scheduled between Nawrocki and Z, and then canceled by the Ukrainian side. Then a visit to Warsaw was proposed, a date and time were established, and then, a day before the visit, Z declined, offering a different time. The Presidential Palace concluded that most likely the Ukrainian side was not interested in the conversation taking place at all.

Lytvyn denied this. “There were no dates agreed upon by both sides,” he said.

This is a dispute about whether dates were agreed upon. Both men have access to the same calendar. The calendar, presumably, knows what happened. The calendar is not talking.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

The Polish Business Council, representing nine of the largest organizations of entrepreneurs and employers, has called for calm, on the grounds that hundreds of billions of euros in infrastructure, city reconstruction, digitalization, and energy investment flow somewhat less efficiently through a diplomatic crisis than through cordial relations. Marek Gora, President of the Lewiatan Confederation, put it plainly: it would be good if Polish firms, together with European partners, participated in this. *It would be good* is the business community’s version of *please stop.*

The Order of the White Eagle is in a box in Kyiv. Z is still analyzing. The conference will definitely take place.

The Polish Business Council would like everyone to calm down.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

*Update, June 23:* Z has finished analyzing. He will not attend.

Szlapka had no signs of this. Lytvyn said they would report immediately when a decision was made, and they have, with two days to spare, which in the current circumstances counts as advance notice. The conference will still definitely take place. There will be Ukrainian representation, since it concerns the reconstruction of Ukraine. The practical dimension, Szlapka had said, is much more important than the political dimension.

The practical dimension is now also absent.

Nawrocki, who was not invited, will also not be there. This means that the Ukraine Recovery Conference in Gdansk will be attended by neither the President of Ukraine nor the President of Poland, which is one way to de-escalate tension, in the sense that it is difficult to have a diplomatic incident between two heads of state who are both somewhere else.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

*Also June 23:* Poroshenko has issued a statement.

He explained that two weeks ago he had promised his colleagues at the conciliation council that he would return his eagle if they failed to convince Nawrocki not to strip Z of the award. They did not succeed. He is therefore returning it — a gesture of solidarity with Z that is notable because Poroshenko and Z have spent the better part of six years in a state of mutual political hostility that has occasionally resembled a second front. The eagle, it turns out, is a more powerful unifying force than most things that have been tried.

Poroshenko noted that Medvedev had already congratulated Nawrocki. Once you have noted that Moscow is applauding, further argument becomes somewhat awkward for the other side, since nobody, in the current climate, wishes to be the thing Moscow is applauding.

He quoted Tusk — “if we quarrel over the past, someone else will win the future” — and Sikorski — “only Moscow can emerge as the winner in the war over history and orders” — and said he agreed with both. This is the political equivalent of citing scripture: you have not made the argument yourself, but you have arranged for two senior Polish officials to make it for you, which is more efficient and considerably harder to rebut.

On the question of textbooks — always, in Polish-Ukrainian relations, the question of textbooks — Poroshenko was precise: he does not want Poles editing Ukrainian textbooks, and he does not claim that Ukrainians should adjust Polish textbooks, because otherwise, he noted, they will send a joint textbook from Moscow. This image — a jointly authored Russo-Polish-Ukrainian history textbook, arriving unannounced, explaining everything — is the most clarifying thing anyone has said about this crisis.

He invoked John Paul II. He invoked Rzeszów as a symbol of solidarity. He proposed that starting Monday, politicians should look for solutions.

Monday is tomorrow. The conference is also tomorrow.

The eagle is in the mail.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

*Further update, same day:* The journalists have issued an appeal.

Twenty-six media organizations — Polish and Ukrainian, broadsheets and digital outlets, radio stations and press clubs, including the redoubtable Gazeta Wyborcza and the Kyiv Independent, who will also publish the appeal, a fact noted at the bottom of the document with the slight emphasis of an institution that wants you to know it is joining but did not organize — have released a joint statement calling on the politicians of both countries to demonstrate wisdom and reasonableness, seek understanding, and find a way out of the crisis.

This is what journalists do when the politicians have made such a thorough mess of things that the only remaining option is to publish a letter. It is a gesture of considerable dignity and complete powerlessness, and the twenty-six organizations who signed it deserve both acknowledgment and sympathy.

The appeal invokes a joint statement from 2013, in which former presidents Kuchma and Kwaśniewski — men who had presided over their respective countries in the 1990s and thus carried the particular authority of people who had watched things be much worse — wrote that the commemoration of the victims of past conflicts should not become an instrument for inflaming relations or squandering the gains of reconciliation. The appeal notes that this obligation now rests on all of us.

*All of us* is doing considerable work in that sentence. It encompasses Z, who is not going to Gdansk. It encompasses Nawrocki, who stripped the Order and was not invited. It encompasses Tusk, who organized the conference and did not invite the President of his own country. It encompasses the politicians, the media, civil society, and the citizens of both nations, which is a list comprehensive enough to include everyone and therefore, in the practical sense, no one in particular.

The time for serious conversations about the past, the appeal concludes, will come when Ukraine defeats the aggressor. This is a reasonable position. It is also, if one is being precise, a deferral. The Volyn massacre occurred in 1943. It has been deferred before. The victims, who have been waiting eighty-three years for the serious conversation, are not available for comment.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

*Final update, also June 23:* Donald Tusk has spoken.

“Regardless of who spoils our work in Warsaw and Kyiv,” the Prime Minister announced from the Council of Ministers meeting, “the conference in Gdansk will take place, and we will do our job.”

Two hundred agreements and contracts between Polish and Ukrainian companies have been prepared for signing. Tusk has spent many hours working to minimize the costs and damages arising from the decisions of the presidents of Ukraine and Poland, which he described not as a diplomatic crisis or an unprecedented moment of tension but simply as *spoiling the work*, with the weary precision of a man who has organized a very large dinner party and is now watching two guests throw the crockery at each other in the foyer.

The practical dimension is arriving by train from Kyiv on Wednesday.

And then there is Z’s statement, which arrived late and which I have saved for last because it is, in its way, the most clarifying thing said by anyone in this entire affair. Z noted that the Order of the White Eagle is a symbol of trust of the Polish state and gratitude of the people, and that Ukraine respects Poland’s contribution to the common fight. And then he added — with the timing of a man who has been waiting several days to say this — that Ukraine will not dispute decisions on awards if such symbols remain with Catherine the Great, Benito Mussolini, and Gerhard Schroeder.

Catherine the Great. Benito Mussolini. Gerhard Schroeder.

This is not a diplomatic statement. It is a list. Each name carries its own considerable freight — an empress who partitioned Poland out of existence, a fascist who invaded it, a chancellor who built the pipeline that funded the army now bombing Ukraine — and the implication is that the Order of the White Eagle has, over the centuries, kept interesting company, and that its current symbolic weight might therefore be assessed accordingly.

The calendar knows what happened.

The conference will definitely take place.

Two hundred contracts are waiting in Gdansk.

And somewhere in the long, unresolved argument between Poland and Ukraine about what history means and who gets to say so, a jointly authored textbook from Moscow sits on a shelf, patient as only unread books can be, waiting for everyone to finish.
