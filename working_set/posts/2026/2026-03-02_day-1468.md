On the final day of diplomacy in Geneva, February 26, two men had a full calendar. In the morning, at the residence of the Omani ambassador in Cologny, they discussed Iran’s nuclear program. After lunch, a short drive across the city, at the Hôtel des Bergues on the Rhône, they discussed Ukraine’s survival. The conference rooms were close together. Efficiency is underrated.

The same men. The same city. The same smiles for photographers. One file concerned uranium enrichment; the other concerned the continued existence of a European state. Both were treated as negotiable.

Serious people observed this calmly. It is considered unhelpful to appear alarmed while history is being itemized.

While the morning talks were still running, a senior Iranian official said that an immediate agreement was within reach. Iran had tabled its first written proposal. The Omani foreign minister reported that both sides had exchanged creative and positive ideas. Talks were set to resume at six in the evening.

Meanwhile, the USS Gerald Ford had left port near Crete, bound for waters off Haifa. A dozen F-22s had been quietly deployed to Israel — the first American combat aircraft sent there for potential wartime operations. Several Western embassies had begun withdrawing non-essential staff from the Middle East.

The transitions in Geneva were seamless. Centrifuges before lunch. Borders after. By evening the word “peace” had been used often enough to feel procedural.

Witkoff and Kushner left Geneva without a deal in either file. But, at the very least, no collapse either.

The war — and it is a war, the President said so himself, in capital letters — required no congressional authorization and no defined end state. It required confidence. The President had promised, fifty-three documented times, to end Russia’s war against Ukraine. He had also promised not to begin new wars. These commitments were not contradictory. They simply belonged to different audiences.

Meanwhile, Persian-language broadcasters were defunded. VPN access programs were cut. Civil-society tools were dismantled. Then bombs were dropped. The Iranian people were encouraged to reclaim their institutions.

“Surrender,” the President said.

The recipient was unspecified. Power, apparently, would organize itself.

Khamenei ruled for thirty-seven years by blood, fire, and tears. Weeks before the bombs fell, thousands of protesters were shot in the streets by his regime. His victims are entitled to feel relief at his absence. That is not a political position. It is a human one.

Relief is not the same as vindication. As Lebanese journalist Diana Moukalled [wrote](https://x.com/dianamoukalled/status/2027989281176698920): what happened was not a clash between right and wrong. It was a collision of domination projects. Khamenei was not a project of resistance. He was a project of control confronting another project of control. To transform him into a martyr — especially by those who remained silent as the grave on his crimes — is not a moral stance. It is a falsification of memory. And to celebrate the men who killed him is to replace one logic of glorification with another.

In the struggle for hegemony, the strongest wins. The problem is that we continue to count the losses.

Z, a serious man in an impossible situation, responded that American resolve was important. When America acts decisively, criminals weaken. He does not mention what follows it.

Seventy percent of Ukrainians do not believe the negotiations will produce lasting peace. They were not asked.

Elsewhere — in a small northern community in British Columbia — a fabric shop posted a call for help. Quilters across Canada answered. The goal: 2,700 quilts, one for every resident of Tumbler Ridge after a senseless tragedy. Each quilt is stitched by hand. Each one meant to feel like a hug.

They may produce too many. There is already a plan for the extras.

It is a modest ambition: one quilt per person. No red lines. No ultimatums. No airstrikes. Just warmth distributed evenly.

In the Kremlin, Putin watched the bombing of Tehran with professional interest. From Belgrade to Baghdad, from Kabul to Tehran, the pattern has been educational. Power arrives dramatically. Responsibility departs quietly.

He indicated the burning city.

*That could have been us*, he said.

He was not wrong.
