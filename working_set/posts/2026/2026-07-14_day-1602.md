Cosmologists have a name for the laziest possible theory: the cosmological constant. A single number stapled onto Einstein’s equations that explains why the universe accelerates—and asks nothing further of you. It fits the data beautifully and is, by design, completely uninteresting: a number with no story behind it.

Ukrainian governance has had its own cosmological constant since roughly forever. The ongoing theoretical fuss about replacing that constant with something dynamical increasingly reads like Bankova with the names filed off.

The constant, in one line: a primitive post-Soviet patronage system that survives by rearranging personnel, never its physics.

Everything else—the ministers, the “programs,” the solemn Telegram choreography—is quintessence: a scalar field, slowly rolling, creating the appearance of motion while the underlying vacuum energy remains exactly still.

Countries are ruled by their diagnoses. The diagnosis here hasn’t changed. It isn’t corruption—that’s a symptom. It’s a management system with no legal mechanism for rotating power short of collapse.

Formally, dismissing a government is parliament’s prerogative. In practice, deputies learn of it from rival factions before their own leadership tells them, and are then asked to ratify a decision made fifteen minutes earlier on Bankova. The vote is less a check than a formality—with a price attached: a case dropped here, an appointment or embassy dangled there.

So: Svyrydenko out after eleven months—before even reaching her first anniversary—thanked, and floated for Washington as ambassador. A posting that remains, for now, a promise rather than an agrément: Washington hasn’t signed off.

The seat is open because her predecessor, Olha Stefanyshina, is being quietly unwound ahead of a reported second NABU suspicion—this time for illicit enrichment—layered atop an older case from a Yanukovych-era justice ministry program, involving roughly 2.5 million hryvnias that never quite adapted anything to European legislation, plus a Kyiv apartment reportedly purchased well below market.

By some accounts, it was Washington itself that nudged the process—preferring a quiet rotation to the embarrassment of a sitting ambassador under formal suspicion.

Svyrydenko, installed as Yermak’s executor, repledged herself to the palace the moment the alignment shifted, with the same fluency. The coupling constant doesn’t care which particle it attaches to—only that the attachment holds.

Along the way, she reportedly drew over three million hryvnias teaching at a university run by a serial state-board veteran, without ever specifying how many hours she taught. A parliamentary inquiry circled back empty—ministry to university and back again—the kind of paper trail that generates its own criminal referral.

Koretsky in: a plausible technocrat, a workaholic, not a politician—which, after three failed premierships, now counts as a credential.

The only question that matters is whether he arrives with a field of his own, or as a lone particle dropped into an otherwise unchanged system. Early signals suggest the latter: most ministers remain, merely reshuffled between portfolios.

Possibly Fedorov out of Defense—not demoted so much as reclassified: deputy prime minister without portfolio, the standard resting state for a rival whose approval ratings begin to rhyme with the president’s.

Zaluzhny, in London, is rumored for the same kind of “rotation”—the polite word for removing a man from a post that was itself the polite word for exile.

This is the coincidence problem in local form.

Why, of all epochs, do we observe dark energy and matter density at comparable scales? Kyiv’s version: why does each reshuffle land precisely when a corruption exposure becomes undeniable—often abroad before it is at home?

Last July: Chernyshov, pulled back under suspicion.
This July: Stefanyshina.
Right on schedule, the system produces a replacement already legible to Washington.

A coincidence that repeats on schedule isn’t one. It’s a coupling nobody writes down, fitted after the fact and called something else.

And the result, after every dynamical model gets its turn—tracker fields, phantom energy, modified gravity—is the same: none of it solves the problem it was built to solve.

Swapping the field doesn’t touch the constant. It just layers a new unnaturalness on top of the old one.

The unequipped version is simpler: stop grading surnames. Watch the program. Check back in three months.

Nothing will have moved.

The honest prediction—the one the smart money still holds:

**w = −1.**

Everything else is well-funded motion against a fixed background.

Last night Kyiv was again hit by something that gives almost no warning.

Not Iskanders. Not cruise missiles. Russia appears to have fired six to eight modified S-400 air-defense missiles in their secondary role as short-range ballistic missiles. They are among the nastiest weapons in Moscow’s inventory because they reach the city in little more than a minute from launch sites across the border. Several of us heard the explosion before our phones even finished announcing the air raid.

That isn’t a glitch. It’s physics.

[Video: https://youtu.be/skHDkOMd54k]

Designed to shoot down aircraft, S-400 missiles can also be fired at ground targets.

Unfortunately, we’re out of Patriot PAC-3s.

The strikes set warehouse facilities ablaze in Holosiivsky district. Falling debris ignited cars in Darnytsky. We heard around eight explosions in rapid succession.

The point isn’t merely to destroy buildings.

It’s to compress the time between ordinary life and war until they become almost indistinguishable.
