More than a dozen dead, scores wounded, in Kyiv and the surrounding region this morning.

On Saturday I wrote that the capital’s residents had become sitting ducks[1](#footnote-1).

Nobody needed today’s attack to confirm it—least of all Z and his senior managers, who will again tell us:

“*Well, we don’t have enough Patriot missiles, so what can we do? Everyone’s on their own now. Things are going to be bad.”*

To me, that’s a sign of incompetence, unprofessionalism, and an unwillingness to do anything.

Picture water coming through the ceiling. Option A: empty the bucket, faster than the water fills it, for as long as you can stand there. Option B: go upstairs and turn off the tap. Ukraine currently owns several excellent buckets, refuses to go upstairs, and calls the resulting posture a strategy.

The Iskander systems and the other launchers firing at us sit close to the border, in fixed and known positions. Nothing prevents striking them—not distance, not a shortage of means. Drones will do it. The same Flamingo F5 missiles built to hit Moscow work equally well pointed at a launch site forty kilometers from Kharkiv.

No launch battery can be guarded everywhere, all the time, with total security. There are several ways to reduce the number of ballistic missiles reaching this Kyiv. All of them share a premise: if the missile cannot be caught in flight, it must never leave the ground. That premise requires a system, a plan, and people whose job is to execute it rather than complain about not having enough interceptors.

Yesterday's meeting between Lukashenko and his flunkies, discussing the creation of an "academy of the future," grabbed my attention for the same reason. It is the same style of governance long practiced in Moscow, and increasingly here. Like Belarus and Russia, Ukraine has no independent parliament and no functioning judiciary, and independent media have been largely marginalized—Z doesn't take questions from them either. The result, in both capitals, is ritualized obeisance, Telegram bot armies, and the kind of cringing sycophancy on display in the video clip below. A government staffed by people whose job is agreement doesn't just fail to go upstairs and find the tap. It stops being able to conceive that upstairs exists.

**EXCLUSIVE:** [Fire Point targets first interception by mid-2027.](https://www.reuters.com/business/aerospace-defense/ukraines-fire-point-starts-integrate-european-tech-into-missile-defence-system-2026-08-04/)

Really? At least we’re no longer looking at PowerPoint slides.

Ten months is an abstraction until you sit with what’s happening in the meantime. Kyiv no longer enjoys anything resembling dependable protection against ballistic missiles. The sirens, when they sound at all, increasingly go off after the explosions, because a ballistic missile’s flight time is shorter than the interval needed to detect it and push a warning to your phone. The advice that circulates, when officials bother to give any, amounts to this: be fast. Keep your shelter route memorized. Physical fitness is, for now, a substitute for air defense.

This is the texture of the “winter humanitarian crisis” Z himself has been warning about—a phrase that sounds like boilerplate until you notice Russian strikes are landing on energy and logistics infrastructure roughly every two or three days, aimed at leaving Ukrainians without heat and light before the cold sets in. It is also, not incidentally, the backdrop to Z’s own offer to negotiate an energy truce directly with Putin: not a sign of confidence, but a request for time the missiles aren’t giving him.

The numbers bear this out district by district. Compiled from Kyiv Military State Administration/Kyiv City State Administration Telegram channels and open-source reporting, recorded strike hits across Kyiv since February 24, 2022 range from 38 in Pechersky to 104 in Shevchenkivsky—and in most districts, a disproportionate share of that total has landed in the last two years alone.

None of this is happening because the danger is ambiguous. It is happening in plain view, on television, discussed by talk-show hosts and their guests with the flat fluency of people describing weather. That fluency is its own symptom: when the discussion of no available interceptors settles into the same register as advice about running shoes, the sycophancy described above has done its work twice over—once on the institutions, and once on how ordinary catastrophe gets talked about.

Which brings us back to Minsk. Lukashenko’s “academy of the future” is a small, almost decorative example of the same disease—officials competing to flatter a man who has already decided what he wants to hear, in a room where no one present has the standing, or the nerve, to tell him otherwise. Kyiv’s version is less cartoonish but not structurally different. It just wears black miltech pajamas and calls itself wartime necessity.

Fire Point sits slightly outside this culture of political theater, which is precisely why it's worth paying attention to. (Reuters reported August 4 that Fire Point has begun integrating European tech into its missile defense system—reason enough to withhold judgment on the company for now, rather than take the announcement at face value)[2](#footnote-2).

An interceptor either works or it doesn’t. It won’t be moved by a flattering seating chart, and it can’t be summoned into existence by a presidential administration deciding not to take questions. If Ukraine gets through this winter, it will be despite the governing style described above, not because of it—on the strength of engineers who answer to physics rather than to Bankova, working against a deadline that has nothing to do with anyone’s approval ratings.

The state doesn’t need to lie to you outright. It only needs to keep handing you small, dignified rituals to perform—running drills, positive symptoms, academies of the future—while the actual protection against the actual threat remains however many months away it will turn out to be once this deadline, too, has come and gone.

In the meantime, be fast, keep your shelter route memorized, and try not to notice how familiar all of this has started to feel.
