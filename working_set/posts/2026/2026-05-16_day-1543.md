Kyiv, like certain beautiful women and certain unreliable relatives, rarely reveals its biography at first meeting. At first you think: ah, a handsome pink building with a whimsical spire. You rent a top-floor apartment because you are young enough to believe that climbing five flights of stairs every day is evidence of character rather than bad judgment. You open the window and see the chestnut tree flowering below, and you imagine that history, having already exhausted itself in this city, will politely remain in museums.

This is where I arrived—I’d spent months drifting through Belgrade and Budapest after leaving Minsk, burned out on foundation work, disappointed by indifference to outcomes. I was looking for something I couldn’t name. By the time I got to Kyiv, the United States was preparing to start bombing Yugoslavia. I had no way of knowing it then, but I’d arrived just in time to watch history decide it wasn’t finished with this part of the world after all.

Naturally, history laughs.

The house at *Будинок із вежею на Рейтарській* — also known as the Shlenzkevich House, and later, with proper Kyiv absurdity, as the House of Tarapunka’s Tower — seems to have been built precisely to mock the idea that buildings are passive things.

First it belonged to a noblewoman. Then to a cavalry captain. Then to a merchant of the first guild, which sounds less like a profession than a title in a Gogol story. Then to a man named Sokolnikov. Then, in 1912, it was purchased by the wife of a barrister, Hanna Shlenzkevich, who commissioned the architect Oleksandr Verbytsky to build something suitably ambitious for the highest point of Old Kyiv.

And Verbytsky, apparently in no mood for modesty, built a house that could not enter a room quietly.

It has a turret. It has a spire. It has mascarons — stone faces frozen in permanent astonishment, which is the correct expression for anyone living through Kyiv’s twentieth century. It has floral ornament, garlands, volutes, decorative vases, curving lines of late modernism and enough ornamental confidence to suggest that the Austro-Hungarian Empire may have sent emotional support from Vienna.

Then came revolution, civil war, Bolshevik nationalization, communal apartments, Soviet neglect, and the special brutality of “renovation,” in which officials improve a building by removing everything that made it worth preserving.

In apartment number 6, from 1915 through the 1930s, lived Igor Sikorsky — the architect’s own apartment, though Sikorsky was no architect but an engineer designing helicopters, which meant the building housed both the man who built it and the man who was learning to build things that flew[1](#footnote-1). (No relation, incidentally, to Radek Sikorski, Poland’s current foreign minister — just a coincidence of surnames.)

In apartment number 4, from 1914–1916, the editors of *Vestnik Magnetizma* produced a journal devoted to animal magnetism, hypnotism, and mediumism — the kind of popular science that seems quaint now but felt urgent then.

IIn apartment number 14, during the brief and feverish years of Ukrainian independence in 1917–1918, editors produced the satirical magazine *[Ґедз](https://kp.nlu.org.ua/item/1875) (The Gadfly)* — ten issues spanning October 1917 to July 1918, edited by Zakhar Biksky.[2](#footnote-2)

[Image]

*Ґедз (The Gadfly)*

It was one of three Ukrainian-language satirical journals that emerged during the Ukrainian Central Rada period, alongside *Budiak* and *Rep’iakhy*. The magazine carried more than two hundred caricatures and satirical illustrations by artists including Ivan Buryachok, Yuri Vovk, Hryhoriy Zolotov, and Okhrim Sudomora. Rather than mere entertainment, these journals served as constructive opposition to the authorities, using wit and caricature to comment on the nation’s founding moments — the declaration of independence, failed negotiations with Russia, the succession of regimes. The artists understood something vital: that laughter could unite a fractured people, and that satire was a form of nation-building. In Kyiv’s chaos, where power changed hands repeatedly, *Ґедз* became a mirror in which Ukrainians could see themselves clearly, if darkly. Writers gathered there to mock power — always a dangerous profession in this region.

Decades later, comedian Yuriy Tymoshenko lived there, and the tower acquired his nickname, as though the building had decided that surviving empire and ideology was not enough; it also wanted a career in comedy.

And then, many decades later, I arrived on the top floor.

History likes to introduce itself through dramatic gestures — murdered journalists like Heorhiy Gongadze, revolutions in orange scarves, presidents escaping in convoys of black cars. It prefers headlines because headlines are vain.

I chatted up Gongadze months before they killed him. He was running *Ukrainska Pravda* from somewhere in the city—an independent website republishing things the government didn't want reported. He was desperate, borderline incoherent, nervous in a way that people are when they understand exactly what unafraid can cost. The security services were already harassing his staff, showing up at their doors with questions about Chechen separatists and drug trafficking—the kind of questions that aren't really questions at all, just theater meant to intimidate. He'd written about it, complained to the Prosecutor General. He was trying to document his own persecution as though the record itself might save him, but you could hear the fear underneath it all—the understanding that he'd said hings that powerful people wanted unsaid, and that being right about corruption was not going to protect him.

Gongadze was a gadfly. That's what they killed him for. Not for anything he'd done, but for the simple act of biting—of being the irritant that makes power uncomfortable, that keeps it from sleeping.

[I kept writing about it](https://maidan.org.ua/arch/arch2003/1060931792.html)—the men who’d followed him, the Interior Ministry apparatus, the whole machinery of state violence. I reported on Oleh, an investigative journalist who’d also been tracking the case, and who paid a price for it: threatening calls, strip searches on trains, the State Security Service (run then by Russia) launching a criminal investigation into whether he’d published state secrets. The state’s message was clear: we know who’s asking questions, and we know how to make them stop. By then, the secret recordings had emerged—the Melnychenko tapes, Kuchma and his security chief Derkach discussing Gongadze’s disappearance in casual, profanity-laced tones. They were caught on tape, and they knew it. Which meant the machinery of denial had to work twice as hard, and twice as ruthlessly.

But real life is usually happening upstairs. Someone burns onions. Someone practices scales as an opera singer, hitting the high notes with heroic competence. Someone hauls groceries up stairs designed before elevators were considered a human right. Someone leans out a window in late spring and watches the chestnut tree bloom below, convinced for a moment that history has finally grown tired and gone elsewhere.

Naturally, this belief never lasts long in Kyiv.

This is where you would have noticed something important: an old building eventually becomes what all old buildings become if they’re lucky enough not to be demolished — a witness.

Its pink façade remains slightly theatrical. Its spire still points upward with unreasonable optimism. Beneath it passes all of modern Ukrainian history — empire, revolution, censorship, independence, betrayal, protest, invasion.

And the chestnut tree, indifferent but loyal, flowers on schedule. In Kyiv this passes for permanence.

“Under the spreading chestnut tree I sold you and you sold me:
There lie they, and here lie we
Under the spreading chestnut tree.”

―**George Orwell,[1984](https://www.goodreads.com/work/quotes/153313)**

[1](#footnote-anchor-1)

Igor Ivanovich Sikorsky (1889–1972) was a Russian-American aviation pioneer born in Kyiv. He studied engineering at the Kyiv Polytechnic Institute and by the 1910s was designing fixed-wing aircraft and early helicopters in Russia. After the Bolshevik Revolution, he emigrated to the United States in 1919, where he eventually founded the Sikorsky Aero Engineering Corporation. He is best known for designing the VS-300, the first truly practical helicopter, which he piloted himself in 1939. His single-rotor design became the standard configuration for helicopters and remains dominant today — making him, in a manner of speaking, the great-great-grandfather of all modern vertical-lift aircraft, including the Ukrainian drones that now dominate the skies above Kyiv.

[2](#footnote-anchor-2)

For a detailed scholarly examination of *Ґедз* and its companion satirical journals (*Budiak* and *Rep’iakhy*) as historical sources on the Ukrainian Revolution of 1917–1921, see Svitlana Pankova, *[Teкст, контекст і підтекст](https://antikvar.ua/tekst-kontekst-i-pidtekst)* (Text, Context, and Subtext), . Pankova’s work recovers the identities of the artists and traces the political significance of satirical graphics during the Central Rada period, treating these magazines not merely as ephemeral publications but as instruments of nation-building during Ukraine’s founding moment.
