Team USA’s mistake was thinking it could woo Russia away from China. Just ask Elbridge Colby, undersecretary of war, who can’t bring himself to say that Russia invaded Ukraine. He argues unconvincingly this is not only practical but essential strategy. Spoiler: it isn’t. China’s big mistake was thinking it could use Russia to further its own interests at the expense of western civilization everywhere. Putin made two catastrophic errors: thinking he could walk over Ukraine in 2022, and deciding that Russia becoming a vassal again—1,000 years later—of its eastern neighbor was safer than playing by civilized rules

[Image]

As for less-developed nations—the so-called global south—they don’t get a vote, because, collectively, they account for less than 20% of the world’s GDP (once you strip out China) and we’re still bailing some of them out, e.g. Argentina.

Let’s unpack this spectacular failure of strategic thinking.

Washington spent eight months trying to invent a wedge between Moscow and Beijing. The logic seemed sound: exploit historical tensions, offer economic carrots, remind Putin that Russia is a European power. Colby and others sold this vision hard—détente with Moscow, focus on real threat in Beijing. But this fundamentally misread Putin’s calculus. He isn’t shopping for the best deal—he is shopping for survival.

And here’s the kicker: even if the strategy had been sound, Team USA has become too *unwoke* to execute it. The bozos in charge can’t even keep the government running. Hard to run sophisticated great power diplomacy when you are fomenting civil war at home and deploying soldiers to large American cities.

Beijing made its own blunder. Xi thought he could harness Russian resentment as a battering ram while keeping Moscow on a short leash. Instead, Russia’s Ukraine invasion—which was supposed to last days, not years—created a junior partner so economically needy and diplomatically isolated that it became a liability. China got a dependent, not a weapon.

And Russia? Putin looked at his options after the Ukraine debacle and chose subordination. The irony is brutal: a country that spent centuries expanding eastward, that still nurses historical grievances about Mongol domination, willingly walked into economic and strategic dependence on China.

Here’s the darkest part: Russia’s elite couldn’t care less that the country’s population will essentially become a large slave labor force used by China to extract natural resources. As long as the oligarchs and siloviki keep their dachas and offshore accounts, what’s a generation or three of resource colonization?

Russia now sells oil at a discount to China, buys Chinese goods at a premium, and watches Chinese influence creep into Central Asia. Moscow calls it a “strategic partnership.” Beijing calls it whatever makes Putin feel better. Team USA calls it a problem—when it can spare attention from MAGA chaos.

Sometimes everyone loses. But Russia’s people will lose longest.

### The Algorithm Ate My Lunch

I wanted everyone to feel the way my teeth vibrated, but when I posted my glow-in-the-dark selfie only the closet mermaids noticed. I didn’t blame anyone. I knew the oceans were walking heavy-footed on the land. It was one of those afternoons when I was sure that God was the series of tubes connecting my brain to the torso of a deer. Was it possible that the self was larger than the country that birthed it? Could a mouth open large enough to accommodate the girth of a bloated nation’s whale? I hung a sign that read “Hope” on the taxidermied body of an owl and waited for applause. Children gathered around to worship, sticking microchips and slivers of sunlight in its missing, blinking eye. — [Joanna Fuhrman](https://poetrysociety.org/poems-essays/in-their-own-words/joanna-fuhrman-on-the-algorithm-ate-my-lunch)

---

Computational social scientist Petter Tornberg [built](https://www.businessinsider.com/ai-chatbots-less-toxic-social-networks-twitter-simulation-2023-11) a fake social media platform, populated it with 500 LLMs playing ordinary Americans, and watched them recreate every pathology we’ve spent twenty years complaining about—without any algorithmic nudging required.

Starting from Thomas Schelling’s 1969 checkerboard model showing how mild preferences for similar neighbors produce total segregation, Tornberg extended the logic to online communities. Turns out digital spaces segregate even faster than physical ones. The integrated state is just unstable. One person leaves, the neighborhood tips, cascade complete.

**People act against their own stated preferences due to problems of self-control and commitment**.

Then came the main event: LLMs with backstories from actual voter surveys, turned loose on a bare-bones platform where they could post, repost, and follow each other. No engagement algorithms. No trigger bubbles. Just agents interacting.

Results? Echo chambers formed immediately—Democrats and Republicans stopped following each other. Attention distributed via power law—a few users dominated while most got ignored. The social media prism emerged—extreme voices accumulated the most followers. Everything we hate about TikTok, generated organically from basic human (or human-simulating) behavior.

Tornberg tried fixes: chronological timelines, hiding political affiliations, showing constructive posts first, even showing the *least* liked posts first. Nothing worked. Some interventions made things worse. The structural outcomes proved robust to perturbation, like the Schelling effect on steroids.

The kicker: platforms aren’t just reflecting our preferences, they’re reshaping our politics by defining who gets attention. Tornberg’s separate study of politicians across countries found **radical right populists specifically leveraging misinformation as strategy**, that is, conspiricism, intertwined with social media’s attention economy. We recall that The New York Times started writing clickbait headlines on Bill Keller’s watch.

So no, you can’t just log off. The incentive structures are leaking into everything—media, politics, culture. We moved from Fordist machine epistemology (society as designed system) to complexity epistemology (**society as self-organizing swarm**), and discovered that emergent outcomes still embed power structures. They’re just harder to see.

Tornberg’s nostalgic for ‘90s internet—ICQ’s “talk to random strangers” button, genuine connection, pre-commercialized chaos. **Maybe we need fundamental redesign**, not cosmetic algorithm tweaks. But nobody’s quite figured out how to build platforms that don’t automatically generate exactly the problems we’re trying to escape.
