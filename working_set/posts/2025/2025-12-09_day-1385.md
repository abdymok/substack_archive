The fall of the Berlin Wall validated the open society model for, er, at least ten years, maybe 15, depending on how you count. The Revolution of Dignity came after a years-long malaise following the Orange Revolution. It marked the end of the democratic recession.

The West still retains considerable leverage over Russian weakness, economic and military. Victory for Moscow is far from inevitable. But we might first answer a more fundamental question: Why does one combatant investigate its own corruption during existential crisis while the other simply murders investigators and calls the result statecraft?

The answer, of course, is that corruption *costs wars*. It siphons funds from ammunition to oligarchs’ pockets, places incompetents in command positions through bribery rather than merit, and corrodes the citizen’s willingness to sacrifice when they know officials are enriching themselves. Ukraine investigates corruption precisely *because* it’s fighting for survival—every hryvnia stolen from defense procurement is a Ukrainian life endangered. Russia’s model—Putin’s palace, his cronies’ yachts, the systematic looting of state resources—is not a sign of strength but of a military that can afford to waste men like ammunition because it views both as equally expendable.

The tragedy is that Ukraine’s necessary anti-corruption efforts weaken it at the precise moment the country needs it most. Russia suffers no such impediment because it has simply abandoned the pretense that the state serves anyone beyond the kleptocrats who own it. One might call this an advantage, until one recalls that this very corruption is why Russian logistics collapse, why generals send untrained conscripts into minefields, why Moscow must recruit North Korean infantry to sustain offensives.

Back to first principles, then. And not a moment too soon.
