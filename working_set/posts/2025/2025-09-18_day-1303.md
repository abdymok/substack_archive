Yesterday, I listened to Lukashenko opine on historiography.

> "We understand that Belarus, a third of a century into its sovereign history, is still very young. The history of a sovereign state is only just beginning," he said. ([Belta](https://belta.by/president/view/lukashenko-orientiruet-na-edinuju-strategiju-formirovanija-istoricheskoj-pamjati-737783-2025/). September 17, 2025)

The Belarusian dictator said historians analyze documents and facts, constructing a chronology of events. The ideological task, he emphasized, is to transform this material into narratives, arguments and counterarguments, and work with them in the information field. He used the unconstitutional referendum in early 1995 as an example.

> "Here, scholars, educators, journalists, and experts must act within the framework of a unified strategy for the formation of historical memory," he said.

[Image]

That was 30 years ago, when Viktor Sheiman was the hatchetman of the make-Belarus-a-dictatorship project. Kash reminded me of Viktor.

What Lukashenko articulated wasn't novel—it was a playbook. The same playbook that began in Minsk in 1995, moved to Moscow in 2000, and now defines The Golden Age of Ameriʞa.

The pattern: seize the historical narrative first, then create a "unified strategy for the formation of historical memory." Politicians, scholars, educators, journalists, photographers, comedians, cartoonists and experts fall in line—or get replaced.

Lukashenko's casual reference to transforming historical material into "narratives, arguments and counterarguments" for deployment "in the information field" reveals how authoritarians view truth: as raw material to be processed, shaped, weaponized.

This is what I meant when I wrote that under illiberalism, "the future is the past and where cranes fear to tread, the bulldozer will boldly go." The bulldozer doesn't just destroy buildings—it levels the landscape of memory itself[1](#footnote-1).

The unconstitutional referendum wasn't just politics—it was a laboratory experiment in rewriting reality in real time. Thirty years later, he's still perfecting the formula.

## The Sophistication Problem

Those shocked by rising demagoguery should remember we're dealing with leaders "vaccinated" by social media algorithms against progress. Why use force when you can rewrite what happened? Why break bodies when you can break the collective capacity to distinguish truth from fiction?

The most chilling aspect is the matter-of-fact assumption that of course historians should serve power, of course memory should be managed, of course truth should be strategic.

After thirty years of practice, it's no longer ideology—it's just technique.

When the population internalizes the habit of accepting manufactured memory as authentic experience, the work is done. The most unpleasant consequence of patrimonialism is exactly what Lukashenko described: institutionalized historical revisionism.

The Kash-Viktor connection isn't casual. Both represent the operational hatchetmen of authoritarianism—people who transform crude power instincts into systematic methodology.

We're moving toward a new normal, but not the one we hoped for. The arrow of historical time is accelerating, but acceleration just means reaching the destination faster. And the destination, if current trends hold, is a world where confidence about controlling historical memory becomes universal standard.

The template spreads. The memory police are hiring.
