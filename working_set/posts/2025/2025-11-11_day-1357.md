So that’s the external threat: genocidal bikers on a border-crossing field trip. Now for the internal one, which is less Mad Max and more clownish, but just as corrosive[1](#footnote-1).

The president’s former business partner and birthday buddy, Tymur Mindich, fled the country yesterday hours before anti-corruption agents raided dozens of offices and residences as part of a 15-month long investigation into an elaborate kickback scheme run by an organized criminal group. Former Energy Minister, Herman Halushchenko, currently Ukraine’s Justice Minister, is a suspect. Har, har[2](#footnote-2).

There are more than **a thousand hours** of recorded conversations backing up the charges, investigators [said](https://t.me/nab_ukraine/3504)[3](#footnote-3).

That’s not a data point. It’s a mountain range.

**One thousand hours** equals **sixty thousand minutes**. Conversational Russian—the language that they speak—runs around 180 words per minute, but only about seventy percent of that time is active speech—people pause, interrupt, repeat, mumble, smoke. Do the math and you get roughly 42,000 minutes of actual talking, or about **7.5 million words**.

That’s thirteen *War and Peaces*, or eighty-four average novels—all dictated by men who assumed no one was listening.

And that’s *before* transcription. Even with software and 4× playback, it would take months to process. Listening is one thing; understanding is another. The work isn’t to *hear* the words—it’s to hear what they mean, in context, through the evasions and half-sentences.

Anyone who has ever tried to transcribe a real conversation knows that speech is mud. Overlaps, fillers, grunts, the sound of a chair scraping—everything matters. Meaning hides in rhythm and omission. A “hmm” can signal agreement, contempt, or the start of an heart attack.

Now imagine **seven and a half million** of those words, traded between men who know when not to say the *thing* itself. What sounds like boredom may be fear; what sounds like a joke may be an order.

Western analysts during the early 2000s failed miserably to keep pace with Kuchma’s conversational jazz. Two decades later, the problem isn’t comprehension—it’s scale. The signal hasn’t vanished; it’s drowned in abundance.

Until those thousand hours are released, we can only picture the investigators listening again and again, headphones on, mapping the contours of corruption—one strategic state enterprise at a time.
