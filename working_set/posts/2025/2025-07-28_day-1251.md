Reading this through Grice's framework, the moral becomes quite clear:

Franklin's opening paragraph systematically violates [Grice's maxims](https://www.sas.upenn.edu/~haroldfs/dravling/grice.html) while maintaining the surface appearance of cooperative communication. He violates **quantity** by omitting crucial context (that journalists like himself created Z's "mythic reputation"). He violates **quality** by presenting a misleading narrative unsupported by the fuller evidence. He violates **relation** by burying the key point about Trump's pressure and the mineral deal. And he violates **manner** through selective presentation that obscures rather than clarifies.

But here's the deeper issue: Franklin likely expects readers to *recognize* these intentions - he's not accidentally misleading but strategically framing. Yet he's counting on readers *not* to consciously deliberate about his bullshit or remember what he wrote four months ago[1](#footnote-1)[2](#footnote-2).

**The moral**: When communicative trust is weaponized, when someone deliberately violates Gricean maxims while relying on our automatic assumption of good faith, critical reading requires us to temporarily suspend that default trust. We need to consciously examine not just *what* is being communicated, but *how* and *why* - even though life is too short for constant vigilance.

Sometimes we must make the invisible visible, even if briefly.
