*Those lips, that brain, this delusion[1](#footnote-1)*

The difference between everyone knowing something and everyone knowing that everyone else knows it sounds like academic hairsplitting until you realize it’s the difference between whispered corridors and newspaper headlines. Between maintaining a fiction and admitting a truth.

Take this proposition: *Ukraine’s wartime leadership has become increasingly authoritarian while its Western allies either don’t notice or don’t care.*

Until recently, this was merely mutual knowledge—diplomats discussed it in secure rooms, journalists alluded to it carefully, Ukrainian MPs grumbled privately. Everyone knew it, but nobody was sure that everyone else knew that they knew it.

Then came July’s cardboard revolution. A small protest near Z’s compound with handwritten signs: “You aren’t a tsar” and “If you stole less, I’d bury my friends less.” Like the child in Andersen’s tale, these protesters pointed and said what everyone could see: the democratic clothes weren’t there.

The moment was transformative not because of what it revealed—everyone already knew about the power consolidation, attacks on anti-corruption agencies, increasingly personalized rule—but because it made this knowledge *common*. Suddenly, everyone knew that everyone else had seen the signs.

What’s deliciously ironic is how Trump’s about-face on Ukraine promises to make things even worse. As America signals its willingness to sell Ukraine a bunch of weapons, the incentives for maintaining even democratic pretense are evaporating. Why worry about oversight when Washington values “getting things done”? Why fret about press freedom when your most important ally campaigns against “fake news”?

[Image]

The common knowledge problem works in reverse too. Just as everyone knowing that everyone knows about democratic backsliding can catalyze change, everyone knowing that everyone knows that *nobody really cares* can catalyze acceleration. When European diplomats express private concerns while maintaining public support, when Western media writes careful analyses while avoiding condemnation, when Ukrainian MPs grumble quietly while voting in line—this creates common knowledge of complicity.

Trump offers Z something invaluable: a role model who successfully transitioned from con man to strongman while keeping key constituencies happy. The new transactional US-Ukraine relationship means results matter more than process, outcomes more than institutions. Why maintain the exhausting fiction of sharing liberal values when autocratic efficiency gets better reviews from your most important audience?

Z’s new clothes are becoming invisible just when visibility matters least.

In Andersen’s fairy tale, the child’s observation breaks the spell because everyone suddenly realizes they’ve been participating in collective self-deception. But what happens when the revelation comes just as the kingdom decides that naked emperors are actually rather fashionable? What happens when common knowledge of democratic decay coincides with common knowledge that such decay is the price of doing business?

The answer is that the clothes disappear entirely. And everyone pretends not to notice, because noticing has become inconvenient to the new consensus. The child pointing at the naked emperor isn’t brave anymore—they’re just rude.

*Mutual knowledge becomes common knowledge through simultaneous revelation. But sometimes, common knowledge of uncomfortable truths is less powerful than common knowledge of comfortable lies.*
