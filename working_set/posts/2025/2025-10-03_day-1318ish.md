I was time travelling in still frames.

Meanwhile, someone was nice enough to put me back on YouTube’s family premium plan, enabling me to [listen](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=IN5lTJtkZrA), ad-free, to Kiril Rogov talk about his essay, titled “Military Dead End” ([Военный Тупик](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=IN5lTJtkZrA))[1](#footnote-1).

Here’s the nut graph:

> A military **stalemate** is a new framework that will shape how both Russia and the international community understand the current stage of the conflict. It will strip away arguments from those opposed to military aid to Ukraine, increase discipline within the sanctions coalition, change the agenda of potential negotiations on freezing the conflict, and force Putin to face a difficult choice: a deep financial crisis by the end of next year or abandoning another attempt to seize northern Donbas.

Kirill traces how understanding of the war has evolved through several phases. Initially, Russian military superiority seemed absolute. By summer 2022, it became clear Russia lacked a war-ready army. Ukraine’s autumn counteroffensive suggested Western support could tip the balance. However, Ukraine’s failed 2023 offensive showed Russia had stabilized its military operations, leading to the “war of attrition” framework that dominated thinking for two years.

This framework assumed Russia would eventually prevail due to superior manpower and economic resources. However, several factors prevented this outcome: Putin’s reluctance to conduct broad mobilization, drone technology neutralizing numerical advantages, and continued Western support for Ukraine. As former Ukrainian Commander-in-Chief Valeriy Zaluzhny argued in 2023, technological capabilities have reached a stage—similar to World War I—where offensive breakthroughs are impossible without new technological advances[2](#footnote-2).

So, stalemate.

Next up, Anne, who in an interview with the [Evropeiska Pravda](https://www.eurointegration.com.ua/eng/interview/2025/10/2/7221502/) ezine doesn’t directly address Russia’s military capabilities, but her comments about Europe needing to take ownership of its defense suggest she also sees Russia as a manageable threat if properly confronted[3](#footnote-3).

While Kirill credits continued Western (especially European) support as crucial to preventing Ukraine’s defeat in the “war of attrition,” Anne explicitly challenges the premise that “the US is the key to Ukraine’s security,” noting that European financial and military contributions actually exceed American aid when properly calculated. She, like [Timothy Snyder](https://yes-ukraine.org/en/photo-and-video/video/shchorichna-zustrich-yaltinskoyi-yevropeyskoyi-strategiyi-yes-yak-zavershiti-viynu/shcho-istoriki-vvazhatimut-ukrayinskoyu-peremogoyu), argues that waiting for Team USA to solve the problem is dangerous.

According to The [Economist](https://www.economist.com/briefing/2025/10/02/why-russias-micro-aggressions-against-europe-are-proliferating), Russia’s is deploying grey-zone tactics precisely *because* conventional warfare has stalled[4](#footnote-4). The drone incursions, airspace violations, and sabotage operations across Europe aren’t signs of strength—they’re strategic pivots born from battlefield failure. As Romania’s defense minister notes: “They cannot make even one step ahead in Ukraine.”

Russia’s grey-zone escalation actually validates both Kirill’s **stalemate** thesis and Applebaum’s argument. If Russia truly had conventional military superiority, it wouldn’t need to fly drones over Danish oilfields or hack Norwegian dams. These are tactics of weakness masquerading as intimidation.

Anduril Industries’ Barracuda missile family can end this mess in a couple of months.
