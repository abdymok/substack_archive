Around the 55-minute mark, the conversation pivots to Russia and Ukraine. Brace yourself.

> Putin has huge respect for President Donald Trump. And you saw what happened in the Oval Office between Z and the president. Disrespecting him is not a healthy way to have a good relationship… [Ukraine] is dependent on us, and we have been so good to them. — *Steven*

Who, exactly, is *we*?

Steven assures us that achieving “an ultimate ceasefire” is complicated. He then demonstrates exactly why — starting with Kursk.

> First, there is Kursk, where Ukrainian troops are surrounded. Fact. Kursk is within Russia. The Russians have taken it back. And they have people trapped there and the president doesn’t want to see everyone getting killed. — *Steven*

Actually, no. Intelligence shared with the White House indicated that Ukrainian forces were not encircled. (Steven’s defense: *“A lot of these newspaper stories about Ukraine are agenda-driven.”* Convenient.)

Then comes the geography portion of the interview. Steven identifies what he calls “the largest issue in the conflict” — “these so-called four regions, Donbas, Crimea… uh… Luhansk… uh… and there are two others…”

There are. Steven cannot name them.

> They are Russian-speaking. There have been referendums held there, and the overwhelming majority of residents have indicated that they want to be under Russian rule… I think this is the key issue in the conflict. — *Steven*

Actually, no — again. There have been no legitimate acts of self-determination in any part of Ukraine, including Crimea, that call into question Ukraine’s sovereignty over its own territory. None. Zero.

If Steven wants to adopt Moscow’s reading of events as his own, he owes the rest of us an explanation. Without one, he is simply another Kremlin stooge — distinguished from Tucker mainly by the business card.
