> […] Our government doesn't listen to those who are smarter and more experienced than it. Advice from smarter people irritates the government and shows its level, and it gets offended. Secondly, you can lead a horse to water, but you can't make it drink. It's the same with the government. You can advise it or criticize it, but it doesn't want to hear advice and listen to criticism, because it believes that it is the smartest, and Ukraine began in 2019. The Dunning-Kruger effect in all its glory. Although sometimes it reacts to criticism to stop the rating from falling. Our government reacts to fluctuations in the rating. And thirdly, I tried. I gave advice and ideas. I suggested solutions. I formulated step-by-step action plans. But the result was zero, because I am not loyal and I am talking about the mistakes of the authorities. And our authorities do not need smart people who point out mistakes. Our authorities need loyal sycophants who silently support everything. …Volodymyr Oleksandrovych does not want to hear advice, but wants to hear that he is right. Sigh.

To show solidarity, I asked three LLMs to cook up an op-ed less than 1,000 words from at least one half dozen random posts appearing on this Substack.

**This is what it spit out:**

> When experts systematically violate basic rules of honest communication while maintaining authority, we must jettison our default trust in "institutional wisdom." In a media landscape where being spectacularly wrong about Ukraine practically qualifies you to explain Ukraine, skepticism isn't just healthy—it's survival.
>
> The same talking heads who missed everything are still everywhere, recycling busted frameworks, mistranslating policy and citing themselves. People who got everything wrong remain on every panel, confidently narrating a war they swore would end by the weekend. The recipe for disaster hasn't changed: Putin thought Ukrainians wouldn't fight, Ukraine thought the West would fight and the West thought it could avoid fighting altogether. Every assumption was wrong, yet these miscalculations still flavor every bad decision and worse analysis.
>
> **Language as Battlefield**
>
> Language became another battlefield. U.S. officials stopped calling the invasion "imminent" while Ukrainian officials declared victory—because they thought "imminent" meant "inevitable." It doesn't. "Imminent" means soon; "inevitable" means it will happen, period. Meanwhile, Ukraine's Commander-in-Chief couldn't thank America "just for being America" as the fourth U.S. weapons plane landed in Kyiv. Words mattered—just not to those using them.
>
> **Ukrainian Political Reality**
>
> For a masterclass in Ukrainian political reality, observe the "thug life" in Kyiv's corridors of power. Ukraine's politics are less *House of Cards*, more street brawl. Take Ivan Bakanov, sacked as State Security Service of Ukraine (SBU) head, and Iryna Venedyktova, Ukraine's Prosecutor General—both axed the same day in July 2022. No one knows what Bakanov did (least of all Bakanov), but everyone suspected he wasn't good at it. Venedyktova spent more time chasing political rivals than rooting out corruption. Their departures produced shrugs, not shock—careers end not with bangs but unexplained boots out the door.
>
> This is the lived reality behind headlines Western experts claim to decipher. While analysts churn out war-adjacent think pieces from home offices, the real story involves institutional rot, personal ambition and leaders improvising through fog.
>
> **Community Theater Intelligence**
>
> Institutional analysis became less "intelligence community," more "community theater." Analysts warned of Russian invasion, citing Belarus's "de facto absorption"—diplomatic Anschluss straight from 1938. Yet America's top brass predicted Kyiv would fall in 72 hours. The "experts" doubled down with what some call "non-predictive intelligence floppery"—a phrase that sounds like lost Monty Python. Some cited their own previous work, creating recursive echo chambers bordering on performance art.
>
> **Democratic Discourse Failure**
>
> The real problem isn't just bureaucratic incompetence—it's fundamental failure of democratic discourse. Months before tanks rolled, voices noted the obvious: many Ukraine supporters didn't think the president was qualified, but nobody said it aloud. That mutual knowledge never became common knowledge. Rather than face reality, elite consensus doubled down: "He's not naked; you're just not looking hard enough."
>
> The expert ecosystem around Ukraine analysis was toxic from the start. The best label for these folks wasn't "analyst" but "cannon fodder"—pundits who never risked anything except being spectacularly wrong. Many suffered imposter syndrome, but uniquely, they were the only ones who didn't realize it.
>
> **Breaking Patterns**
>
> What's unique about this war is how quickly old patterns broke down. This wasn't power balancing—it was mass murder, confirmed during "slow dark blackout days in Kyiv" as Russia targeted civilian infrastructure. Putin doesn't eat because he's hungry; he eats because he's afraid. Fear has large eyes. Good luck applying conventional deterrence theories to "oligophrens and psychopaths."
>
> Meanwhile, the Western expert class keeps spinning. Beltway warbloggers and think-tankers continue circular self-citation, producing commentary less about Ukraine than maintaining their own relevance. In Ukrainian politics' "thug life" reality, their expertise looks less like insight, more like cosplay—performance for each other, not the public.
>
> **The Real Lesson**
>
> After 1252 days, the real lesson isn't about Russia, Ukraine, or the West—it's about institutions and personalities who, having failed upward, still hold the microphone.
>
> In this war, as in many others, honesty—not expertise—remains the rarest and most valuable form of accuracy. If Ukraine's brutal, improvisational politics have taught us anything, it's that reality will always outpace narratives experts try to impose. The sooner we accept that, the better chance we have to understand what's actually happening—not just in Ukraine, but anywhere stakes are this high and experts this wrong.
