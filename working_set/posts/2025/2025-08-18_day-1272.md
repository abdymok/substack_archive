**The Performance of Agency**

Two years into his presidency, Z began invoking Ukrainian "subjectiveness" (субъектність) to describe Ukraine's foreign policy position. He insisted Ukraine was "definitely becoming more like subjects of political activity."

But almost every time Z invoked this term, he was describing the opposite of reality. True political subjects reshape their environments—they don't spend months lying about special operations, covering up corruption and ignoring invasion warnings.

As I recall, Bellingcat's Christo Grozev watched the June 2021 interview “fifty times,” particularly the Wagnergate bits, and concluded the president meant "sovereignty," not "subjectiveness." The distinction matters: sovereignty can be performed through treaties and ceremonies, but subjectiveness requires actual agency to transform political systems.

George Bernard Shaw would have recognized this pattern immediately. In 1914, he dismantled Britain's pretensions about fighting for Belgian neutrality. Britain had spent a decade militarizing against Germany, then claimed moral outrage when Germany took the bait. "We cannot shout for years that we are boys of the bulldog breed, and then suddenly pose as gazelles," Shaw wrote.

Shaw's insight was devastating because it came from an outsider—an Irishman who understood imperial dynamics without psychological investment in British exceptionalism. He saw what English patriots couldn't: their country was trapped in rivalry patterns with inexorable logic.

On July 12, 2021 Putin produced a [7,000-word treatise](http://en.kremlin.ru/events/president/news/66181) explaining how Russians and Ukrainians “are one people — a single whole.” From that moment Russian propaganda of war has never ceased. Yet when Russian tanks approached Kyiv, Team USA and Europe convinced themselves they were reluctantly defending international law. The performance had become the policy.

Ukraine, meanwhile, was posing like a gazelle. Ukraine officials dismissed unfavorable intelligence as "fake news" designed to "sow panic." Political editors complained about foreign coverage making Ukrainians "nervous." Even Russian Security Council Secretary Patrushev noted the irony: "Ukrainians themselves, including officials, say there is no threat."

The cruel paradox: the more Z insists on Ukrainian "subjectiveness," the harder it becomes to acknowledge the structural dependencies that actually govern Ukrainian policy options. The rhetoric enabling psychological survival prevents the strategic realism that might enable actual survival.

Shaw's solution was characteristically brutal: strip away moral theater and face the mutual culpabilities making diplomatic resolution possible. For Ukraine, this would mean distinguishing between performing agency and building it—but that would require abandoning the very discourse that makes current policy psychologically tolerable.

Political science textbooks may define subjectiveness clearly, but confusing it with sovereignty can be fatal.
