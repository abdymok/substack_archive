# Annotated bibliography: Ukraine and the war, 2023

*Working draft · v0.1 · compiled from the 2023 Substack archive and its linked-reference dataset*

## Scope and method

The 2023 archive contains 243 posts and 1,248 external and internal link occurrences. This bibliography is a selective guide to sources relevant to the war, Ukraine's political and military position, international support, accountability, and the information environment. It is not a list of every link in the archive.

Selection is based on a combination of:
- recurrence in the archive;
- importance as a primary document, official record, investigation, specialist analysis, or substantial field report;
- relevance to a major event or argument in the year's posts;
- value as a source that the archive explicitly questions or disputes.

The number of links records how often a URL appears in the 2023 linked-reference file. It is not a measure of reliability, quality, or readership. A source's inclusion does not imply endorsement. Publication date and the date on which the archive linked to it are shown separately. Some sources published before 2023 were reused as background.

This first pass is Ukraine-war focused. Personal, cultural, literary, running, and other non-war sources are outside its scope for now.

## Primary documents, official positions, and accountability

### 1. International Criminal Court. “Situation in Ukraine: ICC judges issue arrest warrants against Vladimir Vladimirovich Putin and Maria Alekseyevna Lvova-Belova.” 17 March 2023.
[ICC press release](https://www.icc-cpi.int/news/situation-ukraine-icc-judges-issue-arrest-warrants-against-vladimir-vladimirovich-putin-and)

The primary public record of the Pre-Trial Chamber's warrants concerning the alleged unlawful deportation and transfer of Ukrainian children from occupied territory to Russia. Essential for precise wording: the warrants concern alleged crimes and the Court's finding of reasonable grounds, not a conviction. Linked in the archive on 18 March.

### 2. Office of the Director of National Intelligence. “2023 Annual Threat Assessment of the U.S. Intelligence Community.” March 2023.
[Annual Threat Assessment](https://www.dni.gov/index.php/newsroom/reports-publications/reports-publications-2023/3676-2023-annual-threat-assessment-of-the-u-s-intelligence-community)

A consolidated statement of the U.S. intelligence community's public assessment of threats, including Russia and the war against Ukraine. Useful as an official baseline for what the U.S. government was prepared to say publicly, rather than as a substitute for battlefield reporting. Linked on 9 March.

### 3. Inspectors General of the U.S. Department of Defense, Department of State, and USAID. “Joint Strategic Oversight Plan for the Ukraine Response.” January 2023.
[Joint Strategic Oversight Plan (PDF)](https://oig.usaid.gov/sites/default/files/2023-01/Joint%20Strategic%20Oversight%20Plan%20for%20Ukraine%20Response_0.pdf)

A 36-page interagency plan setting out oversight of the U.S. response to Russia's invasion. It is a map of planned audit and investigative work, not a finding that assistance was properly spent or that every risk had been resolved. The archive uses it to examine the oversight machinery behind U.S. assistance. Linked on 8 February.

### 4. Ministry of Foreign Affairs of the People's Republic of China. “China's Position on the Political Settlement of the Ukraine Crisis.” 24 February 2023.
[China's position paper](https://www.fmprc.gov.cn/eng/wjb/zzjg_663340/dozys_664276/xwlb_664278/202302/t20230224_11030713.html)

The Chinese government's 12-point position paper, covering sovereignty, ceasefire, negotiations, sanctions, grain exports, nuclear risks, and reconstruction. Read as a statement of Beijing's diplomatic position, not as a neutral account of the war or a negotiated settlement. Linked on 24 February.

### 5. Group of Seven. “G7: Joint declaration of support for Ukraine.” 12 July 2023.
[Joint declaration](https://www.consilium.europa.eu/en/press/press-releases/2023/07/12/g7-joint-declaration-of-support-for-ukraine/)

The G7 leaders' collective statement on Ukraine's sovereignty, security, and long-term support. Important as a record of commitments and diplomatic language; it should be distinguished from specific weapons deliveries, funding appropriations, or binding security guarantees. Linked in the archive on 17 July.

### 6. Office of the UN High Commissioner for Human Rights. Independent International Commission of Inquiry on Ukraine.
[Commission page](https://www.ohchr.org/en/hr-bodies/hrc/iicihr-ukraine/index)

The institutional entry point for the Human Rights Council's independent commission investigating alleged violations in the context of Russia's aggression against Ukraine. Useful for locating the Commission's mandate and reporting. The archive linked the commission page on 5 September; this entry is not a substitute for citing a particular report or finding.

### 7. International Committee of the Red Cross. “Protocol Additional to the Geneva Conventions of 12 August 1949, and relating to the Protection of Victims of International Armed Conflicts (Protocol I).” 8 June 1977.
[Additional Protocol I](https://ihl-databases.icrc.org/en/ihl-treaties/api-1977)

A foundational treaty text for the protection of civilians and the conduct of hostilities. It is a legal reference, not a report on whether a particular incident violated the law. Linked in the archive on 7 June.

## Strategic and specialist analysis

### 8. Swedish Defence Research Agency (FOI). “Russia's War Against Ukraine and the West: The First Year.” FOI-R--5479--SE. 20 June 2023.
[FOI report summary](https://www.foi.se/en/foi/reports/report-summary.html?reportNo=FOI-R--5479--SE)

A 120-page multi-author assessment of the political, economic, military, and wider security consequences of the first year of the full-scale invasion. Valuable for its breadth and its focus on Russia and the international security environment. The archive's post discussing it also challenges omissions and framing in the report; it should therefore be read as a substantial analytical source, not adopted uncritically. Linked on 21 June.

### 9. Institute for the Study of War. “Russian Offensive Campaign Assessment, June 13, 2023.” 13 June 2023.
[ISW assessment](https://www.understandingwar.org/backgrounder/russian-offensive-campaign-assessment-june-13-2023)

One dated instalment in ISW's continuing operational assessments. Useful for tracking the institute's contemporaneous interpretation of Russian and Ukrainian operations, claims, and battlefield developments. As with any think-tank assessment, distinguish reported events from the institute's own analysis and projections. Linked twice on 15 June.

### 10. Institute for the Study of War. “The Kremlin's Irregular Army: Ukrainian Separatist Order of Battle.” 2023.
[ISW backgrounder](https://www.understandingwar.org/backgrounder/kremlin%E2%80%99s-irregular-army-ukrainian-separatist-order-battle)

A specialist reference on Russia's use of irregular and proxy formations and the armed structures associated with the occupation of parts of Ukraine. Useful background for understanding the relationship between Russian forces and nominally local formations. Linked on 7 May.

### 11. Institute for the Study of War. “The High Price of Losing Ukraine: The Military Threat and Beyond,” Part 2. December 2023.
[ISW report](https://understandingwar.org/backgrounder/high-price-losing-ukraine-part-2-%E2%80%94-military-threat-and-beyond)

A strategic argument about the consequences of a Russian victory or Ukrainian defeat, extending beyond the immediate battlefield. Best treated as an advocacy-oriented strategic assessment: useful for understanding the case being made to Western audiences, but not a disinterested forecast. Linked twice on 24 December.

### 12. Watling, Jack. “The Ukraine War Has Found the Machinery of Western Governments Wanting.” Royal United Services Institute, 8 August 2023.
[RUSI commentary](https://www.rusi.org/explore-our-research/publications/commentary/ukraine-war-has-found-machinery-western-governments-wanting)

An analysis of delays and coordination failures in Western decisions on training, equipment, ammunition, and industrial capacity. Particularly useful for connecting battlefield requirements to the time needed for governments and defence industries to respond. The author writes from a defence-policy perspective; the piece is commentary, not an audit. Linked on 9 August.

### 13. Ukraine Economic Outlook. “Annual Report 2022.”
[Annual report](https://www.ukraine-economic-outlook.com/annual-report-2022)

A domestic economic-research source used in the archive as background on Ukraine's economic trajectory. The report covers 2022, although it was linked in a 2023 post. Retain the publication period when citing figures; do not present them as 2023 data. Linked on 17 April.

## Reporting, investigations, and field accounts

### 14. Daalder, Ivo H., and James Goldgeier. “The Long War in Ukraine: The West Needs to Plan for a Protracted Conflict With Russia.” *Foreign Affairs*, 9 January 2023.
[Foreign Affairs essay](https://www.foreignaffairs.com/ukraine/long-war-ukraine-russia-protracted-conflict)

An influential early-year argument about the need for Western policy to plan for a protracted conflict. The archive links to it twice on 10 January and explicitly disputes its framing and conclusions. It belongs here as a source in the debate, not as an endorsed position. Note: the 2023 archive text describes it as published in 2022; the publication date is 9 January 2023.

### 15. “U.S. Warms to Helping Ukraine Target Crimea.” *The New York Times*, 18 January 2023.
[New York Times report](https://www.nytimes.com/2023/01/18/us/politics/ukraine-crimea-military.html)

Reporting on a shift in U.S. thinking about Ukrainian military operations against Russian-occupied Crimea. The archive uses the article's phrase “a Russian sanctuary” as a point of dispute, challenging language that can obscure Crimea's status as internationally recognized Ukrainian territory. Linked twice on 19 January.

### 16. “Ukraine Prepares for Renewed Russian Offensive.” *Financial Times*, 5 February 2023.
[Financial Times report](https://www.ft.com/content/268bd522-4794-4f3d-895f-f58a55536af9)

An early-2023 report on warnings of a renewed Russian offensive, particularly in the eastern Donbas. Useful as a record of expectations and threat assessments at the beginning of the year. Linked on 8 February.

### 17. “Ukraine War Plans Leak Prompts Pentagon Investigation.” *The New York Times*, 6 April 2023.
[New York Times report](https://www.nytimes.com/2023/04/06/us/politics/ukraine-war-plan-russia.html)

Coverage of classified U.S. and NATO documents appearing on social media, and the resulting Pentagon investigation. Useful for reconstructing what the leaked documents did and did not disclose, and for the operational-security debate that followed. Linked three times on 7 April.

### 18. “The Counteroffensive.” *The Atlantic*, June 2023 issue.
[The Atlantic feature](https://www.theatlantic.com/magazine/archive/2023/06/counteroffensive-ukraine-Z-crimea/673781/)

A long-form essay on Ukraine's counteroffensive and the political and strategic stakes surrounding it. The archive describes it as worth reading while noting what it leaves out about Ukraine's internal governance. Linked on 4 May.

### 19. “Two Weeks at the Front in Ukraine.” *The New Yorker*, 22 May 2023.
[The New Yorker report](https://www.newyorker.com/magazine/2023/05/29/two-weeks-at-the-front-in-ukraine)

A frontline account of infantry conditions in the Donbas, including the exposure of soldiers to artillery, missiles, grenades, and helicopter fire. Provides a ground-level counterweight to high-level strategic discussion. Linked in the archive on 24 May.

### 20. Smart, Jason Jay. “How Ukraine's Air Defense Spots Russian Missiles.” *Kyiv Post*, 22 May 2023.
[Kyiv Post interview](https://www.kyivpost.com/post/17322)

An explanatory interview with military-technology researcher Mauro Gilli on radar, detection, tracking, interception, and Ukraine's layered air-defence response. A useful technical primer, though it is an expert interview rather than an operational disclosure from Ukraine's air-defence command. Linked on 23 May.

### 21. “Ukraine Sends Conflicting Signals on Crimea Talks Idea.” Reuters, 6 April 2023.
[Reuters report](https://www.reuters.com/world/europe/ukraine-sends-conflicting-signals-crimea-talks-idea-2023-04-06/)

A report on public statements about the conditions under which Russia might leave Crimea and the question of negotiations. The archive uses it alongside direct statements from Ukrainian officials, making it useful for distinguishing a reported diplomatic debate from the government's stated position. Linked on 7 April.

### 22. “In Rush to Arm Ukraine, Weapons Are Bought but Not Delivered, or Too Broken to Use.” *The New York Times*, 19 June 2023.
[New York Times investigation](https://www.nytimes.com/2023/06/19/world/europe/ukraine-weapons-howitzers-contracts.html)

An investigation into procurement and delivery problems affecting Western military assistance. Important for the gap between pledged or purchased equipment and what Ukrainian forces can actually use. The archive cites it while making its own critical argument about the quality of supplied equipment; the article should not be generalized into a claim that all Western aid was defective. Linked twice on 19 June.

### 23. “Russia-Ukraine War: Live — Dam Near Kherson Blown Up by Russian Forces, Ukrainian Military Says.” *The Guardian*, 6 June 2023.
[The Guardian live coverage](https://www.theguardian.com/world/live/2023/jun/06/russia-ukraine-war-live-dam-near-kherson-blown-up-by-russian-forces-ukrainian-military-says)

Contemporaneous live coverage of the destruction of the Kakhovka dam, as the first reports and official claims emerged. Useful as a time-stamped record of the information environment on the day; for attribution and technical conclusions, it should be read alongside later investigations and official findings. Linked on 6 June.

### 24. “Ukrainian Orphans Taste Freedom After Fleeing Russian Occupation.” Reuters, 4 August 2023.
[Reuters report](https://www.reuters.com/world/europe/ukrainian-orphans-taste-freedom-after-fleeing-russian-occupation-2023-08-04/)

A reported account of children escaping Russian occupation. Adds an individual and humanitarian dimension to the record of occupation, displacement, and the return of Ukrainian children. Linked on 8 August.

### 25. “Ukrainian Military Officer Coordinated Nord Stream Pipeline Attack.” *The Washington Post*, 11 November 2023.
[Washington Post investigation](https://www.washingtonpost.com/national-security/2023/11/11/nordstream-bombing-ukraine-chervinsky/)

An influential report alleging a central role for Ukrainian officer Roman Chervinsky in the Nord Stream sabotage. The archive links to it five times, but explicitly challenges its evidentiary basis, especially its reliance on unnamed sources. Include it as an important and contested piece of reporting, not as an established account of responsibility.

### 26. “The AP Interview: Ukraine's Z Says the War With Russia Is in a New Phase as Winter Looms.” Associated Press, 1 December 2023.
[Associated Press interview](https://apnews.com/article/Z-ukraine-russia-war-interview-winter-75f1f785b17452fc23819d459e6ab64b)

A year-end interview with Ukraine's president on the state of the war as winter approached. Useful as a primary interview record of the president's public assessment, while remembering that an interview is also a political communication. Linked on 3 December.

### 27. “Miscalculations, Divisions Marked Offensive Planning by U.S., Ukraine.” *The Washington Post*, 4 December 2023.
[Washington Post report](https://www.washingtonpost.com/world/2023/12/04/ukraine-counteroffensive-us-planning-russia-war/)

The first of the Post's two-part examination of planning for the 2023 counteroffensive, based on interviews with more than 30 senior officials from Ukraine, the United States, and European countries. It offers unusually detailed reporting on disagreements over strategy, timing, training, and the assumptions behind the operation. The archive links to it five times and subjects the newspaper's reporting to critical scrutiny.

### 28. “In Ukraine, a War of Incremental Gains as Counteroffensive Stalls.” *The Washington Post*, 4 December 2023.
[Washington Post report](https://www.washingtonpost.com/world/2023/12/04/ukraine-counteroffensive-stalled-russia-war-defenses/)

The companion account of how the counteroffensive unfolded on the ground over the summer and autumn. Read alongside the planning article above, and against Ukrainian and frontline sources; it is one newspaper's reconstruction, not the final word on the campaign. Linked twice in the archive.

## Ukrainian reporting and domestic cases

### 29. Censor.NET. Reporting on the detention and court proceedings involving Colonel Roman Chervinsky. June–November 2023.
[June 19 report: court leaves Chervinsky in custody](https://censor.net/ua/news/3425649/sud_zalyshyv_chervinskogo_pid_vartoyu) · [June 19 court report](https://censor.net/ua/news/3425540/u_shevchenkivskomu_rayisudi_kyyeva_rozpochalosya_sudove_zasidannya_u_spravi_chervinskogo_videofotoreportaj)

A small cluster of contemporaneous Ukrainian reports on the proceedings against Chervinsky. Useful for following the domestic legal case that later became central to the Washington Post's Nord Stream reporting. Treat the news reports as records of procedural developments, not as substitutes for court documents or findings on the underlying allegations.

### 30. Ministry of Defence of Ukraine. “Recruiting instead of conscription: Rustem Umerov approves concept of military personnel policy.” 5 November 2023.
[Ministry of Defence announcement](https://www.mil.gov.ua/news/2023/11/05/rekruting-zamist-prizovu-rustem-umerov-zatverdiv-konczepcziyu-vijskovoi-kadровoi-politiki-minoboroni/)

An official announcement of a new military personnel-policy concept, useful for documenting the government's stated direction on recruitment and staffing. As a ministry announcement, it records policy intent; it does not establish how quickly or fully the policy was implemented. Linked on 5 November.

## Editorial and verification notes for the next pass

1. Check every link for current accessibility and preserve an archived copy where the original has moved, become paywalled, or disappeared.
2. Separate primary records, independent reporting, specialist analysis, opinion, and official claims more rigorously than a publisher-level classification can.
3. Add author names, exact publication dates, and article titles to entries where the archive's link text is incomplete.
4. Where a source is explicitly disputed in the archive, preserve that distinction in the annotation rather than flattening the source into a neutral citation.
5. Decide whether the final bibliography should include the broader 2023 reading list: culture, books, history, personal reporting, technology, running, and other non-war material.
