# Annotated bibliography: Ukraine and the war, 2024

*Working draft · v0.1 · compiled from the 2024 Substack archive and its linked-reference dataset*

## Scope and method

The 2024 archive contains 300 posts and 1,717 linked-reference records, spanning 348 distinct URL hostnames before publisher normalization. This bibliography is a selective guide to sources relevant to the war, Ukraine's political and military position, international support, accountability, and the information environment. It is not a list of every link in the archive.

Selection is based on a combination of:
- recurrence in the archive;
- importance as a primary document, official record, survey, investigation, specialist analysis, or substantial field report;
- relevance to a major event or argument in the year's posts;
- value as a source that the archive explicitly questions or disputes.

Raw hostname totals are not publisher totals. For example, *The Washington Post* appears through both washingtonpost.com and wapo.st, while the archive's own Substack links, publisher homepages, social platforms, maps, and reference pages also contribute to the raw counts. Link frequency is not a measure of reliability, quality, or readership. A source's inclusion does not imply endorsement. Publication date and the date on which the archive linked to it are shown separately where known. Some sources published before 2024 were reused as background.

This first pass is Ukraine-war focused. Personal, cultural, literary, photographic, running, and other non-war sources are outside its scope for now.

## Primary documents, official positions, and accountability

### 1. Office of the Director of National Intelligence. “2024 Annual Threat Assessment of the U.S. Intelligence Community.” 2024.
[2024 Annual Threat Assessment (PDF)](https://www.odni.gov/files/ODNI/documents/assessments/ATA-2024-Unclassified-Report.pdf)

The public intelligence community assessment provides an official U.S. baseline for how Washington described Russia, the war, and the wider security environment in 2024. It is useful as a record of the administration's stated assessment, not as a substitute for independent reporting or a complete account of classified judgments. Linked in the archive on 3 April.

### 2. Kyiv International Institute of Sociology. “Trust in Military Commanders and Attitude to Possible Resignation of Zaluzhny.” January 2024.
[KIIS survey](https://www.kiis.com.ua/?lang=eng&cat=reports&id=1338&page=1)

A contemporaneous survey relevant to the public dispute surrounding Commander-in-Chief Valerii Zaluzhnyi and the eventual change in military leadership. The questionnaire, field dates, sample, and exact wording matter: the results should not be reduced to a generic measure of support for either Zaluzhnyi or the president. Linked on 31 January.

### 3. Kyiv International Institute of Sociology. Public-opinion survey, July 2024.
[KIIS survey archive](https://kiis.com.ua/?lang=ukr&cat=reports&id=1421&page=1)

A second KIIS source in the archive, useful for following changes in public attitudes during the summer. Consult the original report for the questions and fieldwork before comparing its results with the January survey. Linked on 25 July.

### 4. North Atlantic Treaty Organization. “Washington Summit Declaration.” 10 July 2024.
[NATO summit declaration](https://www.nato.int/cps/en/natohq/official_texts_227678.htm)

The leaders' formal statement at the Washington summit, recording NATO's position on Ukraine, allied support, and the alliance's wider security commitments. Read it as a diplomatic and political declaration. It is not itself a delivery schedule, a bilateral security treaty, or a guarantee of a particular military outcome. Linked on 25 July.

### 5. U.S. Department of Defense / Special Inspector General for Operation Atlantic Resolve. Quarterly report, April–June 2024. Released August 2024.
[Quarterly report (PDF)](https://media.defense.gov/2024/Aug/14/2003525006/-1/-1/1/OAR_Q3_JUN2024_FINAL_508.PDF)

A congressional oversight report covering U.S. government activities related to Ukraine and the broader response to Russia's invasion. It belongs in the bibliography because aid totals, delivery, monitoring, and oversight are recurring questions in the archive. The report's scope and reporting period should be kept distinct from claims about the effectiveness of any individual assistance programme. Linked on 21 August.

### 6. Special Inspector General for Operation Atlantic Resolve. “Special Inspector General Report to the United States Congress, July 1, 2024–September 30, 2024.” November 2024.
[Quarterly report (PDF)](https://oig.usaid.gov/sites/default/files/2024-11/OAR_Q4_Final.pdf)

The next quarterly oversight report, covering the U.S. response from July through September. It provides an official oversight record during the period of Ukraine's Kursk operation and the continuing debate over the scale, timing, and control of Western assistance. It is an oversight document, not an independent operational history of the war. Linked on 18 November. The report's publication and reporting dates should not be conflated.

### 7. President of Ukraine. “Ukraine's Plan for Victory Contains a Clear Vision of Steps Towards a Just and Lasting Peace – Andriy Yermak.” October 2024.
[President's Office](https://www.president.gov.ua/en/news/plan-peremogi-ukrayini-mistit-chitke-bachennya-krokiv-do-spr-93441)

An official presentation of the Victory Plan through the President's Office. It is essential for establishing what the Ukrainian leadership publicly proposed and how it framed the plan. It should be read alongside the president's own presentation, allied responses, and the archive's scrutiny of the plan's assumptions. Linked on 3 October.

### 8. President of Ukraine. Address at the Ukraine Recovery Conference. 12 June 2024.
[President's Office speech](https://www.president.gov.ua/en/news/vistup-volodimira-zelenskogo-na-ukraine-recovery-conference-91437)

A primary record of the president's public case for support for Ukraine's recovery. Useful for the connection between wartime resilience, energy infrastructure, reconstruction, and international assistance. As with other official statements, it records the government's position rather than independently verifying its claims. Linked on 12 June.

### 9. The White House. “Statement from President Joe Biden on the House's National Security Supplemental.” 17 April 2024.
[White House statement](https://www.whitehouse.gov/briefing-room/statements-releases/2024/04/17/statement-from-president-joe-biden-on-the-houses-national-security-supplemental/)

A primary source for the administration's public response to the House passage of the national-security supplemental, after months of delay in U.S. assistance. It helps establish the official political framing of the legislation; it should be paired with the enacted law, appropriations detail, and subsequent delivery and oversight records. Linked on 18 April.

### 10. U.S. Department of Defense. “Fact Sheet on Efforts of Ukraine Defense Contact Group National Armaments Directors.” October 2024.
[Department of Defense fact sheet](https://www.defense.gov/News/Releases/Release/Article/3897721/fact-sheet-on-efforts-of-ukraine-defense-contact-group-national-armaments-direc/)

An official account of allied armaments coordination. Useful for understanding the institutional machinery behind support, production, and procurement, but not proof that announced initiatives translated into timely battlefield deliveries. Linked on 7 October.

## Operational analysis, maps, and specialist research

### 11. Institute for the Study of War. “Russian Offensive Campaign Assessments.” 2024.
[ISW assessment archive](https://www.understandingwar.org/)

The archive repeatedly uses ISW's dated assessments to follow Russian and Ukrainian operations. These reports provide a useful running operational record and explicit analytical judgments, but their claims should be separated into confirmed events, attributed reporting, and ISW interpretation. The archive links individual assessments throughout the year, including 13 March, 9 June, and 7 August.

### 12. DeepState. Interactive map of the Russo-Ukrainian war.
[DeepStateMap](https://deepstatemap.live/en)

A recurring map reference for changes in the line of contact and the geography of fighting. Particularly useful in the archive's discussion of settlements and front-line movement in Donetsk Oblast. A map is a changing analytical product, not a contemporaneous ground-truth record: check its update time, source notes, and later revisions before treating a boundary as definitive. Linked repeatedly from April through October.

### 13. Center for Strategic and International Studies. “Patriot to Ukraine: What Does it Mean?” March 2024.
[CSIS analysis](https://www.csis.org/analysis/patriot-ukraine-what-does-it-mean)

A specialist explainer on the Patriot air-defence system and what its transfer means for Ukraine. Useful for grounding discussion of air defence in the system's capabilities and constraints rather than treating a delivery announcement as a complete solution. Linked on 14 March.

### 14. Royal United Services Institute. “Russia's Cyber Campaign Shifts Ukraine's Frontlines.” July 2024.
[RUSI commentary](https://rusi.org/explore-our-research/publications/commentary/russias-cyber-campaign-shifts-ukraines-frontlines)

A specialist analysis of the relationship between cyber activity and the physical battlefield. Its value is in connecting digital operations to operational effects, a subject that can otherwise be discussed in vague or inflated terms. Read the underlying evidence carefully before generalizing from particular cases. Linked on 26 July.

### 15. Valerii Zaluzhnyi. “Modern Positional Warfare and How to Win in It.” Originally published in 2023; linked in the archive in March 2024.
[Essay (PDF)](https://infographics.economist.com/2023/ExternalContent/ZALUZHNYI_FULL_VERSION.pdf)

The then-commander-in-chief's own strategic argument about the character of the war and the challenge of breaking out of positional warfare. It is a primary source for Zaluzhnyi's public military thinking, not a neutral assessment of the campaign or a complete account of internal disagreements. The publication predates 2024; the archive returned to it on 9 March.

## Reporting, investigations, and battlefield accounts

### 16. The New York Times. “Surprisingly Weak Ukrainian Defenses Help Russian Advance.” 2 March 2024.
[Interactive report](https://www.nytimes.com/interactive/2024/03/02/world/europe/ukraine-defenses-avdiivka.html)

An interactive examination of Ukrainian defensive weaknesses after the fall of Avdiivka. Important because it places battlefield setbacks in the context of fortifications, manpower, ammunition, and command decisions. Its framing and evidence should be assessed separately; one sector's failures should not be treated as a complete diagnosis of the Ukrainian army. Linked on 2 and 5 March.

### 17. The New York Times. “Too Little Ammunition, Too Many Russians: The Harrowing Retreat From Avdiivka.” 6 March 2024.
[Report](https://www.nytimes.com/2024/03/06/world/europe/ukraine-avdiivka-russia.html)

A substantial account of the Ukrainian withdrawal from Avdiivka and the pressures facing its defenders. Useful as a contemporaneous field report on ammunition shortages, Russian numerical advantage, and the cost of delayed assistance. As with all battlefield reporting, distinguish direct observation and named testimony from estimates and claims attributed to officials. Linked on 7 March.

### 18. The Washington Post. “Z in bind over how to draft more troops as Russian forces advance.” 4 March 2024.
[Report](https://www.washingtonpost.com/world/2024/03/04/ukraine-mobilization-Z-russia/)

A report on the political and practical difficulty of mobilization as Ukraine faced renewed Russian pressure. It belongs beside the KIIS polling and the subsequent mobilisation legislation: the manpower debate cannot be understood through battlefield reporting alone. Linked on 4 March.

### 19. Financial Times. “US has urged Ukraine to halt strikes on Russian oil refineries.” March 2024.
[Report](https://www.ft.com/content/98f15b60-bc4d-4d3c-9e57-cbdde122ac0c)

A consequential report on reported U.S. pressure over Ukrainian long-range attacks on Russian oil infrastructure. It raises questions about the limits allies seek to place on Ukraine's conduct of the war and the tension between escalation management and Ukraine's own military strategy. The claim concerns reported private pressure and should be read with attention to sourcing and subsequent official responses. Linked on 22 March.

### 20. The New York Times. “The Spy War: How the C.I.A. Secretly Helps Ukraine Fight Putin.” 25 February 2024.
[Investigation](https://www.nytimes.com/2024/02/25/world/europe/the-spy-war-how-the-cia-secretly-helps-ukraine-fight-putin.html)

A major account of the intelligence relationship between the United States and Ukraine. Important for understanding the long development of intelligence cooperation, but necessarily dependent on sources and details that are difficult for readers to verify independently. It should be read alongside other accounts, including the archive's links to earlier reporting on the CIA's role. Linked on 26 February.

### 21. Bihus.Info. Investigation into surveillance of the newsroom and journalists. January 2024.
[Bihus.Info investigation](https://bihus.info/shhonajmenshe-30-lyudej-braly-uchast-v-operacziyi-stezhennya-za-bihus-info-novi-detali/)

A Ukrainian investigative report into the surveillance operation targeting Bihus.Info. The case is important not only as a press-freedom story but as a test of state accountability during wartime. Keep the outlet's documented findings, its allegations about those responsible, and subsequent official investigations distinct. Linked on 20 January.

### 22. The Washington Post. “Second Russian invasion of Kharkiv caught Ukraine unprepared.” 17 May 2024.
[Report](https://www.washingtonpost.com/world/2024/05/17/kharkive-defenses-ukraine-russia-reinvasion/)

A report on the Russian offensive in Kharkiv Oblast and the weaknesses exposed in Ukraine's border defences. Read alongside reporting from the ground and official Ukrainian statements. The headline's broad formulation should not obscure the differences between local defensive failures, the wider operational picture, and the subsequent Russian inability to achieve all its apparent objectives. Linked on 20 May.

### 23. The New York Times. “Russians Poured Over Ukraine's Border. There Was Little to Stop Them.” 18 May 2024.
[Report](https://www.nytimes.com/2024/05/18/world/europe/ukraine-russia-advance-kharkiv.html)

A companion account of the opening phase of the Kharkiv offensive, focused on the thin Ukrainian defences near the border. It is useful when read against later developments and the Ukrainian response, rather than as a final verdict on the campaign. Linked on 20 May.

### 24. The New York Times. “Deception and a Gamble: How Ukrainian Troops Invaded Russia.” 13 August 2024.
[Report](https://www.nytimes.com/2024/08/13/world/europe/ukraine-russia-war-kursk-offensive.html)

An early detailed account of the planning and execution of the Ukrainian operation in Russia's Kursk region. It is an important source on the element of surprise and the operational gamble, but early reporting should not be mistaken for a full assessment of the operation's strategic costs or results. Linked on 14 August.

### 25. Financial Times. “Russian reinforcements fail to push back Ukrainian incursion.” August 2024.
[Report](https://www.ft.com/content/7dcb3009-ec9a-417a-b2e1-01c26c9349a0)

A contemporaneous account of Russia's initial response to the Kursk operation. Useful as a time-stamped report, especially when compared with later assessments of Russian adaptation and the changing front line. Linked on 12 August.

### 26. The Wall Street Journal. “A Drunken Evening, a Rented Yacht: The Real Story of the Nord Stream Pipeline Sabotage.” August 2024.
[Report](https://www.wsj.com/world/europe/nord-stream-pipeline-explosion-real-story-da24839c)

A major investigative account advancing a specific reconstruction of the Nord Stream sabotage. The attribution of responsibility remains a contested and politically consequential matter. Treat the article as a reported reconstruction, not a judicial finding or a settled account. The archive also links earlier Washington Post reporting on a Ukrainian military officer's alleged role, which should likewise be handled as contested reporting. Linked on 16 August.

### 27. Associated Press. “Ukrainian recruiters descend on Kyiv's nightlife in search of men not registered for conscription.” October 2024.
[AP report](https://apnews.com/article/russia-ukraine-war-drones-oil-depot-mobilization-40c6a27b9f98876b913a4daee0403c2c)

A report on the enforcement of mobilisation rules in Kyiv. It is useful as a street-level account of the friction between recruitment policy, military needs, and civilian life. It should not be treated as a statistical picture of mobilisation nationwide. Linked on 13 October.

### 28. Associated Press. “Desertion threatens to starve Ukraine's forces at a crucial time in its war with Russia.” November 2024.
[AP report](https://apnews.com/article/deserters-awol-ukraine-russia-war-def676562552d42bc5d593363c9e5ea0)

A substantial report on desertion and unauthorised absence in the Ukrainian armed forces. Important for understanding the manpower crisis beyond the formal mobilisation debate. The scale, definitions, and official data behind the report need to be kept in view; individual accounts do not by themselves establish national prevalence. Linked on 29 November.

### 29. Associated Press. “Russia grinds deeper into Ukraine after 1,000 days of grueling war.” November 2024.
[AP report](https://apnews.com/article/ukraine-russia-war-f7f56e494df1dbbcdec1853001796c45)

A year-end battlefield account published around the 1,000-day mark. Useful as a concise contemporary snapshot of Russian advances and the pressures on Ukrainian forces. It is a dated dispatch, not a comprehensive operational history. Linked on 18 and 19 November.

## Strategic debate, diplomacy, and political framing

### 30. Foreign Affairs. “How to Pave the Way for Diplomacy to End the War in Ukraine.” March 2024.
[Foreign Affairs essay](https://www.foreignaffairs.com/ukraine/how-pave-way-diplomacy-end-war-ukraine)

An argument for preparing the conditions and channels for diplomacy. It is valuable as a clearly articulated policy position in the debate over negotiations, but should be read as an argument rather than evidence that a settlement was available or that the parties' aims had converged. Linked on 6 March and revisited in June.

### 31. Foreign Affairs. “The Talks That Could Have Ended the War in Ukraine.” September 2024.
[Foreign Affairs essay](https://www.foreignaffairs.com/ukraine/talks-could-have-ended-war-ukraine)

A retrospective argument about the negotiations and diplomatic possibilities earlier in the war. Its claims should be tested against the documentary record, the parties' stated positions, and the changing military situation. It is included because the archive engages with the politics of negotiation, not because the title's counterfactual is accepted as fact. Linked on 23 September.

### 32. Richard Haass. “The Perfect Has Become the Enemy of the Good in Ukraine.” Foreign Affairs, November 2024.
[Foreign Affairs essay](https://www.foreignaffairs.com/ukraine/perfect-has-become-enemy-good-ukraine-haass)

An argument for a more limited and pragmatic approach to the war's possible end state. Useful for understanding one influential strand of the Western policy debate after the U.S. election. It is a prescriptive essay, not a neutral forecast or an account of what Ukrainians would accept. Linked on 5 November.

### 33. The New Yorker. “Volodymyr Z Has a Plan for Ukraine's Victory.” September 2024.
[Interview](https://www.newyorker.com/news/the-new-yorker-interview/volodymyr-Z-has-a-plan-for-ukraines-victory)

A major interview with the Ukrainian president ahead of his presentation of the Victory Plan to Western leaders. It is a primary source for the president's public case and a useful companion to the official text of the plan. Distinguish what the president says the plan requires from what allies subsequently agree to do. Linked on 26 September.

### 34. Financial Times. “Ukraine, Nato membership and the West Germany model.” October 2024.
[FT analysis](https://www.ft.com/content/b70972d6-3e7f-4a87-8bc5-ac0699f6e7fc)

A contribution to the debate over a possible security arrangement that would leave some Ukrainian territory outside Kyiv's control while anchoring the rest in Western security structures. The historical analogy is politically loaded and should be treated as a proposal for debate, not as an agreed plan or a direct historical equivalent. Linked on 11 October.

## Editorial verification checklist

Before treating this bibliography as final:

- Confirm publication dates, author names, and exact titles against the original pages, especially where the archive's link text is a paraphrase rather than the publisher's headline.
- Separate official statements from independently verified facts.
- For surveys, record field dates, sample size, question wording, and methodology before comparing results.
- For battlefield reporting, distinguish direct observation, named sources, anonymous sourcing, official claims, and editorial inference.
- For the Kursk operation and the Kharkiv offensive, retain the time sequence: early assessments should not be silently rewritten in light of later events.
- Treat Nord Stream attribution, intelligence reporting, and claims about covert operations as contested unless supported by independently established evidence.
- Distinguish mobilisation, desertion, unauthorised absence, and recruitment enforcement. They are related but not interchangeable measures.
- Normalize publisher names and URL aliases before producing final source-frequency statistics. In particular, consolidate wapo.st with washingtonpost.com and nyt.com with nytimes.com, while keeping the archive's own Substack links separate.
- Decide whether to add a second, broader bibliography for the archive's cultural, historical, literary, photographic, economic, and personal sources.

*This is a working selection, not an exhaustive bibliography. Inclusion records the source's relevance to the archive and does not imply agreement with its claims.*
